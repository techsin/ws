# Step 2 — Critique Pass 1: Does It Teach True Entrepreneurship?

Each of the 500 ideas graded on the TEACH dimension: is uncertainty epistemic (a hidden but learnable, shifting system) or empty RNG; is rejection/silence load-bearing or cosmetic; does the player learn founder judgment or just game mechanics; does the lesson survive community wikis; would a real founder recognize it. Scored /10 with forced spread.

### 1. Reply-All Ghosts — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Cold outreach as inference under near-total silence — deliverability, list quality, and message-market fit are learnable only from faint patterns across hundreds of sends.
Cheats: The hidden deliverability/domain-burn rules are a fixed learnable system, so the community will eventually publish the correct sending playbook and reduce the void to a checklist.
Note: Epistemic uncertainty done right (ambiguous open-pixels, lemon-market lead lists between real players), but it needs drifting spam-filter rules or the wiki kills it, and raw send volume risks becoming the grind.

### 2. The Dashboard Lies — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Spending scarce cash on instrumentation versus product, then betting on data you only half-trust, is a genuinely real and rarely-gamified founder skill.
Cheats: Reducing product-market fit to a single hidden scalar you "perturb" is a cartoon — real PMF is multi-dimensional and partly created, not just measured, so optimal play collapses into signal-filtering statistics rather than judgment.
Note: Player-inflated benchmark reports are a superb trust-calibration touch; epistemic, but it degenerates into a Kalman-filter puzzle once the community characterizes the noise model.

### 3. Ghost Round — TEACH: 9/10
Teaches: VC ghosting as the emergent byproduct of the other side's real portfolio incentives — the pass was never about you — which is the truest fundraising lesson in this batch.
Cheats: The entire teach depends on a healthy, genuinely-incentivized VC-player population; if that side thins out or its meta gets fully published, ghosting decays back into scripted flavor text.
Note: Structural and un-wikiable while both populations are live humans; the founder whisper network on responsive funds mirrors reality one-to-one.

### 4. Runway — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: The playerbase-driven thesis drift delivers one true lesson — last cycle's winning pitch pattern-matches to this cycle's pass.
Cheats: Deck-building reduces pitching to collectible-card combo optimization, which is precisely the grind-your-attributes trap the brief forbids, with investor rejection as a cosmetic boss-fight fail state.
Note: The self-staling meta is real anti-degeneration design, but the medium teaches deck meta-gaming, not fundraising judgment.

### 5. Cap Table — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Term-sheet literacy and negotiating against hidden counterparty pressure (fund vintage, reserve math) is a real skill most founders learn by getting burned once.
Cheats: A ranked dueling game with no company, customers, or runway attached strips out the asymmetric desperation that makes real term negotiations terrifying — both sides are just playing chess.
Note: Ladder play degenerates into solved openings and the historical puzzle scenarios are literally wiki content; human bluffing keeps a pulse, but this is a slice of entrepreneurship, not the thing.

### 6. Churnyard — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Churn arrives silent and unexplained from real customers who genuinely depended on you — the purest delivery of "nobody tells you why they left" in the pool.
Cheats: Everything hinges on players actually needing your tool for their own in-game businesses; if that utility is shallow, "features" become stat-boosts and the game collapses into exactly the attribute grind it was built to avoid.
Note: Real-player reliance is the hardest teach to fake and is un-wikiable; execution risk is enormous but the design is honest.

### 7. API Wars — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Platform-dependency economics — breaking-change trust, whale concentration risk, and losing your biggest integrator overnight without a word of explanation.
Cheats: Network effects ossify the dependency graph, so late joiners face a solved political landscape where the operative lesson is "you're too late" — true, but unplayable.
Note: Usage-graphs-without-reasons is load-bearing epistemic silence; degeneration here is social (incumbent lock-in), not wiki-based, which is better but still real.

### 8. Cold Start — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Learning subsidy elasticities only by burning money on them is exactly how marketplace founders actually validate — every experiment has a cash price.
Cheats: As pitched the hidden elasticities are static numbers, so the community will measure and publish them, turning liquidity ignition into a subsidy spreadsheet you copy from a wiki.
Note: Needs demand drift to stay epistemic; real players poaching your supply side is the redeeming unscriptable pressure.

### 9. Standup — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Attribution fog across delayed consequences, and identical-seed ghost companies let players see luck separated from skill — a quietly profound mechanic almost no game has shipped.
Cheats: A shared daily hidden market is Wordle-solvable — early-timezone players publish what worked and everyone downstream follows the answer key by lunch, killing the lesson daily.
Note: One decision per day risks a thin menu-picking decision space; needs per-player market instances or the seed-sharing degeneration is fatal.

### 10. Post-Mortem — TEACH: 9/10
Teaches: Progress measured purely in epistemics — you die, learn one verified truth, and the drifting economy silently rots the community's accumulated wisdom, forcing perpetual re-validation of received startup advice.
Cheats: Death handing you a clean verified fact is generous to the point of fantasy — real markets never confirm anything — and the shared codex lets followers freeload on other people's learning.
Note: Knowledge decay is the only structural anti-wiki mechanism in the batch besides live humans; even the freeloading teaches, because trusting a rotted codex entry is itself the founder mistake.

### 11. The Feed — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Reflexive markets — every discovered growth hack destroys itself as players pile in, which is the actual physics of every distribution channel ever exploited.
Cheats: Your only customer is an algorithm, so it teaches ASO/SEO divination rather than reading humans, validating demand, or selling anything to anyone.
Note: Permanently shifting AND learnable — the retraining loop is textbook epistemic uncertainty, and guilds with wrong folk theories are founder Twitter rendered exactly.

### 12. Voicemail — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Enterprise sales heartbreak — hidden org charts, champions without budgets, and discovering three weeks of calls were wasted on the wrong person.
Cheats: LLM gatekeepers invite a jailbreak meta — the community will trade magic phrases that talk past any receptionist, degrading sales craft into prompt-injection speedrunning.
Note: Voicemail purgatory is load-bearing silence, and the shared org-chart map being part rumor mill is honest trust-calibration curriculum.

### 13. Burn — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: The emotional truth of runway — payroll stalking the calendar and split cofounder bank authority genuinely deliver cash-flow dread and financial trust friction.
Cheats: Revenue arriving "late, disputed, or not at all, and you can't see which" is explicitly unlearnable noise — pure RNG in a horror costume, teaching helplessness where a founder would learn which customers pay and why.
Note: Atmosphere-forward; invoice triage is a real but narrow skill, and nothing in the core loop is epistemic.

### 14. Term Sheet Poker — TEACH: 3/10
Teaches: Liquidation preference as the tell of what an investor actually believes is one sharp, real insight.
Cheats: This is poker with startup-flavored cards — episodic hands with no company, customers, runway, or consequences beyond the table make it a business-themed party game, not a game about entrepreneurship.
Note: Fun is not the graded dimension; cross-hand reputation is the only mechanic gesturing at real founder life, and it's thin.

### 15. Fake Door — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Validation as a discipline with perishable output — demand signal decays between measurement and execution, and buyers of "proven" concepts sit inside a lemons market.
Cheats: If the weekly truth-shifts are arbitrary rather than pattern-bearing, reading conversion noise collapses into RNG divination and the concept exchange just prices luck.
Note: Selling validation creates honest incentive rot — sellers want to inflate signals — which is accidental but excellent trust-calibration teaching.

### 16. Null Result — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Distinguishing "no" from "not yet" from "wrong audience" via controlled experiment design is arguably THE core entrepreneurial skill, taught here with unusual purity.
Cheats: The pitch celebrates its own degeneration — published designs "reused like speedrun routes" means every follower runs a wiki recipe and learns nothing, and puzzle levels with correct solutions teach a certainty markets never grant.
Note: Also fails the MMO-ish brief outright; deeply epistemic for the first solver, dead curriculum for everyone after.

### 17. Warm Intro — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Capital access is a social graph and reputation is staked collateral — starting outside the graph entirely is the most honest cold-start in the pool.
Cheats: Player-built graphs ossify into kingmaker oligarchies, so within months the lesson curdles into "know the right three people," taught mostly by locking newcomers out until they churn.
Note: Un-wikiable because the gate is human favor, but it models only access to capital, not building anything worth funding.

### 18. Ticket Queue — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: The support inbox as the company's only sensory organ — extracting market signal from noisy, exaggerated, adversarially-planted customer contact.
Cheats: The player is purely reactive-inferential — no outbound, no validation, no deals — so it trains detectives, not founders.
Note: If the event-to-ticket mapping is stable the community ships a decoder wiki fast; rival sabotage tickets are the one freshness mechanism keeping inference alive.

### 19. Soft Landing — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: When and how to quit with dignity — acquihire-versus-fire-sale judgment against counterparties who go silent mid-diligence for internal-politics reasons you will never see.
Cheats: Procedurally generated "books full of surprises" risks gotcha-RNG landmines instead of learnable diligence patterns, and weeks of authentic silence is pacing most players will not survive.
Note: The least-taught real founder lesson in the batch, made structural by human corp-dev players with hidden mandates rather than scripted ghosting.

### 20. Replumb — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Pivot cost made physical — re-plumbing a live machine under falling cash pressure is the best mechanical metaphor for pivoting here, and blueprint staleness teaches playbook rot.
Cheats: Factorio players will optimize throughput because that is what the medium rewards — it trains factory engineering against faceless fluid "customers," not judgment about humans.
Note: Crude-gauge demand sampling is properly epistemic and stale blueprints are genuine anti-wiki design; the failure mode is the genre, not the systems.

### 21. Cofounder — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Trust calibration with real stakes — splitting equity with a stranger before you can know them, then discovering over real weeks exactly why vesting exists.
Cheats: The lesson is frequently delivered by your partner simply vanishing, which is realistic but leaves the abandoned player holding dead weeks of non-game rather than a playable teach.
Note: Un-wikiable because the hidden system is a human being; the month-long prisoner's dilemma is structural, and the postmortems really would be the content.

### 22. Compliance — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Reverse-engineering an opaque, undocumented authority, plus the live dilemma of sharing regulatory intel versus weaponizing a rival's non-compliance.
Cheats: Silently patching rules specifically to invalidate community folklore shows the designer's hand rigging freshness — real regulators are slow and indifferent, not adversarial dungeon masters.
Note: Deeply epistemic with built-in knowledge rot; the risk is attracting TIS-100 puzzle-solvers who learn parser archaeology rather than founder judgment.

### 23. Out of Office — TEACH: 4/10
Teaches: Founding intrudes on your actual life — the 11pm investor reply and the mid-dinner crisis ping are emotionally true.
Cheats: Timed replies to scripted push notifications is reaction-speed triage, not judgment — there is no hidden system to form beliefs about, so the uncertainty is event-queue RNG.
Note: A notification-UX gimmick wearing a startup costume; the ambience is real but nothing survives the epistemic or judgment tests.

### 24. Board Deck — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Narrative debt compounding — every gap between reality and your board story accrues interest until it detonates, a real and almost never taught founder failure mode.
Cheats: The sim "mostly runs itself," so the player's only agency is spin, and once the community reverse-engineers the debt meter's math, optimal deception becomes a solved calculation and the moral lesson dies.
Note: Real investor-players on the board is the load-bearing freshness; the hidden meter is the wiki-vulnerable single point of failure.

### 25. Fork Threat — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Community goodwill as a balance-sheet asset — monetize too hard and real players genuinely fork you and take your contributors, with the exit visible only as a quietly bending download graph.
Cheats: OSS maintainership is a niche slice of entrepreneurship, and star/issue accumulation between fork crises risks sliding back into the attribute-grind loop.
Note: A real-player fork threat is structural and un-wikiable; silent-exit signal reading is properly epistemic rather than RNG.

### 26. Dead Corner — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: You commit irreversible capital (a lease) on rumor-grade information, and the market only tells you the truth through the till after it's too late to un-sign — real location due diligence with real sunk cost.
Cheats: If corner-death causes are engine-scripted events (bus reroute, rival opening), the community will fingerprint their tells and pre-lease scouting becomes a checklist rather than judgment.
Note: Traffic generated by actual player errands keeps the ground truth permanently shifting, so the uncertainty stays epistemic-but-moving — the strongest anti-wiki asset here; ghost signage as visible survivorship-bias correction is a genuinely founder-shaped touch.

### 27. Form 27-B — TEACH: 4/10
Teaches: Gatekeepers are people with quotas and moods, and befriending or outlasting them is a real entrepreneurial skill founders learn in permitting hell.
Cheats: The actual business is entirely offstage — this is a dungeon crawler about bureaucracy wearing an entrepreneurship costume, and stamp codes with fixed meanings will be fully decoded on a wiki within a week.
Note: Player-clerks keep the social layer alive, but the core maze is a solvable puzzle, so the lesson collapses to "bureaucracy is annoying" — a cartoon, not judgment.

### 28. Fade — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Customers don't quit loudly, they just never come back, and your only recourse is disciplined experimentation against confounded signals — the true shape of retention.
Cheats: The tactile haircut mini-game threatens to become the skill ceiling, letting mechanically gifted players win without ever developing customer judgment.
Note: Because churn comes from real humans with real preferences, the silence is load-bearing and structurally un-wikiable; the overheard waiting-room gossip is a beautifully partial feedback channel rather than a solution key.

### 29. Quarters — TEACH: 4/10
Teaches: Cash flow is physical and rent day is non-negotiable — making the player literally consolidate quarters is the best visceral runway mechanic in the batch.
Cheats: By its own pitch the uncertainty is unlearnable ("breakdowns you can't predict, demand swings nobody explains") — that is pure RNG frustration teaching fatalism, not hypothesis-testing.
Note: Strip out the skimming runners and this degenerates into a maintenance-logistics grind — exactly the attribute-grinding trap the product brief forbids.

### 30. The Landlord's Table — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Silence is a legal move and you never learn the winning terms — losing deals without feedback and negotiating under total information asymmetry is authentic founder pain.
Cheats: It's one slice of entrepreneurship (getting a lease) inflated into the whole game, so the tenant side risks becoming pure waiting-room theater with no business underneath to fight for.
Note: Human landlords plus deliberately poisonable reputation forums make the epistemics permanently unstable in the right way — nothing here can be solved, only calibrated per-person.

### 31. Footfall Futures — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Paid market research is a market for lemons, and vendor trust calibration — not the data itself — is the actual skill; real founders who've bought consultant reports will wince in recognition.
Cheats: Broker rebranding makes reputation nearly free to launder, so the rational equilibrium is "never buy data," at which point the entire premise self-destructs into an Akerlof collapse nobody plays through.
Note: Twelve-month feedback lag is honest but brutal for a game loop; if reputation persistence is patched to fix the collapse, the market becomes "trust the leaderboard broker" and the lesson dies.

### 32. Ghosted Gym — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Retention data is confounded, and running deliberate experiments against noisy churn curves is the closest thing here to real validation discipline.
Cheats: If members are simulated from a hidden churn model, the theory-swapping leagues the pitch celebrates are precisely the engine that will solve it — six months in, everyone sets the same music and price from a spreadsheet.
Note: Epistemic uncertainty done right at launch, but with no stated mechanism for the hidden model to shift, this is the batch's clearest case of a lesson with an expiration date.

### 33. Bench Fee — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Quoting a fixed price before you've seen inside the box is the estimator's dilemma every agency and contractor founder knows — underquote and eat the loss, overquote and lose them silently.
Cheats: The adversarial breaker economy pulls it toward being a puzzle-design arms race about the boxes themselves, where a great diagnostician needs zero business judgment beyond the quote.
Note: Player-authored problems make degeneration structurally impossible — the arms race IS the content pipeline — but the entrepreneurship taught is narrow: pricing under uncertainty, full stop.

### 34. Ninety Days — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: You learn the rules of a new market by being fined for breaking them, under a hard 90-day runway — the meta-skill of fast, cheap hypothesis-testing in an undocumented system is genuinely founder-grade.
Cheats: The shared weekly seed means the community solves the city by Wednesday and everyone after that is following a Discord guide — the game's own leaderboard mechanic is its degeneration engine.
Note: Death from a rule you never saw is epistemic for the community but pure RNG for the individual first-timer; also the weakest MMO fit in the batch, since other players are meta-collaborators rather than customers or counterparties.

### 35. Endcap — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: You live on both sides of cold outreach — ignored by distributors while player-suppliers pitch you — so you learn what makes a pitch land by being the silence yourself.
Cheats: The register's refusal to distinguish price, placement, or neighborhood failure can tip from productive ambiguity into uninterpretable noise if the sim doesn't reward controlled shelf experiments.
Note: Persistent supplier relationships where burning one "gets around" makes reputation a real asset with real carrying cost; human counterparties on both sides keep this permanently unsolvable.

### 36. Hold Music — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Eighty percent of outreach vanishing into voicemail — not rejected, just unanswered — is the most honest depiction of cold outreach in the pool, and the days-later live callback teaches follow-up discipline for real.
Cheats: If the callback rate is a flat dice roll rather than responsive to when you call and what voicemail you leave, the silence is aesthetic frustration rather than a system you can get better at.
Note: "Which numbers are human" lists will be wiki'd within days, gutting the trust-calibration layer; the phone-only interface is a bold constraint but risks being a novelty about waiting rather than a game about building.

### 37. The Strip — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Your business lives or dies on an ecosystem you only partially control, and merchant-association politics over tenant mix is a real, underrated founder experience.
Cheats: A hidden but fixed synergy formula is premium wiki bait — once the coefficients are regressed out (and they will be), vacancy politics becomes spreadsheet-backed lobbying for the mathematically correct tenant.
Note: The social layer of twelve real owners survives the formula being solved, but at that point the game teaches coalition politics, not market uncertainty.

### 38. Inspection Day — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Compliance regimes are opaque, enforcement is a person not a rulebook, and you build judgment from accumulated scar tissue and contradictory folklore — exactly how real founders learn regulators.
Cheats: "Hardening your shop against audits" can quietly become tower-defense against griefers, where the optimal play is appeasing inspector-players' egos rather than running a better business.
Note: Structurally wiki-proof — ground truth is per-human, private, and shifting, so folklore stays genuinely contradictory forever; bare citation codes make rejection-without-explanation fully load-bearing.

### 39. Change of Hands — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Reading a business from its ledgers and deciding which inherited commitments are genius versus traps is real acquisition-entrepreneurship due diligence.
Cheats: Weekly forced rotation destroys the one thing entrepreneurship requires — consequences that are yours — so the rational strategy is to strip-mine the shop before handoff, and the game's incentives actively teach anti-stewardship.
Note: The archaeology is richly epistemic (human-authored ambiguity can't be wiki'd), but no runway, no skin in the game, and no ownership makes this a forensics exercise, not founding.

### 40. Till Tape — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Managing a business you can only perceive through its numbers — inferring theft, weather, and demand shifts from receipt anomalies — is real financial-statement literacy taught mechanically.
Cheats: It amputates everything else about entrepreneurship — no customers, no selling, no outreach, no negotiation — leaving a brilliant forensic-accounting puzzle wearing a shopkeeper's apron.
Note: Guild pattern libraries are the obvious degeneration vector, but seeding staff behavior from other players' management styles keeps the anomaly distribution genuinely non-stationary — a smart structural hedge.

### 41. Variance — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Verdicts from human gatekeepers arrive without reasons, and the pre-hearing lobbying matters more than the three-minute pitch — which is fundraising in a nutshell, as any founder who's pitched a partnership meeting knows.
Cheats: It's an event mini-game bolted onto an unspecified shop game; the "theater is the game" framing concedes that the business itself is set dressing for a persuasion party game.
Note: Rotating player jurors announced at the door make outcomes permanently un-wikiable, but also risk pure social RNG — a board of trolls teaches nothing except that boards can be trolls.

### 42. The Playbook — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Staking your savings on someone else's system and discovering only months later whether it was brilliance or garbage is the guru-course economy rendered mechanically honest — with when-to-quit-versus-keep-complying as the core judgment call.
Cheats: The months-long feedback loop that makes it truthful also makes it near-unplayable, and the pressure to compress it will gut the exact lesson (slow, expensive validation of trust) the design exists to teach.
Note: Community playbook-ranking is inevitable but mirrors real franchise-disclosure markets, so the lesson survives it; human-authored manuals mean the content can never be solved, only litigated in public blowups — deliciously combustible.

### 43. Open Up — TEACH: 4/10
Teaches: Leading indicators live in the environment, not the dashboard — noticing scaffolding before it hits your till is real street-level founder awareness.
Cheats: A finite library of visual signals with deterministic consequences is a spot-the-difference Wordle, and the signal-to-effect wiki ("papered window = +10% traffic in 7 days") kills the observation lesson within a season.
Note: No outreach, no rejection, no cash pressure, no counterparties — five meditative minutes a day is a lovely ritual and a hollow curriculum.

### 44. Store Hours — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Every hour you're closed is invisible lost demand you can only infer from silence — the missed walk-in whose identity you never learn is the sharpest opportunity-cost mechanic in the batch.
Cheats: Binding the game to your real-life schedule is doing pedagogical work with a dark pattern — the "lesson" of commitment is partly just notification hostage-taking, and players will resent it rather than learn from it.
Note: Neighborhood demand rhythms are genuinely learnable through open-hours experiments (properly epistemic), and the emergent street-level scheduling negotiation between real players keeps equilibria local and alive.

### 45. Word on the Street — TEACH: 9/10
Teaches: Information is a decaying, purchasable, poisonable asset, and deciding whose word to trust is the actual job — the exact epistemic humility that separates founders who survive from founders who read wikis.
Cheats: It's a meta-layer in search of a core sim — the pitch never says what business you're actually running, and if seasonal mechanic rotation is too aggressive it curdles from "markets shift" into "the devs rug-pulled me," teaching cynicism instead of calibration.
Note: The only idea in the batch where wiki-immunity is structural rather than incidental — undocumented-by-design with rotating ground truth means the community's knowledge market IS the game, and it cannot degenerate without ceasing to exist.

### 46. As-Is — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Buying a business from one photo and one self-reported number from a seller incentivized to lie is due diligence, winner's curse, and the market for lemons compressed into a single mechanic.
Cheats: If lying carries no durable consequence, rational bidders price everything as mold, the auction floor craters, and the game dies of the very Akerlof collapse it's teaching.
Note: Player-sellers keep deception genuinely human and un-wikiable, and cross-season grudges give reputation real carrying cost — the fix for the collapse is already half-designed into the pitch.

### 47. The Regulars — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Customer concentration is existential — twelve customers with an invisible referral graph means losing one quietly costs you four, which is a lesson every services founder learns in the worst way.
Cheats: If the regulars are NPCs with archetyped hidden needs, the community will publish the twelve personality decoder rings within a month and micro-signal reading becomes rote pattern-matching.
Note: The pitch is ambiguous about whether regulars are real players — and the entire epistemic integrity of the design hangs on that one unanswered question.

### 48. Across the Street — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Competitive intelligence is mostly theater — the rival's thriving look may be staged for your benefit, and copying what you see means copying their mistakes, which is a genuinely sophisticated founder lesson.
Cheats: A purely zero-sum shared traffic pool teaches a cartoon of competition-as-duel, when the actual entrepreneurial insight is that most markets aren't won by starving one visible rival.
Note: A live human opponent makes it permanently unsolvable (this is poker with storefronts), but there's no customer, no outreach, and no validation anywhere — it's a mind-game, not a market.

### 49. Small Fires — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: There are always more fires than hours, dropped commitments compound, and nobody ever tells you which fire was the one that mattered — this is the founder's actual Tuesday, plus a favor-debt economy with real people who collect at the worst time.
Cheats: If fire types have fixed compounding coefficients, the community tier-lists them and triage judgment collapses into a lookup table — the attribution ambiguity must be genuinely entangled with your specific commitment web or it's fake.
Note: The favor-debts with real players are the durable core (human reciprocity can't be wiki'd); the fire generator is the fragile part, hovering between epistemic overload and an RNG chore dispenser.

### 50. Curb Appeal — TEACH: 4/10
Teaches: Marketing without funnel visibility — conversions against an invisible denominator — captures the true horror of never knowing who walked past and why they didn't stop.
Cheats: With no denominator, no customer identity, and no other levers, the data is formally uninterpretable — the player can't distinguish a bad sign from a slow street, which is pure noise teaching learned helplessness, not judgment.
Note: The visual arms race between neighbors produces escalation aesthetics, not differentiation strategy; this is one clever mechanic (a good subsystem for another game on this list) stretched past its load rating into a whole product.

### 51. Dead Letters — TEACH: 9/10
Teaches: Committing capital against silence whose cause you cannot know — shipwreck, insult, or a rival's better offer all look identical from your desk — which is cold outreach rendered as a core resource decision.
Cheats: If NPC reply behavior is a dice roll rather than a learnable disposition, half the correspondence pool is pure noise wearing an epistemic costume, and the player/NPC ambiguity becomes a shrug instead of a lesson.
Note: Structurally wiki-proof — real-hours latency and real-player correspondents cannot be datamined — but the design lives or dies on whether silence carries correlated, eventually-inferable signal.

### 52. The Incanto — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: The winner's curse — bidding blind on assets whose true value hinges on information someone else holds, then living with the leverage you took on to win.
Cheats: "Profitability hinges on diplomacy the Senate knows and you don't" is a slot machine unless the game leaks leading indicators; losing bidders learning nothing is realistic but risks teaching only "bid lower," not why.
Note: Spies and syndicates make the hidden state purchasable, which keeps the uncertainty epistemic; community bid-shading heuristics will emerge, but that's real auction markets, not degeneration.

### 53. Kontor — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Unexplained rejection by a gatekeeping in-group — the anonymous blackball with no feedback is the most honest model of "we passed, best of luck" in the batch.
Cheats: The hidden system is literally other players' moods, so it can collapse into a popularity contest where veteran cabals haze newcomers out of the funnel — and once admitted, the entrepreneurship evaporates into guild politics.
Note: Epistemic in the truest sense (you form theories about whom you offended and test them next season), but the business layer is thin garnish on a social-club simulator.

### 54. The Relay — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Operating a business on distorted secondhand signal — demand three handoffs away that you will never observe directly — plus silent counterparty churn when your partner simply stops appearing.
Cheats: If NPC traders backfill dead segments, the "distortion" becomes designed noise with a tunable dial, and grinders will solve crate-price arbitrage tables within a month.
Note: Epistemic and permanently shifting when populated by real players; catastrophically dependent on population density — an empty adjacent oasis is not uncertainty, it's a dead game.

### 55. Ledger of Little Falls — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Credit judgment with batch-delayed feedback — extending trust on gossip and gut all year, then getting graded once at settling day, which is exactly how receivables actually punish you.
Cheats: If crop failures and skip-towns are uncorrelated dice, the player's "judgment" is being graded on noise, and the settling-day reckoning teaches superstition, not underwriting.
Note: Falsifiable shared blacklists are a genuinely nasty trust-market mechanic; the risk is a cozy single-player lending sim with MMO trim rather than an MMO.

### 56. Underwriters' Row — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Pricing risk under radical ignorance with live adverse selection — the players buying your coverage are precisely the ones who know something you don't, including their own plan to scuttle.
Cheats: The entrepreneurial seat is arguably the wrong one — you learn underwriting and trust calibration, while the founders (the voyage-runners) live in a different game you only watch through shipping lists.
Note: Actuarial spreadsheets will emerge, but real players gaming the underwriters keeps the distribution non-stationary — this is epistemic uncertainty that regenerates itself, which is rare and valuable.

### 57. The Rumor Bourse — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Information as a depreciating asset of unknowable freshness — you must price a report without knowing how many copies already exist, which is real information-market economics.
Cheats: Out-of-game Discord verification shreds the opacity on day one, and legal false rumors push the market toward a lemons equilibrium where rational players simply stop buying — pedagogically correct, commercially dead.
Note: Source-reputation death "without anyone announcing it" is beautifully load-bearing silence; the degeneration vector is external tooling, not internal solving.

### 58. Dead Reckoning — TEACH: 4/10
Teaches: Executing with honest error bars — planning margins for water and landfall when you cannot know your own position.
Cheats: The uncertainty is about your latitude, not your market — this is a navigation roguelike with cargo flavor, and nothing in the loop resembles customers, validation, or cash-flow judgment.
Note: Navigation is a masterable skill, so the epistemic content burns off with practice and the game converges to execution; the poisoned position-swap is the only entrepreneurial cell in the body.

### 59. The Revenue Men — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Playing under rules that even the enforcers haven't received yet — regulatory ambiguity where both sides are guessing at the decree is a real founder feeling.
Cheats: Ten-minute bluffing rounds make this a poker variant in a tricorn hat — no runway, no customers, no compounding — so the "psychological metagame" teaches yomi, not judgment.

Cheats: Rendering a thousand counterfactual yous requires a fully simulated generative model, which quietly deletes the MMO — real rival players cannot be replayed — so the profound chart is only true of a dice world, not a market.
Note: One magnificent aha, then it degenerates into EV-grinding with a prettier report card; the status economy around fat right-shifted distributions is the only durable piece.

### 488. The Herd — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Cash flow as something you can hear starving — payroll eating animals monthly makes runway visceral in a way no dashboard ever has.
Cheats: It is metaphor-skinning — the herd is a resource bar with legs, the uncertainty is weather-and-rustler RNG, and no belief about any market ever gets formed or tested.
Note: Handshake breeding-stock credit is real trust calibration with real players; the rest risks being a logistics survival game in a poncho.

### 489. Wire Deals — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Negotiating under permanently unverifiable identity claims — counterparty reading with no resolution even after close mirrors how real deals die unexplained.
Cheats: Refusing to ever resolve the fog means the game can never confirm your reads, so learning rides entirely on slow long-horizon pattern memory — and bots will flatten style-recognition into noise.
Note: Epistemic and adversarially shifting; the biggest degeneration threat is out-of-game stylometry tooling deanonymizing the wire.

### 490. The Lying Manual — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: The founder's core scar — confidently delivered best practices that are specifically wrong, discoverable only by betting your business and doing the paranoid re-read afterward.
Cheats: The MMO is vestigial — the multiplayer is a shared annotation project rather than customers and competitors — and "wrong" is only meaningful if the underlying sim is deep enough to make the guru's lies falsifiable instead of arbitrary.
Note: The estate re-poisoning settled knowledge with revised editions is a genuine structural anti-wiki engine — rare in this pool, and exactly what degeneration-proofing looks like.

### 491. Strata — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: The backtest trap — richly legible historical demand that may describe a dead regime is non-stationarity taught mechanically, and "everyone is also a fossil" is a real epiphany.
Cheats: Your customers are recorded ghosts, so the live-player market the product demands is deferred to a hand-waved surface layer — who actually pays you today is the unpitched half of the game.
Note: Degeneration-resistant by construction since live behavior keeps depositing new strata; the uncertainty is fully epistemic and self-renewing.

### 492. Burn to Speak — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Costly signaling in the flesh — quality claims mean nothing unless they hurt, and the newcomer who cannot afford to say anything is the honest cold-start problem.
Cheats: One econ-seminar theorem inflated into an entire game — entrepreneurship reduced to bonfire sizing, and the poor-but-honest player's experience is designed misery with no counterplay.
Note: The counterfeit arms race keeps the signaling equilibrium moving, so it resists wikification better than it first appears; epistemic for customers, punishing for sellers.

### 493. Anecdata — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Statistical humility under N=1 — resisting the overcorrection reflex that genuinely kills companies who reroute roadmaps around one furious email.
Cheats: A single daily sample may be too little signal to ever act on rationally, so the optimal strategy could be ignoring the anecdote entirely — a game best played by not engaging with its one mechanic.
Note: Epistemic and non-degenerating — customers are live players and the folk census is unverifiable and lie-able by design; the absence of aggregates is genuinely load-bearing.

### 494. Eminent Domain — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Platform and regulatory risk — your distribution is a bridge a committee of strangers can vote away, so you learn lobbying, hedging, and building resilience over location bets.
Cheats: Randomly conscripted councils may vote as chaos rather than politics, tipping the uncertainty from readable-people epistemics into pure civic dice.
Note: Vote-trading cartels are the inevitable degeneration — and also an accurate simulation of zoning boards, so the rot is arguably the curriculum.

### 495. Control Group — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Causal attribution — was it me or the market — via a counterfactual twin, plus the meta-lesson that counterfactuals decay and trusting your own baseline is itself a judgment call.
Cheats: It gifts founders the one thing reality never provides — a control — and only partially earns forgiveness by corroding the gift with drift.
Note: Epistemic and per-company, so hard to wikify; the danger is it plays as an A/B-testing seminar rather than a company you grieve losing.

### 496. The Seeded Deck — TEACH: 3/10
Teaches: Attribution under adversity — deciding whether the strike was weather or an enemy is a real, if narrow, judgment.
Cheats: Real markets almost never sabotage you on purpose, so this trains malice-attribution — an anti-lesson that makes players worse founders — atop mechanics that are literally purchasable dice-griefing.
Note: It is Diplomacy-flavored whodunnit in a business skin; the uncertainty is opponent mischief, not a market you can form beliefs about.

### 497. Honesty Box — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: The scaling thesis in a tin — growth buys revenue with less knowledge, and every empty box is an attribution problem you must design experiments to decompose.
Cheats: The venture is a lemonade stand — no payroll, no pivots, no counterparties — so the profound observability lesson floats on a business too small to actually need it.
Note: Epistemic (vary stock, placement, and route timing to identify the residue) and slow to degenerate since the hidden variable is live-player honesty, which drifts with the population.

### 498. Séance Round — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Advisor triage — most mentors are fighting the last war, and judging which stale fragment still transfers across regimes is real founder work.
Cheats: The teach depends wholly on dead-player roleplay quality, and the one-letter-at-a-time ouija is friction cosplaying as depth — remove the pacing gimmick and it is a chat window with retirees.
Note: Post-bankruptcy haunting is a lovely loser-retention loop, but the uncertainty here is social RNG, not a system anyone can form beliefs about.

### 499. Same Product, Different Day — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Timing humility — the season-end scatter plot publicly executing everyone's "I read the regime" manifesto is an honest monument to calendar luck.
Cheats: By stripping product, customer, and validation it amputates most of entrepreneurship to isolate one variable — and the design's own thesis is that the variable is mostly unlearnable, which makes it a roulette wheel with an excellent postmortem.
Note: If the regime clocks were genuinely inferable it would be epistemic; the pitch winks that they are not, and one humility-aha cannot sustain a second season.

### 500. Shopkeeper in Someone Else's Game — TEACH: 9/10
Teaches: Building on a platform you do not control — undocumented systems, patch notes as macro shocks, real customers who never explain themselves, and rent due regardless; the Amazon-seller and iOS-dev condition rendered fully playable.
Cheats: It needs a host MMO willing to be colonized, and if the host's community wiki is good, half your hard-won opacity is outsourced to someone else's documentation.
Note: The uncertainty is learnable-but-permanently-repatched — the exact structure of real markets — and being unmasked as human adds reflexive social epistemics no wiki can solve; degeneration is continuously reset by the host's balance team.
