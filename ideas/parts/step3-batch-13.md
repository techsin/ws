### 301. Ex Post Facto — FUN: 5/10
Hook: Waking up to learn your spring bestseller is now last winter's crime — a screenshot-and-scream moment that seeds great war stories.
Poison: Punishment untethered from any action you took is dread without catharsis; moment-to-moment play is reading rumor gazettes and flinching.
Fix: Make legislative sessions visible theater — public hearings players can watch, lobby, and bet on — so retroactive laws arrive as anticipated cliffhangers instead of ambushes.

### 302. Indulgences, Inc. — FUN: 5/10
Hook: Writing blasphemously funny marketing copy for absolution futures, and the vintage-void panics that wipe fortunes in a single afternoon of glorious chat chaos.
Poison: Settlement never arrives, so nobody ever wins anything — a market with no scoreboard decays into a role-play chat room.
Fix: Schedule periodic public "Judgment Days" where a random cohort of client souls settles on-screen, creating jackpot reveals, crashes, and receipts for bragging rights.

### 303. Built to Flip — FUN: 7/10
Hook: The ghosting — you reshaped your whole company for BuyCo, they went silent, and that heartbreak becomes the forum story everyone retells.
Poison: Between flirtations the verb is grooming spreadsheet metrics alone, and droughts with no acquirer in the room are long stretches of nothing.
Fix: Stage courtship as live set-pieces — diligence dinners and data-room tours with readable tells — so the seduction is a playable scene, not async metric edits.

### 304. Spin Cycle — FUN: 5/10
Hook: The men in gray suits suddenly asking about your fabric softener orders — dread comedy with a genuinely tasty two-ledger puzzle underneath.
Poison: Getting your whole network wiped by an invisible stranger's sloppy books is faceless griefing that teaches players to never trust co-op again.
Fix: Give every bust a post-mortem trial scene revealing partial evidence, converting each loss into detective satisfaction and who-sank-us gossip.

### 305. Nice Place You Got Here — FUN: 7/10
Hook: Realizing three weeks later that the compliment about your window display was a demand — and the fire confirms it; the euphemism duels are pure screenshot material.
Poison: As pitched, consequences land weeks later and entirely off-screen, so new players quit before their first cause-effect loop ever closes.
Fix: Compress the lag to days and stage consequences on-screen (the crew comes back), keeping the ambiguity but letting the drama play where players can feel it.

### 306. Spot Zoning — FUN: 6/10
Hook: The morning map-reveal ritual, and the stampedes triggered by forged leak-maps drawn by rivals — a daily check-in with genuine deception play on top.
Poison: Nightly rug-pulls on your physical shop mean any night offline can ruin you — a lifestyle demand welded to dice.
Fix: Slow redraws to weekly with escalating public hearing-rumors in between, turning each map drop into an anticipated communal event you can hedge and bet around.

### 307. Batch 12 — FUN: 8/10
Hook: Demo Day — a live communal reckoning where the mystery algorithm anoints winners and the whole cohort melts down reverse-engineering why.
Poison: Sitting bottom-of-leaderboard for invisible reasons while your real product goes unused is compounding humiliation with no comeback verb.
Fix: Add a mid-cohort pivot-week wildcard that publicly resets perception, giving bottom-ranked founders a spectator-worthy redemption arc.

### 308. Reaper, Inc. — FUN: 4/10
Hook: The gallows-humor fantasy of racing timers to collect souls and sweating a five-star rating from the bereaved.
Poison: Deactivation ejects you from the game itself for unexplained reasons — the punishment is "stop playing," the one sentence live-service can never say.
Fix: Make deactivation the door into the better game — banned reapers immediately join or found rival dispatch co-ops, so punishment recruits instead of churns.

### 309. Dead Letter Office — FUN: 6/10
Hook: Opening your box to find another player's misdelivered letter — scandal, leverage, and a story you will tell for months.
Poison: Three-week silences against a five-minute session mean most days the mailbox is empty and the game is literally nothing.
Fix: Guarantee something arrives every day — newspapers, gossip circulars, creditor notices, wrong mail — so the ritual always pays even when your own answers don't come.

### 310. Motion to Dismay — FUN: 6/10
Hook: Landing a devastating counter-filing combo that buries a rival's business in injunctions — real deckbuilder crunch in legal drag.
Poison: The invisible clerks re-rolling the meta reads as random nerfs to a deck you spent weeks tuning — patch-note rage without patch notes.
Fix: Make dockets public so clerk "personalities" can be studied through observable rulings, turning meta shifts into judge-reading detective work instead of nerfs.

### 311. We Regret to Inform You — FUN: 7/10
Hook: Reading victims' forum rants about "the algorithm" that is actually you — plus the artisanal cruelty of a perfectly crafted rejection letter.
Poison: Once the black joke lands, the loop is an inbox-triage job with your own contracts dying silently in the background.
Fix: Give bureaus recognizable rejection "voices" that recipients learn to spot and dread, so style and infamy become the payoff for the grind.

### 312. Focus Group of the Damned — FUN: 6/10
Hook: The interviews themselves — cryptic, funny, haunting testimony ("asked about cereal, got a forty-year grudge") that begs to be screenshotted.
Poison: The detective work never gets its reveal — you never learn whether you were right, so the case ends mid-sentence every time.
Fix: Let some client products visibly launch and thrive or flop in-world, so a fraction of insights get spectacularly and publicly graded.

### 313. Trust Exercise — FUN: 9/10
Hook: The collapse — the frozen second when everyone realizes the run has started, then the mixed lobby where the wiped-out interrogate the escaped.
Poison: The accumulation phase between collapses is slow, and whales recruiting hardest can make newcomers feel like designated exit liquidity.
Fix: Shape rounds to an evening (60-90 minutes with escalating public math) so it becomes a repeatable social night instead of a weeks-long slow bleed.

### 314. Laurels — FUN: 6/10
Hook: Gala ceremonies as live social events — table-selling, snubs, and whisper campaigns cratering a trophy's book value overnight in front of everyone.
Poison: For everyone not running an award body, the game is a spreadsheet of haircut percentages; the fun concentrates in a tiny elite.
Fix: Make every player a juror somewhere, so gala season hands everyone a ballot, a bribe inbox, and personal gossip stakes.

### 315. Past Due — FUN: 7/10
Hook: Cracking open a blind-bought portfolio like a cursed loot box — which IOUs are gold, which debtor reincarnated, which notes a rival fabricated.
Poison: The door-knocking legwork between auctions is grind, and opaque head-office remittance pricing makes even wins feel arbitrarily taxed.
Fix: Make auction day a scheduled communal event with public post-auction reveals, so the winner's curse plays out as spectator sport and seller reputations burn live.

### 316. Knock Knock — FUN: 7/10
Hook: The door that finally opens after a dead street — a slot-machine jolt into a high-wire improv dialogue with the whole day's commission on the line.
Poison: As pitched the dominant experience is standing in silence at doors that never open, and the game proudly refuses to tell you you're wasting your time.
Fix: Fill the streets with ambiguous readable texture — twitching curtains, piled mail, barking dogs — so silent doors become a scanning game instead of dead air.

### 317. The 41st Floor — FUN: 5/10
Hook: The quarterly reveal — discovering what your directives actually collided with, followed by the boardroom knife-fight over numbers everyone suspects are fiction.
Poison: The CEO's verb set is reading memos and waiting a quarter — a passivity sandwich for the player nominally in charge.
Fix: Add weekly "site visits" — one narrow, unfiltered ground-truth peek the CEO aims anywhere — creating an active investigation verb between quarters.

### 318. Feed the Code — FUN: 6/10
Hook: The creature — players will screenshot every grotesque new organ and trade allergy-form pranks on rivals like contraband.
Poison: Daily feed-or-it-screams obligations are a lifestyle demand that turns the pet into a nagging boss by week three.
Fix: Let a happy, well-fed Code work FOR you — auto-filing, charming auditors — so care builds power instead of merely deferring punishment.

### 319. Post-Mortem — FUN: 4/10
Hook: Playing a stranger's real failed company backwards like a true-crime documentary and arguing about the actual fatal scene.
Poison: A causal engine designed to defeat your hopes turns replay into learned futility — by run three you stop caring what you change.
Fix: Score runs on diagnosis, not rescue — reward correctly fingering the fatal decision (engine-verified) so beating hindsight bias becomes the win condition.

### 320. Five Stars, Or Else — FUN: 5/10
Hook: The protection pitch — walking into a shop you just review-bombed and gently offering to make it stop.
Poison: The fun is strictly extracted from other players' misery; the prey churn out, and a racket with no victims starves.
Fix: Give shopkeepers a playable counterintelligence game — tracing review waves, hiring rival rackets — so the war has two willing teams instead of predators and food.

### 321. The Stamp — FUN: 4/10
Hook: Stamp heists — server-wide manhunts, betrayals, and who-has-it-now rumor storms that become instant server legend.
Poison: One bottleneck means one guild plays the game while 9,900 players queue at their pleasure — spectacular content for twelve people.
Fix: Shatter it into many lesser seals with murky overlapping jurisdictions, so gatekeeper politics scales to every neighborhood instead of one throne.

### 322. Pending — FUN: 7/10
Hook: Sweating a three-week-old permit you've bet on like a slow-motion horse race while the whole parlor watches the odds twitch.
Poison: Week-scale resolution means thin action — a betting parlor with no race closing tonight is an empty room.
Fix: Seed a continuous calendar of ministry decisions — daily dockets, batch announcement days — so there is always a race closing this evening.

### 323. No-Show — FUN: 5/10
Hook: The roster itself — new names appearing overnight after a nod, and the office folklore about which ones you must never say aloud.
Poison: The defining choice (fire or carry) is a blind coin flip with instant catastrophe, so the optimal play is never touching the game's central mechanic.
Fix: Let protection status leak through play — funerals attended, favors called, who collects whose paycheck — so firing becomes a researched gamble instead of Russian roulette.

### 324. Going Up — FUN: 8/10
Hook: The doors-close rejection — wordless, instant, architecturally brutal, and so darkly funny it will be clipped a thousand times a day.
Poison: Unknown players who can't engineer penthouse rides spend hours loitering in elevator lobbies hoping to share air with someone who matters.
Fix: Give investor-players a real game of their own — quotas, portfolio pressure, rival poaching — so the listening seat is as fun as the pitching one and the elevators stay populated.

### 325. Audit Siege — FUN: 7/10
Hook: Watching an auditor squad wander into your decoy-subsidiary infinite loop while allies cheer — then the gut-punch when one of them cuts a deal and opens a gate.
Poison: Deterministic pathing off your own bookkeeping means the wiki solves defense in a month and every tower converges on the same paperwork wall.
Fix: Staff the sieges with player-run audit firms whose tactics evolve, replacing solvable AI pathing with a living adversary that keeps builds honest.
