### 201. Dead Air Dispatch — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Cold outreach mostly lands in silence you cannot even interpret, and trust must be bootstrapped through a reputation network you can't see into — the actual first-year-broker experience.
Cheats: Rendering silence as a "resource drain" risks converting the emotional lesson into a mana bar you manage, letting players optimize attrition instead of adapting their approach.
Note: Epistemic uncertainty is real because carriers are players with learnable but shifting preferences; the unreadable rumor channel is the anti-wiki mechanism, but only if reputation stays player-whispered rather than eventually surfaced as a stat.

### 202. Golden Sample — TEACH: 9/10
Teaches: Supplier trust calibration under information asymmetry — betting working capital on fragments and learning that the sample you approved is not the production run you'll receive — is a scar every hardware founder carries.
Cheats: Almost nothing; the one soft spot is that if factory reputations consolidate into public tier lists, newcomers skip the tuition that IS the lesson.
Note: Structurally anti-degenerate because factory players profit from selectively defecting (good line vs. night shift), so reputation intel decays the moment it's trusted — exactly like real sourcing.

### 203. Preorder Purgatory — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: The crowdfunding curse in its true order — money arrives before knowledge, and the real job becomes managing broken promises to real humans who can organize against you.
Cheats: The game leans on backer psychology being interesting, but if backers are mostly other founders playing tourists, refund pressure becomes performative rather than the cold financial terror it is in reality.
Note: Epistemic through and through (which update tone works is learnable per-audience but never stated); degeneration risk is meta-templates for "the apology post that retains 80%," which real Kickstarter forums already prove will emerge.

### 204. Demurrage — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Operating inside opaque bureaucratic systems where the rules exist, are unpublished, and can only be inferred from expensive experiments — a real and underrated business skill.
Cheats: It's rent-seeking navigation, not entrepreneurship — there is no customer, no validation, no product, just a queue tax to be minimized, so the judgment learned is narrow ops cunning.
Note: Uncertainty is genuinely epistemic (port authority players SET rules, so they're learnable), and alliance intel-sharing is the intended meta rather than degeneration — but real-time fee bleed on mobile smells like an energy-timer monetization trap.

### 205. Scrap Rate — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Root-cause inference from indirect evidence — reading the scrap bin instead of the dashboard is honest epistemology and the folklore-that's-half-wrong trade is a nice trust-calibration garnish.
Cheats: This is a statistics puzzle wearing a factory costume; there is no customer, no cash-flow terror, no silence — the roguelike frame makes failure cheap, which is precisely what real manufacturing failure is not.
Note: High degeneration risk: hidden defect distributions are a finite system, and the community will publish inference flowcharts within a month, collapsing it into a solved deduction game.

### 206. Keystone — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: True unknown unknowns — you do not know what you depend on until it fails — which is the rarest lesson on this list and the one 2021's chip shortage taught a generation of founders.
Cheats: The lesson fires once per cascade and the "core loop" of dependency mapping is undescribed, suggesting the game is one great revelation stapled to an unclear daily activity.
Note: The secret-selling market keeps it epistemic and player-driven, but after the community internalizes "always trace upstream," the surprise becomes a checklist and the game must keep generating novel keystones to stay alive.

### 207. Cross-Dock — TEACH: 4/10
Teaches: Your operational performance is your marketing — other players' businesses publicly depending on your floor is a genuinely good reputation mechanic.
Cheats: The ghosting prospects are pure flavor text bolted onto a warehouse Tetris game; nothing about the spatial stacking loop forces you to adapt to the silence, so the rejection is cosmetic in exactly the way the brief warns against.
Note: The real skill trained is real-time spatial optimization — an arcade skill, wiki-solvable and grindable — while the entrepreneurial layer (why prospects ghost) has no learnable structure hinted at all, making it RNG frustration.

### 208. Winner's Curse — TEACH: 9/10
Teaches: The most expensive lesson in contract manufacturing — reading what the drawing doesn't say, and understanding that winning a competitive bid is evidence you mispriced — delivered structurally, not narratively.
Cheats: If spec incompleteness is procedurally generated from a finite trap library, veterans will pattern-match traps instead of developing genuine "what's missing here" judgment.
Note: Adverse selection against real player bidders is self-refreshing and cannot be wikied (the meta shifts as everyone prices in known traps, exactly like real reverse auctions), and the seeded-misinformation Q&A is real-world-accurate dirty pool.

### 209. Lost in Translation Mfg. Co. — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Communication robustness — that specs, jigs, and golden references beat prose, and that the product you design is secondary to the message channel you design — is a real and specific sourcing lesson.
Cheats: The garble is an algorithm, and algorithms get decoded; once the community publishes the corruption grammar, this becomes an encoding minigame and the humility evaporates.
Note: Per-factory "dialects" and unreliable translation-memory sellers slow degeneration but don't stop it; the uncertainty is epistemic today and wiki-solved in six weeks.

### 210. Pull — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: The bullwhip effect experienced from inside — dying of a whip nobody in your chain cracked is a profound systems lesson about how little of your fate is locally caused.
Cheats: A game with literally no money teaches no cash-flow pressure, no runway, no pricing, no customer acquisition — it's an operations-management seminar, and lean gospel at that, not entrepreneurship.
Note: Elegant and epistemic (you learn to infer distant demand from card rhythms), but the lesson is singular; once you've felt one bullwhip, repetition adds nothing a founder needs.

### 211. Lot 4471 — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Acting irreversibly on an unproven theory while the clock and the market punish deliberation — the game never confirming root cause is genuinely brave design that mirrors real recalls.
Cheats: It's a crisis scenario, not a company; the entrepreneurial aperture is one bad month in the life of a business the player never had to build, so judgment about validation, sales, and cash never enters.
Note: Suppliers amending records plus permanent root-cause ambiguity makes this structurally anti-wiki — there is no truth to publish — which is rare and worth stealing even if this game stays narrow.

### 212. Last Time Buy — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: The lifetime-buy bet is real founder judgment distilled — irreversible capital commitment against unforecastable demand, with the gorgeous twist that verifying gray-market stock consumes the very units you're trying to save.
Cheats: It's one decision stretched into a game; if end-customer demand is developer dice rather than real player purchasing, the central bet is unlearnable RNG and the whole thing is a coin flip with pallets.
Note: The information-has-a-unit-cost mechanic (sacrifice reels to verify brokers) is the best single mechanic in this batch; player speculators cornering supply keeps the endgame human and unwikiable.

### 213. Line Down — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Social capital is an asset with a balance — who answers your 3 a.m. call is a ledger you built or didn't build over months — and the public callback scoreboard makes that brutally legible.
Cheats: A 20-minute crisis sprint trains adrenaline triage, not entrepreneurship; nothing here touches building the business whose line went down, and "charter a jet for a $4 part" is a war story, not a judgment loop.
Note: The favor economy only teaches if favors were accumulated through prior real interactions; as a standalone session game the phone-tree silence is just RNG dressed as relationships.

### 214. Upstream — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Due diligence with no diligence — buying businesses on plant visits and gut reads, then discovering the liabilities after closing — is exactly how small-business M&A actually works, and player-crafted seller deception makes trust calibration the real skill.
Cheats: The acquisition ladder frame risks rewarding empire-grinding, and burned buyers quitting the game (rather than eating the loss and continuing) would gut the consequence the lesson depends on.
Note: Structurally anti-degenerate because deception is authored by adversarial humans who adapt to every published tell; the war-stories-as-content instinct is correct — that IS the real M&A community.

### 215. MOQ — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: The MOQ ante — that inventory is the founder's real gamble and you must commit cash you don't have before demand exists — is the correct core truth of physical products.
Cheats: "The house never shows you true demand" reveals the fatal flaw: if demand is house-generated dice rather than real player purchasing, this is a casino with cardboard chips, teaching that entrepreneurship is luck management rather than belief-testing.
Note: The poker framing actively miseducates — bluffing sell-through is fun but implies rivals are the game, when the real MOQ game is against your own demand thesis; uncertainty here is RNG unless demand becomes endogenous.

### 216. Ghost Shift — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: You manage what you design, not what you watch — incentive schemes and standing orders executing in your absence is the founder's scaling lesson (culture is what happens when you're logged off) made literal.
Cheats: If the ghost shift is a simulation rather than emergent from real player interference, the community will reverse-engineer the optimal incentive config and post it, converting management judgment into a settings screen.
Note: Rival poaching and bribery of your night shift is the load-bearing anti-wiki element — it keeps the opacity adversarial and human; without it this degenerates into an idle game with an HR skin.

### 217. Tariff Weather — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Reflexivity — the herd's panic causes the storm it fears, and positioning on rumor when the rumor mill is also player-made is real macro judgment that no tutorial can convey.
Cheats: The player is a trader/positioner, not a builder; timing and luck are visible (good) but validation, customers, and unit economics are absent, so it teaches one facet of founding brilliantly and ignores the rest.
Note: Endogenous weather is the strongest anti-degeneration design in the batch — even the developers can't publish the answer key because there isn't one — this mechanic deserves to be stolen for a bigger game.

### 218. Rolodex — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Networks decay when you only call to take — attention as the scarce input to relationship maintenance is a lesson most first-time founders learn too late.
Cheats: Rendering humans as decaying cards invites exactly the degenerate behavior it should punish: players will schedule "warmth upkeep" like watering crops in FarmVille, performing relationship theater to keep a stat from ticking down.
Note: The saving grace is that cards are real opted-in players who can simply stop answering with no explanation — genuinely epistemic, unwikiable silence — but the deck-builder frame fights the lesson at every turn.

### 219. Bill of Monsters — TEACH: 3/10
Teaches: Single-source suppliers as unroutable bosses is a decent metaphor for a real BOM truth, and rivals locking exclusive supply is the one honest multiplayer tooth.
Cheats: This is the cartoon the brief warns about — sourcing rendered as dungeon-clearing means the player experiences boss fights and loot logic, not silence, cash pressure, or judgment; the metaphor is legible precisely because nothing real is felt.
Note: Lead-time traps are RNG in a trenchcoat, the dungeon layout will be mapped on a wiki within days, and "clear every node before launch" is literally a completion checklist — the opposite of radical uncertainty.

### 220. Co-Packer Speed Dating — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Iterating a pitch against pure silence — matched or not, no scores, no notes — is the load-bearing rejection mechanic done right, and paying for unreliable intel on "what Hillside actually wants" is real founder behavior.
Cheats: The activity is narrow (pitching is one skill), and if co-packer players get bored and match randomly, the epistemic signal collapses into noise and weeks of pitch refinement teach superstition instead of positioning.
Note: The whole design lives or dies on co-packer players having genuine, consistent, self-interested preferences; with real capacity scarcity that holds and the intel market is the intended meta, not degeneration.

### 221. Box Diaspora — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Pricing risk on assets you cannot observe — leasing into a world that will damage, convert, or vanish your property — is honest counterparty-risk education, and canonically unreliable tracking is a bold anti-dashboard stance.
Cheats: The player's agency is thin (set lease price, hire skip-tracer, wait), so the lesson arrives as portfolio statistics rather than lived judgment, and "your box became someone's site office" is a great anecdote experienced as a line item.
Note: Cross-session asset persistence is structurally unwikiable because the risk source is other humans' chaos, but the loop risks being an actuarial spreadsheet with flavor — epistemic, yet passive.

### 222. The Wrong Map — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Quoting firm promises against unverifiable information, then eating the difference, is a genuine unit-economics-of-commitment lesson, and buying corrections of unknown honesty trains information-vendor skepticism.
Cheats: It's a courier game at heart; the entrepreneurial judgment is real but thin — route knowledge is the entire business, so this teaches intel arbitrage more than company-building.
Note: Degeneration is partially the point (honest cartographers emerging IS the market working), but if one subscription becomes canonically reliable the uncertainty dies — sabotage annotations must stay economically motivated to keep the map permanently wrong.

### 223. Pilot Purgatory — TEACH: 9/10
Teaches: Diagnosing champions without budget authority, watching deals die in silence after months of warm meetings, and learning qualification through scar tissue — this is enterprise sales exactly as founders live it, with no mechanic ever labeling the truth.
Cheats: It needs buyer players to have authentic incentives to run fake pilots (looking innovative must genuinely pay them), otherwise the hidden org charts are just puzzle boxes with a developer-authored solution.
Note: Fantasy-football scouting reports on buyers is degeneration-as-realism — real sales orgs keep exactly these notes and they're exactly this unreliable — making this one of the few designs where the wiki meta deepens the lesson instead of killing it.

### 224. Net-90 — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: The melody-shifted-against-bassline metaphor makes working capital visceral — profitable companies die of timing, and factoring as dragging a beat earlier at a discount is a genuinely elegant mechanical translation.
Cheats: Rhythm games are execution-skill games; once the player internalizes the one insight (cash timing is not profit), the remaining challenge is dexterity and pattern memory, with no silence, no validation, no judgment left to develop.
Note: Multiplayer payment chains add real epistemic texture (whose late beat cascades into yours), but the core loop degenerates fast into a solvable score-attack — this is a brilliant teaching level, not a teaching game.

### 225. Teardown — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Inference from artifacts — reverse-engineering rivals' cost structures from what's physically in the box, while knowing your own products are being read the same way — is real competitive-intelligence judgment, and decoy components are a move actual companies make.
Cheats: It presupposes a rich product economy that this game doesn't itself provide; as a standalone it's an analysis metagame about businesses nobody is actually running, and the entrepreneurship happens offscreen.
Note: Strongly epistemic and self-refreshing (every teardown answer changes as rivals redesign), and public reports drifting into propaganda and libel is anti-wiki by construction — the community's knowledge base is itself untrustworthy, which is the point.
