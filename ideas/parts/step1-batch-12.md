### 276. Earnout
Pitch: You sold your company — congratulations — but half the price is an earnout tied to metrics the acquirer (a real player) now controls: they set your budget, reorg your team, and pick the accounting definitions your payout depends on, all behind a dashboard you can see but not audit. Your levers are political — negotiating measurement clauses upfront, cultivating allies inside the buyer's org, and deciding when quiet leverage beats loud escalation. The acquirer scores by extracting your product's value while paying out as little as possible without making you quit before integration finishes; only post-game do both sides see the true books.

### 277. Term Sheet in the Dark
Pitch: A negotiation card game where the clauses in the term sheet you sign are literally face-down — liquidation preferences, ratchets, and board seats reveal only when a trigger event fires mid-campaign, sometimes hours of play later. Over many runs you slowly learn to read the "clause language" from typography, weight, and dealer tells, mimicking how founders learn deal literacy through scar tissue. Played 1v1 or in draft pods where rival founders bid for the same lead investor's deck.

### 278. The Float
Pitch: You run a scrappy insurance startup that underwrites other real players' in-game ventures — their delivery drones, food stalls, crypto exchanges — and you invest the premium float while praying claims don't cluster. Adverse selection is the opacity: the riskiest players seek you out hardest, and you can't see their books, only their pitch. A single correlated disaster (which players can collude to cause) can wipe you out or make your reinsurance rivals rich.

### 279. Rug
Pitch: Social deduction for 6-10 players: you're all "core contributors" launching a token together, but one of you was dealt the Rugpuller role and is quietly structuring the treasury multisig for an exit. Trust asymmetry is mechanical — every wallet action is public on the in-game chain but pseudonymous, so you're doing forensic accounting on your own friends between rounds of shipping features. Ends in either a legitimate launch payout or a rug, and the post-game chain autopsy is half the fun.

### 280. Dilution Tower
Pitch: Your cap table is a physical tower (digital or actual tabletop blocks): every funding round adds investor blocks by removing and repositioning founder blocks, and the tower must stay standing through "event winds" (co-founder departure, down round, acqui-hire offer). The opacity is that you can't inspect the tower's internal stress — you learn structural intuition only by watching other players' towers collapse in the shared lobby. Multiplayer towers share a table, and an aggressive investor player can place blocks on YOUR tower if you take their money.

### 281. Waterfall
Pitch: Every funding round adds pipes, valves, and preference traps to a Rube Goldberg machine that nobody — including the investors who built it — can fully simulate; when an exit finally fires, the acquisition money pours in the top and everyone watches live where it actually flows. Every clause is technically public, but the opacity is emergent: stacked preferences, ratchets, and carve-outs interact into outcomes that routinely shock the very people who signed them. Founders learn deal literacy by watching other tables' pour ceremonies in the shared lobby, where a $200M exit trickling exactly $0 to common stock always draws a crowd.

### 282. Microloan Mesh
Pitch: You lend real (in-game) money in $20 slices to strangers' ventures across a persistent player economy, and repayment histories weave a public trust graph that becomes the game's actual terrain — you literally travel along edges of trust to find new borrowers. Defaults come without explanation or apology; your only tools are portfolio spread and reading thin signals in someone's repayment rhythm. Lenders can form circles that co-sign each other, importing all the joy and horror of mutual liability.

### 283. The Data Room
Pitch: Due diligence as dread-soaked exploration: you have 72 in-game hours inside an acquired company's virtual data room — thousands of folders, some empty, some mislabeled, a few containing the skeleton that kills the deal. The seller (a real player) curated the room and gets paid more the less you find, but outright deletion is detectable, so they hide things in plain sight with boring filenames. Asymmetric 1v1 with a spectator mode where the audience can see everything and scream silently.

### 284. Peg
Pitch: Real-time defense game where you operate a stablecoin and other actual players coordinate in open chat channels to break your peg — you deploy reserves, adjust rates, and bluff about your backing while watching the depth chart like a heart monitor. The core opacity is reflexive: whether your reserves are sufficient depends on whether people believe they are, and you can choose to lie in your attestation reports. Attackers split the profits from a successful de-peg; survivors earn fee revenue and, more importantly, a believable history.

### 285. Redemption Run
Pitch: You manage a fund whose LPs are real players who can request redemptions at any moment, so your entire game is managing both a portfolio and a mood — one nervous LP posting in the fund's group chat can start a stampede. Mechanically, your assets have honest values only you can see, while LPs see only your (freely written, possibly rosy) quarterly letters. Bank-run psychology is player-generated; the developers just built the exits.

### 286. Sourced By
Pitch: You're a junior VC whose whole career is a credit-attribution problem: you scramble to get your name attached to deals years before anyone can know if they're good, and the partnership's promotion logic is never written down — one anointed winner you barely touched can outweigh three companies you genuinely sourced. Feedback arrives on a seven-year delay, hopelessly entangled with luck, so the community metagame is folk statistics about what actually gets associates carry. Rival associates are real players racing you to the same founders, and the partners whose deal memos you ghost-write are players too, with their own opaque incentives.

### 287. Note Bomb
Pitch: Convertible notes are rendered as literal ticking devices strapped to your startup — each carries a hidden cap, discount, and maturity fuse, and stacking too many creates interactions no one at the table fully modeled. The loop is choosing between defusing (raising a priced round on someone else's timeline) or riding the timer for more runway. In multiplayer, note holders are real angel players who can extend, convert, or call your note based on how much they like your Slack updates.

### 288. Blind Book
Pitch: You bid on portfolios of distressed consumer debt sold as sealed containers — a page of summary statistics that may be lies, then it's yours. The core loop alternates auction-floor bluffing against rival player collectors with the grim, darkly comic work of actually collecting: calling debtors (procedurally voiced) whose stories complicate your spreadsheet. The seller players know exactly what garbage they've packed, and reputation is the only regulation.

### 289. The Charter
Pitch: A bureaucratic labyrinth game about winning a banking license: you file forms into a system that returns them stamped "DEFICIENT" with no elaboration, and the win condition is constructing a mental model of an examiner rulebook the game never shows you. Runs take weeks of casual play; veterans sell (possibly outdated, possibly false) annotated form templates to newcomers in the player bazaar. The regulator's rules genuinely drift each season, so all institutional knowledge decays.

### 290. Reinsurance Pass
Pitch: Hot-potato risk at the syndicate scale — player-run insurance houses slice catastrophe exposure into layers and pass them along chains of treaties, each transfer shaving premium and obscuring the original risk a little more. When the earthquake finally lands, the game traces liability back through the chain in a dramatic public "unwinding" ceremony that reveals who actually held what. The deepest opacity is that nobody, including you, fully knows their own aggregate exposure until the music stops.

### 291. Two-Sided Mirror
Pitch: Asymmetric 1v1: the founder player sees their startup's true books (which are procedurally troubled in hidden ways); the investor player sees only the deck, the data the founder chooses to share, and their questions' answers — which may be technically true. The investor must price a term sheet; the founder must decide what to reveal to close without triggering a walk-away or a fraud clawback later. Post-game, both screens are shown side by side, which is where the real education (and trash talk) happens.

### 292. Burial Society
Pitch: You found a mutual micro-insurance collective where the members are real players, premiums pool in the open, and every claim is adjudicated by member vote — meaning your product is literally other people's willingness to believe each other. Growth is both existential and poisonous: each new member you recruit is either diversification or an incoming fraud the whole pool will pay for, and nothing on their application tells you which. The founder's real job becomes designing rituals of trust — waiting periods, witness rules, public claim hearings — and watching them fail in a brand-new way every season.

### 293. Forty-Eight Hours Ago
Pitch: A fund-management game where every order you place executes 48 real-world hours later, into a player-driven market that will have moved — so you are always trading against a future you can only forecast, never react to. The discipline it teaches is eerie: your dashboard shows two ghost portfolios, "what you own" and "what you've committed to own," permanently out of sync. Guilds share forecasting models, and the best players write eerily calm 48-hour-ahead memos that read like letters to their future selves.

### 294. Pay-to-Play
Pitch: Your startup needs a bridge round and every dollar must come from your existing investors — five real players with fund sizes, reserve pools, and portfolio politics you cannot see — none of whom will commit until someone else leads. The game is engineering that first commitment: cram-down terms that punish holdouts, manufactured deadlines, and private calls where you tell each investor a slightly different story about what the others said. Investors who catch you cross-briefing can walk; investors who wait too long get diluted to dust; the post-close reveal of everyone's actual reserves is where the lesson lands.

### 295. The Audit Lottery
Pitch: All fund managers self-report their returns to the public leaderboard, and only a random 5% get audited each season — so the game is an honesty dial: inflate and risk spectacular public liquidation, or report true numbers and lose LPs to louder liars. Real-player LPs allocate based on the leaderboard they half-know is polluted, creating a market for credibility signals (voluntary audits, boring consistency). The season-end audit reveal is a community-wide spectator event.

### 296. Power Law
Pitch: You write a hundred tiny angel checks into real players' ongoing ventures, and then the game becomes listening to silence: a founder who stops sending updates might be dead, heads-down, or about to return your whole fund — and the power law means misreading one silence costs more than every other decision you make combined. Your only instruments are the texture of old updates, secondhand gossip, and a single awkward "just checking in" ping per company per quarter. Each quarter you must mark every stake up, down, or dead for your own backers, forcing you to publicly put a price on pure fog.

### 297. Secondaries
Pitch: A marketplace game where you buy and sell equity stakes in other players' ongoing venture campaigns — with zero disclosure requirements, so all pricing comes from gossip, screenshots, and whatever the founder deigns to post. Founders can encourage or sabotage their own secondary price (a high one attracts partners; a low one lets them buy back control cheap). The uncertainty is total: you may own 4% of something glorious or of an account that quietly stopped logging in.

### 298. Parametric
Pitch: You underwrite parametric weather insurance for player-run farms and festivals, writing contracts that pay out on real-world weather data feeds for real cities — so your actuarial edge comes from actually out-forecasting both the buyers and rival underwriters. The rejection texture is that buyers simply don't respond to most quotes, and you never learn if you were too expensive, too complicated, or just not trusted. Seasonal leagues track underwriters' loss ratios across an El Niño cycle.

### 299. The LP Dinner
Pitch: A conversation-driven social game set across one long dinner party: you're raising a fund, and the six other seats are real players (family office heirs, pension officers, a crypto whale) with hidden mandates, hidden pools, and hidden opinions of each other. Commitments made over dessert are only "soft-circled" — the post-dinner phase silently evaporates a random-but-influenceable fraction of them, and closing means engineering social proof so each LP believes the others are in. Twenty-minute sessions, savage replayability, best played with voice chat.

### 300. Exit Liquidity
Pitch: You launch a token project into a persistent player community and genuinely cannot tell — mechanically cannot tell — whether the whales accumulating your token are true believers building alongside you or wolves farming you as their exit liquidity, because the game gives both identical on-chain behavior until the moment of divergence. Your loop is shipping real utility fast enough to convert mercenaries into believers before any coordinated dump. Every collapse and every legitimate breakout is archived in a public "chain museum" other players study like case law.
