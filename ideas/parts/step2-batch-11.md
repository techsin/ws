### 251. Sight Unseen — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Winner's curse and pricing under information asymmetry — you learn to bid what the risk is worth, not what the house might be worth, and to calibrate trust in paid intel of dubious provenance.
Cheats: What's behind the door is close to a pure dice roll per property, so the individual outcome teaches nothing — only the portfolio math does, and most players will experience it as a slot machine.
Note: The gray-market drive-by-report economy is the real epistemic engine (trust calibration vs. real sellers); without it this degenerates into wiki'd base rates keyed off tax-record features.

### 252. Test Pit — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: The value-of-information problem — how much due diligence to buy before an irreversible commitment — which is a genuine founder decision most games never touch.
Cheats: "Veterans' only edge is a private mental map" quietly admits the edge is grind-accumulated map knowledge, which is exactly the attribute-grinding the product brief forbids, just stored in the player's head.
Note: Epistemic and adversarial (fraudulent drilling logs from real players keep it alive), but geology base rates per neighborhood will be spreadsheet-solved unless strata distributions drift.

### 253. Lowball — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Inferring an invisible market from nothing but your own wins and losses — "declined" with no number is exactly how real bids, sales, and fundraises fail, and the winner's curse on underpriced jobs is structurally enforced.
Cheats: The hidden-scope bleed on cheap wins risks feeling like a scripted punishment for winning rather than a learnable relationship between price and diligence.
Note: Silence is genuinely load-bearing here; degeneration is naturally resisted because the "market" is other players' bidding psychology, though out-of-band price-sharing cartels will form (which is itself realistic).

### 254. Yellow Letters — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Funnel intuition against an invisible response curve — 97% silence as the baseline condition of cold outreach, with iteration on list/copy/timing as the only lever, is the truest single mechanic in this batch.
Cheats: If the response curve is a static hidden function, the community regression-fits it in a month and the game becomes copy-paste-the-wiki-letter idle farming.
Note: Needs endogenous decay (other players mailing the same lists burns them out) to stay epistemic rather than solved; the stale-lead-list market is a nice trust-calibration layer.

### 255. Anchor — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: That your product can be an invisible relationship graph you can only sense and nudge, and that churn arrives as a terse non-renewal with the real reason forever withheld.
Cheats: With only seating charts and events as levers against weak noisy signals, the feedback loop is so attenuated that players can't distinguish their judgment from the sim's caprice — tea-leaf reading, not hypothesis testing.
Note: Lives or dies on whether tenant behavior is driven by real players (epistemic, alive) or a hidden synergy matrix (wiki'd in weeks); the pitch is ambiguous on exactly this load-bearing point.

### 256. Locker Ghosts — TEACH: 4/10
Teaches: Rapid inventory valuation under a glimpse, and the visceral fact that one entrepreneur's collapse is another's raw material.
Cheats: A five-second door-crack peek is a perception twitch-test bolted to a lottery — there is no belief to form, no experiment to run, and the poignant real-player provenance is pure narrative garnish with zero mechanical teaching.
Note: Outcome uncertainty is almost entirely RNG at the unit level; the only surviving lesson is portfolio-level bankroll management, which the game doesn't foreground.

### 257. Right of Way — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Signal-versus-noise inference under adversarial misinformation, plus the brutal truth that a perfectly reasoned position can be zeroed by an unobservable political decision — luck made visible without being the whole game.
Cheats: It teaches speculation, not entrepreneurship — there is no customer, no product, no cash-flow operation, just an information-trading contest wearing a land-deal costume.
Note: Decoy crews planted by real players make the epistemic layer self-refreshing and wiki-proof; the last-minute route move must stay rare or it curdles into pure RNG nihilism.

### 258. Net-90 — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: That businesses die of cash-flow, not losses — payroll hitting Friday against a receivable that didn't clear is the single most under-taught founder experience, delivered here as the core loop rather than a status bar.
Cheats: The design depends on real-player GCs having authentic reasons to pay late or never; if non-payment is just a griefing option, the whisper network scores trolls instead of teaching credit judgment.
Note: Epistemic (payer reputation is learnable, shifting, human) and hard to degenerate; factoring-at-a-haircut is a quietly excellent unit-economics teacher.

### 259. Assemblage — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Operational secrecy and staged acquisition against counterparties actively pattern-hunting you — information leakage as the thing that kills deals is real and mechanically central.
Cheats: It's a heist set-piece, not a venture — one specialized play pattern (assemble, conceal, close) with no validation, no customers, and no ongoing business to run.
Note: Adversarial human deduction cannot be wiki'd, which makes it durably epistemic; the capital-stranding failure state (five useless lots) is an honest, painful lesson in commitment risk.

### 260. Track Record — TEACH: 9/10
Teaches: That reputation is the only capital that compounds, that fundraising is narrative craft under scrutiny, and that the market never tells you — or your backers — whether you were unlucky or dishonest; that ambiguity IS the founder-investor relationship.
Cheats: Skilled writers may outraise skilled operators, and the cold-start problem (why do early LPs risk anything on nobodies?) could hollow out the trust market before it forms.
Note: Structurally wiki-proof because the hidden system is other humans' judgment of you; the inbox going permanently silent after two blowups is the most honest rejection mechanic in the batch.

### 261. Main Street — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Ecosystem curation — that your revenue depends on cross-business synergies you can sense but never measure, and that your best assets leave without warning for reasons upstream of you.
Cheats: If synergies are a hidden developer matrix, the community datamines "ramen +12% bookstore" within weeks and the landlord game collapses into deck-building from a tier list.
Note: Only stays epistemic if tenant success is genuinely driven by real-player businesses; also the player is a meta-landlord watching entrepreneurship rather than doing it.

### 262. The Walkthrough — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Estimation under time-boxed inspection with binding consequences, and — via the guaranteed uncatchable defect — why contingency budgets exist; the leaderboard spread of estimates is a brilliant lesson in how wide honest expert disagreement runs.
Cheats: It's a single-player appraisal quiz, not entrepreneurship — no counterparty, no cash-flow, no market — and the learnable tells will be a YouTube curriculum within a month.
Note: Weekly identical houses make degeneration terminal (first streamer solves it for everyone); the epistemic core is real but needs asynchronous or personalized houses to survive contact with the community.

### 263. Leave a Message — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Cold outreach as it actually feels — days of structural silence, craft in thirty seconds of voice, and the adrenaline spike of a live human calling back.
Cheats: With a hundred messages out and no attribution for which one landed or why, the feedback loop is so starved that players can't run experiments — the silence risks teaching fatalism instead of iteration.
Note: The bravest structural commitment to silence in the batch, but silence must be biased-learnable (patterns in who calls back) or it's pure noise wearing an art-game's clothes.

### 264. Stamped — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Reverse-engineering an opaque gatekeeper through collective experimentation — exactly how real founders treat App Store review, ad-account bans, and SEO — with silent dev patches honestly modeling platform rule-drift.
Cheats: It's a Zendo-style rule-induction puzzle in a business costume: there are no customers, no economics, and no venture, just a filing window to defeat.
Note: The silent-patch cadence is a rare structural anti-degeneration design — the wiki is always partially stale by construction — making this the most deliberately epistemic engine here even if the entrepreneurship is thin.

### 265. Key Turn — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Life as a platform-dependent operator — opaque mutating ranking factors reward permanent experimentation over solved builds, and a canceled head-lease teaches concentration risk with real pain.
Cheats: The algorithm mutation is developer fiat dressed as market dynamics; if mutations are arbitrary rather than coherent, players learn "the devs moved my cheese" instead of "platforms drift for reasons."
Note: Real-player guests making real choices keep demand epistemic; this is one of the few ideas where constant A/B testing is the win condition rather than a flavor claim.

### 266. Points and Fees — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Underwriting humans — reading numbers, message cadence, and excuses from real players licensed to lie, then structuring terms (points, draw schedules) so you survive being wrong.
Cheats: The player learns capital-allocator judgment rather than founder judgment — it's the view from the other side of the table, valuable but adjacent to the brief.
Note: Trust calibration against live human deception is permanently epistemic and wiki-proof; defaults handing you half-finished houses is an honest lesson in how collateral is worth less than the spreadsheet said.

### 267. Lot 47 — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Dynamic pricing under demand uncertainty polluted by adversarial misinformation, with rumor-source reliability as a learnable asset.
Cheats: It's one mechanic deep — a pricing minigame with sabotage — and nothing about validation, cash pressure, or building anything; the business is a stat you set nightly.
Note: Epistemic only if rumor sources have persistent identities whose reliability can be tracked; anonymous rumors reduce the whole game to coin-flips with a parking skin.

### 268. Holdout — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Negotiation under hidden reservation prices — reading tells, surviving fake final offers, and gambling on walk-away risk against real humans.
Cheats: It's a poker-like negotiation set-piece, not entrepreneurship — a single skill isolated from any business context, closer to a party game than a venture.
Note: Human-vs-human asymmetric information cannot degenerate into a wiki, which makes the small lesson it teaches durable; but "when to hold out" without cash-flow pressure on the holdout is negotiation with training wheels.

### 269. Ten Years a Week — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Cycle-sense — that leverage is genius on Tuesday and lethal by Thursday, that tops are smelled through deal-flow texture rather than announced, and that timing luck is communal and visible.
Cheats: A fixed decade-per-week cadence hands the community a metronome; once cycle statistics are charted, "smell the top" becomes "read the server clock" and the deepest lesson dies.
Note: Needs genuinely variable cycle length and amplitude to stay epistemic; the shared-server crash as communal grief event is a real and rare piece of emotional truth about markets.

### 270. Unit 4B — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Triage under a budget that covers a third of the demands on it, with trust calibration on which real-player complaints are genuine.
Cheats: The adversarial tenant role pays players to grief, which means the hidden system you're learning is troll psychology, not tenant behavior — a cartoon of landlording sold as judgment.
Note: Feedback arrives only after the pipe bursts, which is honest, but without learnable tenant reputations the uncertainty is unfalsifiable noise; and it's ops management, not entrepreneurship.

### 271. Punch List — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: That inspectors are individual humans with inferable obsessions, and that a lender's interest clock turns every delay into existential cash pressure.
Cheats: A finite roster of hidden personas is a solved game walking — the community will name all seven inspectors and publish per-persona decision trees, converting Bayesian inference into flowchart lookup.
Note: Genuinely epistemic on first contact, terminally degenerate after the wiki; it's a tight roguelike puzzle wearing a hard hat, with one small true lesson inside.

### 272. Attorney Review — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: That deals die silently in someone else's inbox, that commission income means months of work can evaporate without explanation, and that a maintained relationship graph is the only durable asset of a pure intermediary.
Cheats: If deal-death is truly unexplained and unlearnable, the game teaches only fatalism; it needs the "who ghosts, who closes" patterns to be real and discoverable or agency evaporates.
Note: Silence is structural, not cosmetic — the pipeline just stops responding — and because the hidden system is real players' reliability, it stays epistemic without any developer trickery.

### 273. The Tower — TEACH: 4/10
Teaches: Coordination costs — governance stalls, faction politics, and holdup problems (the elevator consortium) as the real risk in any co-owned venture.
Cheats: The player experiences committee warfare, not entrepreneurship — no customers, no validation, no cold outreach, no personal runway — a DAO-politics spectacle with a construction crane on top.
Note: The social uncertainty is authentically human and un-wikiable, but the lesson it delivers (co-owners are hell) is a fraction of the brief, and thousands of stakeholders dilute any individual's learning loop to homeopathy.

### 274. Chain of Title — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Tail risk and delayed feedback — you spend the premium now and learn weeks later what you missed, with each loss teaching a defect species no tutorial mentioned; outsourcing searches imports the vendor's risk appetite along with the work.
Cheats: The defect taxonomy is finite content — once the community catalogs every species of forged easement, the detective work becomes checklist QA and the unknown-unknowns engine runs dry.
Note: The claims-lag structure is the truest model of delayed consequence in the batch; survival depends entirely on whether new defect types keep generating faster than the wiki absorbs them.

### 275. Centennial — TEACH: 3/10
Teaches: Path dependence — that choices ossify into constraints others live inside for decades, and that businesses are transient tenants of longer-lived structures.
Cheats: The player IS the scenery: no outreach, no rejection, no runway, no experiments, no judgment under uncertainty — it's a meditation about entrepreneurship in which the player is structurally forbidden from doing any.
Note: A genuinely beautiful art-game premise that fails this brief almost completely; the entrepreneurship happens to other people while you feel vacancies as decay.
