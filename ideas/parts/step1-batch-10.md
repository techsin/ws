### 226. Commissary
Pitch: You can't afford your own kitchen, so you rent hours in a shared commissary alongside the very players you compete against — your prep happens in full view, your ingredient deliveries get read like tea leaves, and the 2-to-6 AM slots go cheap because nobody wants them. The loop is bidding for hours, disguising what you're actually making, and deciding whether the neighbor's offer to "share the mixer" is generosity or reconnaissance, while the good slots get allocated by relationships nobody will admit exist. Graduating to your own space means betting everything on demand you've only ever tested part-time — and giving up the intel you were quietly gathering on everyone else.

### 227. Shelf Space
Pitch: You've invented a CPG snack and the whole game is an email/sample-box interface: you cold-pitch grocery buyers (played by real players running store empires), and the default outcome is nothing — no reply, no read receipt, no reason. Your only lever is iterating packaging, pitch copy, and which 3 sample boxes you can afford to ship each week. When a buyer finally bites, the terms sheet is brutal and you learn slotting fees exist only when the invoice arrives.

### 228. The Palate Problem
Pitch: A cooking-for-crowds game where there is no taste stat: every real player carries a hidden, evolving palate vector, and your only data is what comes back on the plate — cleaned, picked at, or untouched, with no comment card. You tweak dishes like a scientist running experiments on subjects who won't talk. Diners never rate you; they just do or don't come back, and you find out three sessions later.

### 229. Acquired Taste
Pitch: You sell a food your city has no word for — there is no existing demand curve, only blank stares — and every dollar you spend teaching people what it even is builds a category that copycat players can harvest for free. The loop is missionary work: handing out samples, naming the thing, watching strangers' faces, with no way to tell whether the market is slowly forming or you're just early to a party nobody will ever attend. The cruel math is that the second mover into a category you built often wins it, so the real game is timing your evangelism against the free-riders circling your stall.

### 230. Saturday, 6 AM
Pitch: A weekly-session farmers market game: every Saturday (real time) you bid blind for stall positions, guess the weather, and harvest-or-hold perishable stock against a foot-traffic pattern shaped by which other player-vendors show up. Position 12 next to the popular kombucha lady is gold; the same spot next to a fish guy is death, and nobody tells you why sales cratered. Between markets, players trade stalls, poach recipes, and whisper about which market is "over."

### 231. Rot
Pitch: A roguelike where your inventory decays in real time, including while you're logged off — the core loop is buying beautiful ingredients and then racing your own walk-in cooler to turn them into revenue before they turn into losses. Uncertainty lives in demand you can't forecast and a spot market (fed by other players' overbuying) where today's bargain is tomorrow's compost. Runs end in bankruptcy autopsies that show you what rotted, but never what would have sold.

### 232. RFP: Wedding, 150 Guests, Budget Undisclosed
Pitch: A catering bid game where real players post events with incomplete, sometimes contradictory briefs, and you submit proposals into a void — most die in silence, some get a one-word rejection, and the winning bid is never revealed to losers. The craft is reading between the lines of a brief ("elegant but fun, aunt is gluten free, keep it reasonable") and deciding how much unpaid tasting-menu effort to gamble on a maybe. Reputation accrues invisibly: hosts talk to each other in channels you can't see.

### 233. The Cursed Corner
Pitch: A persistent shared city where every retail space remembers the businesses that died there — failed restaurants leave "residue" (customer distrust, weird lease clauses, a lingering smell mechanic) that new tenants inherit without being told. The loop is scouting locations by interviewing neighbors and dumpster-diving old permits, since the landlord (a player) discloses nothing. Some players get rich flipping cursed spaces they've secretly rehabilitated; others sell haunted leases to newbies.

### 234. Everyone's a Critic
Pitch: You run a small restaurant knowing that a few of the anonymous diners each night are player-critics whose single review can triple or gut your bookings — but critics gain score by staying undetected, so they act like nobody. The loop is deciding whether to comp the quiet guy at table 9, and the paranoia of never knowing which service was The One. Critics have their own competitive economy of credibility, and getting caught fishing for good treatment ruins them.

### 235. Mother Culture
Pitch: A slow game about fermentation-driven products (kimchi, natto, sourdough, cheese) where experiments take real-world days and results come back ambiguous — "funkier than last batch" — with no way to know which of the five variables you changed mattered. You build a product line on processes you never fully understand, and one contaminated crock can quietly wreck a month. Players trade starter cultures with hidden lineage traits, so your best asset might be something a stranger gave you.

### 236. Handshake
Pitch: There are no supply contracts in this game — every ingredient comes from deals sealed with a literal in-game handshake with player-farmers, and either side can walk at any moment with zero penalty except reputation you can't see. The loop is nurturing a web of relationships (showing up at harvests, paying early, forgiving a bad crop) against the constant risk that your tomato guy just got a better offer and simply stopped answering. Betrayals ripple through gossip channels, and old players can smell a deal-breaker.

### 237. Blind Menu
Pitch: A pricing duel where you can never see any competitor's menu or prices — only your own demand curve twitching as invisible rivals undercut, overprice, or close. The loop is reading demand shifts like tea leaves: did covers drop because of your price hike, the new place across the street, or just rain? Post-season, the whole market's price history is unsealed, and players discover how wrong their mental model of the neighborhood was.

### 238. Preorder or Perish
Pitch: You launch a food product via a 30-day crowdfunding campaign where the backers are real players spending real in-game money, and the dominant experience is watching a counter not move. There's no feedback channel — you can see visits vs. pledges but never why someone bounced, so mid-campaign pivots (new hero image, lower tier, panic stretch goal) are pure gambles. Funded creators must then actually deliver through the game's manufacturing minigame, and public delivery failures become cautionary legends.

### 239. Pop-Up
Pitch: You're a nomad chef with no venue: the loop is cold-DMing player bar owners, galleries, and laundromats to host one-night pop-ups, negotiating splits with people who mostly leave you on read. Each event is a high-variance spike — the room, the night, the host's crowd, none of it repeatable — so you're building a career out of unrepeatable experiments. Your trail of one-night stands becomes a following that travels with you, or doesn't.

### 240. The Redacted Code
Pitch: The health-and-permit rulebook in this game is literally printed with black bars, and lines only un-redact after you've been cited for violating them — the whole city is learning the law by breaking it. The loop is deciding how aggressively to operate in the fog: cautious players stay clean but slow, reckless ones eat fines that double as intel. Players sell photocopies of their (partially un-redacted, possibly forged) rulebooks to newcomers, creating a black market in regulatory knowledge.

### 241. Word of Mouth
Pitch: Advertising does not exist in this game — the only way customers learn your restaurant exists is real players telling other real players, through channels the game never shows you. The loop is engineering remarkable moments (a dish worth screenshotting, an absurd ritual, a secret menu) and then waiting in genuine silence, unable to see whether the graph is spreading or dead. The devs publish the anonymized contagion map once a season, and studying past epidemics of hype is the closest thing to strategy.

### 242. The Second Location
Pitch: Your first restaurant works, and nobody — including you — knows how much of that is just you standing in it; opening a second one forces the experiment, because you can only be physically present at one venue per session. The absent location runs on your written SOPs, executed by hired player-managers who start improvising the moment you leave, and the numbers that come back never say whether the dip was the manager, the block, or your food without your hands on it. The loop is choosing where your body goes tonight and watching a business drift in ways your playbook never anticipated — learning that the founder is the one ingredient you can't write down.

### 243. Four Walls
Pitch: Your food truck already prints money, and the game dares you to sign a lease — because nothing in the simulation will ever tell you whether customers loved the food, the hunt, the price, or the accident of where you parked. Going brick-and-mortar changes every variable at once: the following you spent months building may simply not cross the threshold, and rent now arrives monthly whether they do or not. The loop is designing cheap probes before the irreversible leap — a trial residency, pre-sold dinner memberships, a lease with an exit clause you overpaid for — to decompose what your success was actually made of, knowing the only conclusive test is the one you can't undo.

### 244. Regulars
Pitch: A quiet, close-up game about one counter and the anonymous players who eat at it: you learn to recognize regulars only by their orders and habits, since the game shows no names or history. Success is retention you can barely measure, and the sharpest pain is a regular who simply stops appearing — no complaint, no explanation, just an empty stool. The social layer is real: regulars are actual players who chose your counter, and remembering that one takes oat milk is a mechanical advantage.

### 245. Co-Packed
Pitch: Your brand scales only by outsourcing production to player-run co-packers, and their quality is invisible to you until customer complaints arrive weeks later under your name, not theirs. The loop is auditing what you can (a factory visit shows a staged snapshot), diversifying across packers you don't trust, and deciding whether a bad batch was sabotage, sloppiness, or your own spec sheet. Co-packers, meanwhile, play their own margin-squeezing game where cutting your corners is a rational move.

### 246. Worth
Pitch: You cook blind tasting menus for tables of real players who pay whatever they decide it was worth, after the fact, with no itemized bill and no obligation to explain. The loop is composing a menu as an argument for its own price, then absorbing the verdict — sometimes a fortune from a table you thought hated it, sometimes insultingly little with compliments. Diners build reputations as generous or stingy, but you can't see them until after you've fed them.

### 247. Franchise Fever
Pitch: Your concept only scales by selling franchises to other players, who then run your brand with their own hands — badly, brilliantly, or fraudulently — while every one of their health violations and viral moments lands on your shared brand score. The loop is writing an operations bible nobody reads, choosing franchisees from applications full of lies, and deciding when to revoke a franchise (public, ugly, expensive) versus let it rot quietly. Empire here means maximal exposure to other people's judgment.

### 248. Outbreak
Pitch: When a foodborne illness hits the shared city, nobody knows the source — every restaurant's bookings crater together, and the traceback investigation (run partly by player-inspectors) takes in-game weeks while suspicion moves like weather. The loop is deciding how much to invest in unglamorous traceability paperwork that only pays off by exonerating you faster during a crisis. Collective punishment makes food safety a social game: players lobby, snitch, scapegoat, and occasionally frame each other.

### 249. Line, Pick Up!
Pitch: An audio-only game played entirely by voice: customers phone in orders, suppliers negotiate by call, and your inspector delivers verdicts through a bad speakerphone — mishearing "no nuts" is a lawsuit. Uncertainty is literal noise: accents, kitchen clatter, and callers (real players) who mumble, lie, or hang up mid-order. Designed for commutes and eyes-free play, with kitchens run by two players sharing one chaotic line.

### 250. Best Before
Pitch: Every venture you start in this game has a hidden expiration date — a lease clause, a trend's half-life, a landlord's secret plan — and you receive only ambiguous omens: a rent survey in the mail, fewer teens at the counter, a competitor sniffing around. The core skill is pivot timing: cash out, reinvent, or ride it into the wall, without ever knowing how much runway was left. Other players' visible sudden deaths ("that boba place lasted 61 days") are your only actuarial table, so the community obsessively maintains a public graveyard of post-mortems.
