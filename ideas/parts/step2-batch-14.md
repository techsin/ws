### 326. Best and Final — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Your negotiating position is literally your set of live alternatives, and the only honest test of a leverage claim is the costly act of walking away.
Cheats: It isolates negotiation from everything upstream — no product, no runway, no reason the deal matters — so leverage becomes a poker chip rather than something you built.
Note: Uncertainty is genuinely epistemic (even the bluffer doesn't know if their alternative is still live), and human counterparties keep it from being wiki-solvable.

### 327. The Gentlemen's Cartel — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Commodity competition grinds margin to zero and trust without enforcement rots on a schedule — a real lesson about why reputation and repeated games exist.
Cheats: Its core entrepreneurship lesson is "learn to collude better," a cartoon where the answer to zero margin is a lounge handshake instead of building something worth a premium.
Note: Deducing the defector from shipment patterns is properly epistemic, but fifty years of iterated-prisoner's-dilemma literature means the meta gets solved fast and re-run as ritual.

### 328. Blacklist — TEACH: 9/10
Teaches: Rejection in real markets arrives as silence with no exit interview — doors quietly stop opening and you must form and test theories about your own conduct from the shape of what's missing.
Cheats: If secret ratings from real players skew petty or capricious, the inference target becomes mob mood rather than your behavior, and the lesson curdles into paranoia.
Note: Structurally wiki-proof — the hidden variable is other humans' live opinion of you specifically — and the silence is fully load-bearing, not flavor text on a fail state.

### 329. Zombie Fund — TEACH: 9/10
Teaches: Qualifying your counterparty is survival — burning finite runway on meetings with investors who cannot wire is exactly the death every second-time founder learned to avoid.
Cheats: Real zombie funds fool founders for months; a season-length game compresses the reveal until insolvency tells become readable poker patterns instead of genuine opacity.
Note: Runway cost per meeting makes silence expensive rather than cosmetic, and the double game (investors bluffing their own LPs) mirrors reality with eerie precision.

### 330. Locked In — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Sunk, specific investment transfers bargaining power to your counterparty — the holdup problem — and the countermeasures (mutual hostages, a secretly warm second buyer) are real transaction-cost engineering.
Cheats: Once veterans "price the holdup in," the game risks becoming a solved contract-design exercise where the betrayal is a known tax rather than a trap.
Note: Reading which specific partner will honor the spirit of a handshake is epistemic and person-bound, which keeps the wiki from ever fully closing it.

### 331. Vesting — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Cofounder effort is unobservable, and vesting cliffs exist because commitment can only be inferred from output, never proven.
Cheats: Secret hour-allocation sliders are exactly the stat-grinding abstraction the brief forbids — effort is a hidden number, not work — and a 2-4 player couch game is flatly off the MMO target.
Note: With a fixed friend group the deduction collapses into known personalities within a few sessions; the lesson is real but exhausts in an evening.

### 332. The Unexplained No — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Buyers say no for invisible structural reasons that have nothing to do with you, and sales judgment is building folk theories from dozens of unexplained rejections — some of them wrong.
Cheats: Mechanically gagging buyers is an artificial crutch; real buyers give you plausible false explanations, which is harder and more instructive than enforced silence.
Note: The constraint taxonomy will get wiki'd, but which constraint this particular buyer carries stays hidden per-instance, so the inference loop survives the solve.

### 333. Word of Mouth — TEACH: 4/10
Teaches: Contracts, witnesses, and paper trails are inventions with a purpose, which you learn by bleeding without them.
Cheats: The entire premise dies to a screenshot — players will record every deal out-of-game on day one, and the "no ledger" world becomes a ledger with extra steps.
Note: Fatal degeneration risk; the jury-of-storytellers rewards charisma over any judgment a founder would recognize, making the intended lesson decorative.

### 334. Floor Pass — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Selling requires disclosure and every disclosure arms competitors — the openness/leakage tradeoff is real trade-show physics.
Cheats: The post-round reveal of exactly who took what destroys the defining feature of real intel leakage: you almost never find out, and must operate under permanent ambiguity.
Note: A 15-minute round with full reveal trains fast-twitch party-game deduction, not entrepreneurial patience — it's Spyfall wearing a lanyard.

### 335. Assay — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Trust is a manufacturable asset with a brutal asymmetry — built through years of unobservable diligence, destroyed in one scandal — which is the actual economics of brands and certifiers.
Cheats: If scandal discovery is a stochastic roll, corner-cutting reduces to an EV calculation the wiki will publish, turning integrity into a spreadsheet setting.
Note: The buyer side (vetting certifiers from gossip and tea leaves) is properly epistemic; the certifier side risks degenerating into a hidden diligence slider.

### 336. Counterparty — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Reputation has a market price and being reliably boring is a monetizable personal brand — a genuinely sharp observation.
Cheats: The ticker does your trust calibration for you, outsourcing the founder's core skill of judging people to a price feed that whale manipulation then corrupts into noise.
Note: Degenerates into a market-manipulation game about the index rather than the people — players learn short squeezes, not character reads.

### 337. Earnout — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: The contract's numbers matter less than who controls the levers after signing — earnout diligence is character diligence, done over dinners and reference whispers.
Cheats: A market that "remembers forever" grants cleaner reputational accountability than real M&A, where serial earnout-stiffers hide behind NDAs and lawyers.
Note: Epistemic to the core (predicting a specific player's post-close behavior), and the sandbag-versus-reputation tradeoff keeps both seats honest without a solvable formula.

### 338. First in Line — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: In a shortage, allocation is a relationship, not a price — and promises cost nothing until the crunch makes them all come due at once.
Cheats: Between crunches the game is loyalty theater and favor-grinding, dangerously close to farming relationship points against a delayed dice roll.
Note: The day-of-shortage reveal of your true queue rank is superb delayed feedback, but the lesson only fires when scarcity bites, so pacing carries the entire design.

### 339. The Mole in the Pipeline — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Counterintelligence has a price — paranoia burns real revenue and reputation, so the correct amount of leaked secrets is not zero.
Cheats: With deliberately noisy churn signals and honest customers acting suspicious "all the time," the deduction may be statistically hopeless — RNG frustration wearing an epistemic costume.
Note: A narrow single-lesson design; once the community computes base rates, optimal play is a fixed leak-tolerance policy and the drama dies.

### 340. The Licensing Office — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Gatekeeping systems are opaque, undocumented, and staffed by humans — you learn them from approval patterns and half-true folklore, and your institutional knowledge expires without notice.
Cheats: An anonymous player-regulator inventing criteria can drift from "opaque but coherent" into caprice, at which point applicants are reading tea leaves in pure noise.
Note: Rotation is the anti-wiki mechanism done right — every community solve expires with the regime, which is itself the lesson real founders learn about app stores and regulators.

### 341. Off the Record — TEACH: 4/10
Teaches: Confirm it in writing — negotiators reinvent summaries, repetition rituals, and witnesses because memory is an adversarial medium.
Cheats: Self-deleting chats lose to a screenshot; the moment players record off-platform, "strategic misremembering" becomes lying about a document everyone secretly holds.
Note: Same fatal out-of-game degeneration as every no-ledger design, and rewarding successful misremembering trains gaslighting, not deal hygiene.

### 342. Boardroom Knives — TEACH: 4/10
Teaches: Founders do get ousted, and the tells — calendar patterns, sudden friendliness, meeting exclusions — are the genuine texture of a boardroom coup.
Cheats: It's Werewolf with a cap table — no customers, no cash flow, nothing built — so the skill trained is party-game whip-counting, not any judgment about running a company.
Note: The deduction is epistemic but its object is vote counts, not markets; sessions are self-contained set pieces with no entrepreneurial arc to learn from.

### 343. Power of Attorney — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: The principal-agent problem in its purest form — outcomes where bad luck and betrayal are observationally identical, and incentive alignment that is nearly impossible to write down.
Cheats: With the conversation permanently unobservable, some principals will rationally conclude the game is unlearnable noise and quit before the real inference skill develops.
Note: The confounded signal is the load-bearing mechanic, not a bug — one of the few designs where the noise itself is the honest lesson about hiring agents.

### 344. Reference Call — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Extracting truth from polite, conflicted people is a learnable interrogation craft — the actual skill behind backchannel references.
Cheats: The three-call structure is a hiring mini-game, not entrepreneurship — no venture is at stake, so the cost of a bad read is abstract rather than existential.
Note: Rotating everyone through all three seats is a genuine empathy engine; question scripts will get wiki'd, but coached human references adapt, keeping it alive.

### 345. The Long Silence — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Cold outreach is mostly unanswered, attribution under sparse feedback is nearly impossible, and enduring the quiet without spiraling is itself a founder competency.
Cheats: One data point per day with weeks-lagged receipts may sit beneath the statistical floor of learnability — the player experiences pure noise even if the pool is a genuinely learnable system.
Note: The most literal execution of the brief in the batch; retention will be brutal, but the silence is structurally load-bearing and no wiki can patch it out.

### 346. Lemon Syndicate — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Adverse selection means you underwrite the lead, not the deal — track record is the product, and one knowing lemon vaporizes five seasons of it.
Cheats: "Procedurally rotten" deals either carry detectable tells (wiki'd within a month) or carry none (backing becomes a coin flip laundered through the lead's honesty).
Note: The lead-side reputation compounding is real epistemic territory; the backer side teeters between judgment and dice depending entirely on how rot is generated.

### 347. Texas Shootout — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Naming a price you must honor from either side is the purest valuation honesty test in existence — and shotgun clauses favor the liquid party, a lesson lawyers charge real money for.
Cheats: It's one immaculate mechanism, not a venture — nothing is built, so the valuation under test has no roots in anything the player actually did.
Note: Hidden liquidity and bluffed poverty keep it permanently epistemic; game theory supplies a formula, but its inputs are other humans' cash positions, so it never fully solves.

### 348. Shelf Space — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Distribution is the choke point, and when betrayal can't be made impossible you engineer it unprofitable — staged payments, hostages, and coalitions are real deal architecture.
Cheats: With zero recourse beyond reputation, the equilibrium likely collapses into a few trusted gatekeeper oligarchs, freezing the very experimentation the game wants to teach.
Note: A two-sided lesson (makers learn deal structure, gatekeepers learn extraction limits) with epistemic trust reads on both ends; the degeneration risk is oligopoly stasis, not wiki-solving.

### 349. The Consortium — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Moral hazard lives in every venture where credit is shared and effort is private, and the post-mortem gets litigated in gossip rather than evidence.
Cheats: If "true effort" is a hidden slider rather than observable player work, the whole game is dice plus finger-pointing — exactly the stat-allocation abstraction the product must avoid.
Note: The sabotage/shirking/bad-luck attribution is epistemically honest only if effort leaves ambiguous-but-real traces; otherwise it's RNG with a blame phase.

### 350. Port of No Return — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Cooperation without repeated play is the founding problem of commerce, and players who invent escrow guilds and bonded couriers are literally doing entrepreneurship — trust infrastructure is a product.
Cheats: Forgeable reputation passports plus legalized scamming may tip the early economy into a lemon-market death spiral before any institutions can emerge.
Note: The anti-wiki property is structural — the community "solving" the game means building institutions, so degeneration and the intended lesson are the same event.
