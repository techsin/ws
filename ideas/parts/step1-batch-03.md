### 51. Dead Letters
Pitch: An MMO played entirely through in-game correspondence: you build a trading house by writing letters to distant agents, suppliers, and strangers, then waiting real hours or days for replies that may never come. Silence is the core mechanic — an unanswered letter could mean shipwreck, insult, a rival's better offer, or simply distance, and you must commit capital before you know which. Every correspondent might be a real player or an NPC, and the game never tells you.

### 52. The Incanto
Pitch: Sealed-bid auctions for state galley routes in Renaissance Venice: each season players bid blind for convoy charters, then must fill holds and hire crews on borrowed ducats before the fleet sails. Route profitability hinges on diplomatic situations the Senate knows and you don't, and losing bidders never learn what the winner paid or why. Players form syndicates to pool bids and plant spies in rivals' warehouses.

### 53. Kontor
Pitch: You are an outsider trying to gain admission to a Hanseatic trading post, doing favors, gifting, and trading on the margins while your membership petition circulates among a committee of veteran players who vote in secret. Blackballing is anonymous and unexplained; you can only guess whom you offended and try a different approach next season. Once inside, you become the gatekeeper, and the guild's internal politics become the endgame.

### 54. The Relay
Pitch: A Silk Road game where no player travels more than one segment: you buy from the westbound trader at your oasis and sell to the next, so your livelihood depends on markets three handoffs away that you will never see. Demand signals arrive only as distorted secondhand rumor and the prices scrawled on crates. Being cut out of a chain is silent — your partner simply stops appearing at the caravanserai, and you must rebuild relationships segment by segment.

### 55. Ledger of Little Falls
Pitch: A cozy-looking frontier general store where the real game is the credit ledger: everyone buys against next harvest, and you decide who gets how much using nothing but gut feel and gossip. Failed crops, sudden illness, and customers skipping town are all invisible until settling day, when your year's judgment is graded at once. Rival players run stores in neighboring towns, and you can share — or quietly falsify — deadbeat blacklists.

### 56. Underwriters' Row
Pitch: You play a marine insurance underwriter initialing slips in a coffeehouse, pricing risk on real voyages run by other players who may be skilled, reckless, or planning to scuttle for the payout. The loop is reading shipping lists, setting premiums under radical ignorance, and sweating for weeks until ships are posted arrived or lost. Syndicates of players share signatures on big risks, so trust among underwriters becomes its own market.

### 57. The Rumor Bourse
Pitch: A market where the only commodity is news: buy sealed reports of distant prices, wars, and sinkings — of unknown age and truthfulness — then trade on them or resell before they go stale. A report's value collapses as it spreads, but you can never see how many copies already exist. Other players are the sources, planting false rumors is perfectly legal, and a burned source's reputation dies without anyone announcing it.

### 58. Dead Reckoning
Pitch: A sailing trade roguelike where the map never shows your position: you navigate by log line, compass, and star fixes, and half of profit is simply arriving where you intended before your water runs out. The uncertainty is spatial — a rich cargo means nothing if you make landfall on the wrong coast. Passing player ships can exchange position estimates that may be honestly mistaken or deliberately poisonous.

### 59. The Revenue Men
Pitch: An asymmetric bluffing game in ten-minute port calls: smuggler players declare cargo manifests while player customs officers, limited to a few inspections per shift, choose which holds to crack open. Tariff schedules change by secret decree that sometimes reaches the officers late too, so both sides are guessing at the rules they're playing under. Long-run pattern reading between regulars becomes a psychological metagame.

### 60. Hawala
Pitch: You move money across a continent without money moving: build a web of player correspondents who honor your paper chits on trust alone, earning spread on every transfer. Defaults surface only months later when a chain unwinds, and you discover who was rotten all along. Established networks refuse to clear a newcomer's paper and never say why, so you start by overpaying on the fringe until someone vouches for you.

### 61. The Sample Case
Pitch: A traveling wholesaler game where you sell from samples: take orders today for goods delivered next season at today's promise, then scramble to source them as prices and fashions drift underneath you. Cancellations arrive as silence, not notice — a retailer who changed their mind simply isn't at the depot. The retailers are other players, and they remember exactly who delivered short last spring.

### 62. Fair Season
Pitch: The world only exists during scheduled real-time fair weeks, Champagne-fair style: four days of frantic simultaneous trading in a massively multiplayer pit, then the market closes and you spend the interval on asynchronous prep — letters, financing, caravan dispatch — with zero price feedback until the gates reopen. Everything between fairs is committed blind against a market you can only imagine. Reputation from last fair is the only signal anyone carries in.

### 63. Bottle Post
Pitch: A stranger-economy game with no chat, no names, and no contracts: you cache goods and handwritten offers at remote drops — beaches, oases, crossroads shrines — for unknown future players to take, honor, rob, or counter. Your entire business is unenforceable one-sided offers to people you will never meet, and the only feedback is what you find (or don't) when you return. Folk conventions of honesty emerge, mutate, and collapse server by server.

### 64. One Hull
Pitch: Your entire enterprise is a single named ship, forever: upgrades, crew, cargo, and reputation all ride on one keel, and if she sinks, your merchant drowns and the account's story ends. The loop is choosing ever-richer voyages against storms and pirates you can only partially foresee through paid intelligence of dubious quality. Famous surviving hulls become server celebrities, courted for escort pacts and worth betraying.

### 65. Terra Incognita, Ltd.
Pitch: You sell maps as a product: fund surveys, compile charts, and sell them to other players who will bet their ships and caravans on your accuracy. The opacity is inverted — you decide how honest your product is, and the market learns the truth slowly, through who fails to come back. Your reputation is a P&L with a months-long feedback lag, and a rival can forge your imprint.

### 66. The Saffron Bubble
Pitch: A commodity whose value is purely social consensus among players: no NPC ever buys it, there is no sink and no floor, only what the next merchant believes. The loop is talking it up, cornering supply, and timing your exit; the crash arrives without announcement because everyone simply got quiet at once. Servers develop their own manias, folklore, and generational scar tissue.

### 67. The Factor
Pitch: You are the overseas agent for other players' capital: principals consign goods and coin to you to trade "at your best judgment," and you remit proceeds with a letter explaining yourself. Clients fire you by simply never consigning again — no exit interview — so your book of business erodes invisibly until dockside gossip tells you why. The game is performance plus correspondence: factors who write good letters keep clients through bad seasons.

### 68. The Patient Market
Pitch: A trading world populated by the absent: standing orders persist for weeks after their owners log off, so you are constantly trading against decisions made by people who are no longer there, under conditions that no longer hold. You can also dispatch cargo timed to arrive into a future market state, effectively doing business with players who haven't logged in yet. Order books show prices but never freshness, and reading staleness becomes the master skill.

### 69. Entrepot
Pitch: You run a free-port warehouse, renting space and handling everyone's transshipments — and every crate through your doors is information you can read, aggregate, and quietly trade on for yourself. Skim too obviously and merchants route around you without a word of complaint; you learn from the empty bays. Rival player warehouses across the strait compete not on price but on discretion.

### 70. Thirty Coins
Pitch: A tactile money-changer game: dozens of local coinages of drifting fineness, sweated edges, and outright counterfeits cross your table, and the loop is fast sensory judgment — weight, ring, shear — under queue pressure. Mints debase their coin by secret decree, and you find out from your own losses weeks later. Bad coin passes player to player through the economy like a disease, and tracing an outbreak back to its source is a community event.

### 71. Change Alley
Pitch: A social-VR coffeehouse where you pitch expeditions to a room of player investors: post a prospectus on the wall, work the tables, and watch most people simply not look up. Rejection is spatial and unspoken — nobody comes to your corner, and you'll never know if it was the numbers, the rumor about your last venture, or your coat. Funded ventures resolve over real weeks while the room gossips about the returns.

### 72. The Tea Race
Pitch: First cargo home takes the premium and the second takes scraps: an annual season where you race rival players' clippers around the world with zero knowledge of their positions after the start gun. Every sail-versus-safety gamble is made against ghosts; intelligence about rivals can be bought in ports they touched, but it is always days old. Between seasons, players finance, crew, and sabotage each other's refits.

### 73. Peddler's Mile
Pitch: A real-world walking mobile game: your pack peddler advances along his territory as you rack up actual steps, knocking on procedurally placed doors — most never open, some slam, and a precious few become weekly route customers with expectations. Houses give no signal before the knock; the only way to learn a street is shoe leather. Other players' peddlers work overlapping territory, and you discover a poached customer only by her absence.

### 74. House of Years
Pitch: A legacy roguelike where each run is one merchant's lifetime and, at death, only what they wrote down passes on: contacts, routes, debts, and who-to-trust survive solely through the will and journal you bothered to maintain. The game deliberately forgets everything you didn't write, so the meta-skill is deciding what your heir will need to know. Rival player dynasties interact across generations, and grudges outlive the people who started them.

### 75. The Uncodified Port
Pitch: Every port's tariffs, bribes, taboos, and paperwork are real rules the simulation enforces but never documents anywhere: you learn by fines, seizures, and watching what the old hands do. The loop is probing, note-taking, and trading hard-won procedural knowledge as a commodity in itself. The only documentation that exists is player-written pilot books sold in-game — some lovingly accurate, some deliberately wrong.
