### 376. Ring Trade
Pitch: A moneyless exchange where nothing has a price — only wants — and profit means discovering closed loops: the smith's surplus iron feeds the brewer, whose casks feed the fisher, whose catch feeds the smith, and you quietly stand inside the circle taking a cut of every leg. Most posted wants are stale, half are bait, and holding an intermediate good overnight means the loop can close without you. Veteran ringers keep private maps of who really wants what, because the moment a loop becomes public knowledge its margin evaporates.

### 377. Caravan Divergence
Pitch: The world is split into distant regional shards with genuinely different player economies, and you only see prices where your caravans physically stand — everywhere else is week-old rumor. The loop is buying local, hauling across dangerous slow routes, and praying the price gap still exists on arrival; sometimes fifty other players had the same idea and you land into a crashed market. Alliances form around sharing (or deliberately poisoning) price intelligence.

### 378. Recipe Shadow
Pitch: You run a raw-materials business selling exclusively to player crafters whose recipes are secret and constantly being reinvented, so demand for your goods spikes and dies for reasons nobody will explain to you. Gameplay is forensic: watching who buys what together, running small pricing experiments, and shipping speculative stock toward inferred fads before they peak. When you guess a hidden recipe correctly you can corner an ingredient — until the crafters pivot and leave you holding a warehouse of worthless moss.

### 379. The Spread
Pitch: You play a market maker posting standing buy and sell quotes in a chaotic player exchange, earning pennies on the spread while informed players try to pick you off with knowledge you don't have. The terror is structural: a sudden run on your quotes means someone knows something, and the game never tells you what. Guilds of quiet quote-watchers form cartels, and surviving a week without being gutted feels like a genuine achievement.

### 380. Shelf Wars
Pitch: The grand bazaar has only 200 visible stall slots, and the slots themselves are a market — auctioned, subleased, and scalped by player landlords, so your real business might become renting attention rather than selling goods. Foot traffic is real players wandering the market, and a premium slot can still die silently if tastes drift two aisles over. The uncertainty lives in paying tomorrow's rent on yesterday's traffic data.

### 381. Rot Ledger
Pitch: Everything you trade is perishable — fish, gossip, concert tickets, medicine — with hidden and variable spoilage clocks, so cash-flow pressure is literal decay on your shelves. You buy in bulk at dawn auctions with no idea whether today's crowd of real players will materialize, and dumping stock late at half price signals desperation that rival players exploit. Mastery is reading the rhythm of a crowd that owes you nothing.

### 382. Handshake Futures
Pitch: Players sell forward contracts on things that don't exist yet — next season's harvest, an unbuilt ship, a promised shipment — and the game enforces nothing: counterparties can simply default and vanish into the playerbase. The loop is pricing trust itself, demanding collateral, and building webs of vouches, while your own defaults follow you as whispered reputation rather than a visible stat. Entire player courts and escrow businesses emerge as third-party services.

### 383. Cap Table
Pitch: You fund your venture by selling equity to other players, which means pitching into a feed where most investors scroll past without a word and the ones who pass never say why. Once funded, your shareholders are real people who can dump your stock, demand dividends, or quietly coordinate to replace you as founder. The endgame twist: your investors' exit behavior sets your valuation, so managing human skittishness is the actual job.

### 384. Mintmakers
Pitch: There is no official currency — any player can issue their own coin, and money is whatever enough strangers decide to accept this week. You play a would-be central banker managing issuance, redemption promises, and confidence, watching your currency's adoption rise or collapse on rumors you can't trace. Hyperinflating a rival's coin via whisper campaigns is a legitimate, brutal strategy.

### 385. The Dumb Ledger
Pitch: A trading empire game where you never see your own real-time numbers — sales, margins, and market share arrive only as delayed, noisy, occasionally wrong quarterly reports compiled by a bored in-game clerk. You steer a live business through fog, running deliberate experiments (raise one price, watch three reports later) to de-noise your own performance. Rivals with better information-buying habits eat you before you even know you're bleeding.

### 386. Allocation Day
Pitch: Your workshop finally makes the thing everyone wants — and now the game is deciding who doesn't get it. Every week you allocate scarce stock across begging player retailers, knowing the ones you short will remember forever, the ones you favor may scalp it and wreck your street price, and the boom will end on a day nobody announces. It inverts the usual struggle: demand is a flood, and the entrepreneurship is spending scarcity to buy the relationships you'll need when you're nobody again.

### 387. Brownout
Pitch: You sell capacity — millstone-hours, kiln heat, stable stalls, arcane compute — to a player city whose demand spikes with wars, fads, and festivals nobody schedules with you. Every unit of capacity is borrowed money burning while idle, yet the one night you run short is the night the whole market would have paid triple and instead learns your name as the outage. The loop is reading aggregate player behavior like weather, overbuilding on a hunch, and selling interruptible contracts to customers who swear they'll never invoke the guarantee.

### 388. Standard Bearers
Pitch: Player industries run on competing incompatible standards — axle widths, thread pitches, socket cuts — and every component you manufacture is a bet on which one the crowd tips toward. Nobody coordinates the tipping: adoption creeps through thousands of individual player purchases until one morning your warehouse is either the industry default or scrap metal. You can give away free adapters, subsidize young workshops, or join a consortium of rivals you don't trust, but the network effect itself stays invisible until it has already happened.

### 389. Built to Last
Pitch: You manufacture durable goods in a world with a thriving player resale market, which means your fiercest competitor is every unit you've ever sold, circulating secondhand at half your price. Build them flimsy and your name rots; build them immortal and you saturate your own demand in a season. The real game is inventing reasons for repeat business — upgrades, styles, service plans, trade-ins — while watching the used market's prices for the earliest whisper of what your customers actually value.

### 390. The Last Shoal
Pitch: Every fishing company works the same invisible stock, and the supply curve is literally the aggregate restraint of strangers: the fish respawn on a biomass nobody can see. Your only sonar is your own falling catch-per-trip, and by the time the numbers turn, the collapse is already priced into everyone else's panic-fishing. Fortunes go to players who read the commons early — pivoting to aquaculture, buying idle boats cheap, or organizing quota cartels that hold together exactly until the first defector cashes out.

### 391. Honesty Box
Pitch: By ancient charter of the market district, posting prices is illegal — every stall is an honesty box where customers take what they want and drop whatever payment they judge fair into a sealed pouch you open at closing. Revenue becomes pure crowd psychology: presentation, scarcity, district norms, and whether the morning's first customer set a generous tone you'll never witness. You run experiments like a scientist of goodwill — same bread, different cloth, different corner — and the data is a bag of mixed coins that never explains itself.

### 392. Taste
Pitch: A trend economy where goods have zero intrinsic stats — value is pure social consensus among real players, and you're trying to manufacture the next fad before it exists. You seed influencer players with free product, stage scarcity, and watch most of your launches die in total silence while some ugly thing a rival made becomes priceless for no discernible reason. The market's only fundamentals are human boredom and imitation.

### 393. Payroll
Pitch: Your business can't scale without hiring actual players as clerks, haulers, and agents, discovered through a labor market where wage norms are unwritten and your best worker can ghost mid-contract without notice. Interviews are real conversations, poaching is rampant, and paying above-market breeds loyalty you can never quite measure until a rival tests it. The uncertainty isn't in spreadsheets — it's in people.

### 394. Idle Iron
Pitch: You buy the expensive machines — cranes, presses, drills, looms — and rent them out to player ventures too poor or too smart to own them. Utilization is the whole religion: every idle hour is depreciation plus loan interest with no revenue against it, yet every rental is a stranger walking off with your capital, returning it broken, late, or never. Demand tracks booms you can't foresee, so the deep skill is smelling the next district-wide construction craze early enough to have iron on the ground before rates spike.

### 395. Pooled
Pitch: You organize group buys: rallying strangers' soft commitments until the pool hits a wholesaler's brutal minimum, fronting the deposit yourself, and praying the yeses turn into coins. The core lesson arrives weekly — interest is not demand — as enthusiastic players evaporate at payment time, leaving you to eat the shortfall or torch your name by canceling. Succeed and you inherit the allocation fights, the quality complaints, and forty new best friends who expect you to do it all again next week, cheaper.

### 396. Tariff Runners
Pitch: Player-run city-states set their own tariffs, embargoes, and product bans — and change them without notice, sometimes specifically to bankrupt a trader they dislike. You build routes that exploit regulatory gaps, knowing the gap exists only until some player-senator notices your margins. Lobbying, bribery, and getting a friend elected to the trade council are all legitimate supply-chain strategies.

### 397. Depth Unknown
Pitch: A commodities exchange that shows only the tape — completed trades print publicly, but order-book depth, standing interest, and who's accumulating are all invisible. You probe the market's hidden shape with small test orders the way a founder probes demand with landing pages, and big players spoof the tape to fake momentum. Reading three suspicious prints correctly can make your month; reading them wrong makes you exit liquidity for someone smarter.

### 398. On Consignment
Pitch: Your shop owns nothing — every shelf holds other players' goods on consignment, and your cut is the price of your judgment about what deserves the window. Consignors are anxious principals who yank stock without warning, accuse you of skimming, and demand to know why their masterpiece sat unsold while a rival's junk moved — questions your foot traffic never answers. You're learning retail with other people's capital, and your real product turns out to be trust, displayed one shelf at a time.

### 399. Downline
Pitch: You grow by recruiting player sellers who may recruit their own, under a commission plan you design yourself — and the plan is the product, because effort is unobservable and every incentive you write gets gamed within a week. Rank-hungry recruits load up on inventory that never reaches a customer, your best sellers defect with their entire branch, and somewhere out in the tree your name is being promised things you never said. The chilling endgame lesson is watching your own compensation math saturate the market, and discovering the exact week a sales channel becomes a pyramid.

### 400. On Spec
Pitch: You build ships nobody has ordered — eighteen weeks of wages and timber aimed at a market with maybe three serious buyers a season, none of whom announce their intentions. Half-built hulls can pivot — refit the cargo frame for war or whaling — but every conversion burns weeks you already paid for, and finishing into a glutted harbor means selling a masterpiece for its nails. It's inventory risk at its most existential: lumpy demand, brutal lead times, and negotiations where every buyer knows precisely how much your unsold hull costs you per day.
