### 301. Ex Post Facto — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Operating and hedging under genuine regulatory ambiguity, where the rules governing you cannot be fully known — a real emerging-market founder condition.
Cheats: Retroactive criminalization severs the feedback loop — you are punished for past actions under future rules, so no belief you form today can ever be validated, which drifts toward fatalism rather than judgment.
Note: Whether this is epistemic hinges entirely on whether legislature behavior has learnable patterns; as pitched it reads as sanctioned rug-pulls with rumor-flavored coping mechanisms.

### 302. Indulgences, Inc. — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Selling credence goods — products whose quality the buyer can never verify — a real and underappreciated market structure (see: carbon credits, consulting, audits).
Cheats: Because settlement never arrives within anyone's lifetime, neither seller nor buyer ever gets feedback, so the game teaches marketing cynicism instead of the validation loop that defines entrepreneurship.
Note: The Curia's unpublished methodology plus retroactive portfolio-wiping voids is RNG dressed as regulation — a satire target, not an epistemic system.

### 303. Built to Flip — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: The signaling-vs-substance trap — reshaping an entire company for a buyer who then goes silent — plus the bitter truth that building real value is the one reliable strategy nobody can stomach.
Cheats: If building a good business is genuinely dominant, the community will eventually just do that, and the seduction the whole satire depends on quietly evaporates.
Note: Silence after courtship is properly load-bearing, and player-run acquirers with secret, shifting theses make the uncertainty epistemic and permanently un-wikiable.

### 304. Spin Cycle — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Counterparty risk in networks — one sloppy partner sinks everyone — a legitimate lesson about who you let touch your books.
Cheats: "You never learn which discrepancy tipped them off" removes exactly the feedback that would make auditor behavior learnable, so compliance play collapses into superstition.
Note: More a stealth-accounting puzzle than entrepreneurship; the crime family's unexplained expectations are flavor text, not a system you can form beliefs about.

### 305. Nice Place You Got Here — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Decoding polite indirection — the founder skill of hearing "keep us posted" as a no — with real consequences for every misread.
Cheats: Consequences surfacing weeks later and entirely off-screen risk decoupling cause from effect so far that players stop updating beliefs and start guessing.
Note: Player crews deliberately drifting the dialect is a structural anti-wiki mechanic — the phrasebooks going stale IS the lesson, and it cannot be patched out.

### 306. Spot Zoning — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Buying information of unknown provenance — the leaked-map market laced with forgeries teaches paying for intel while pricing in the odds it's a rival's trap.
Cheats: Nightly unexplained redraws are whiplash RNG; real regulatory change is slow and telegraphed, and randomizing it nightly teaches learned helplessness, not anticipation.
Note: The forgery-laced intel market is epistemic gold; the underlying map process as pitched is pure dice — the game is only as good as whichever layer dominates.

### 307. Batch 12 — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: That mentors confidently contradict each other and rankings are theater, while shipping tiny real products to real player-customers is the only true signal — the accelerator experience, correctly cynical.
Cheats: The Demo Day algorithm is a fixed hidden function, so the community WILL reverse-engineer it, and the moment they do the game becomes Goodhart cosplay with a wiki.
Note: The real-products-for-real-players core is structurally sound validation; the mystery-algorithm layer is the degeneration risk unless it re-randomizes by design.

### 308. Reaper, Inc. — TEACH: 4/10
Teaches: Platform dependency and algorithmic precarity — real, but it is the gig-worker lesson, not the founder lesson.
Cheats: The player is an employee of the algorithm; rating drift with undisclosable causes is unlearnable noise, and the loop is timer-grinding jobs — precisely the attribute-grind the brief forbids.
Note: The rival dispatch co-op is the only entrepreneurial seed here, and it's a footnote punished "in ways nobody can prove," i.e., more noise.

### 309. Dead Letter Office — TEACH: 9/10
Teaches: Committing capital against stale information — every real founder decision is made about a market that has moved since you last measured it, and this design makes that lag physical and unfakeable.
Cheats: Real-week latency will bleed out all but the most patient players — the design teaches beautifully but to an audience that may churn before the lesson lands.
Note: Structurally wiki-proof since each player's information lag is their own; silence ("if it arrives") is load-bearing, and misdelivered mail makes luck visible without letting it decide everything.

### 310. Motion to Dismay — TEACH: 4/10
Teaches: That legal process is a competitive weapon and that buying representation from the guilds is a real make-or-buy decision.
Cheats: "Your company exists only as a stack of filings" concedes the game — it's a deckbuilder in legal skin, and deckbuilder metas are exactly the wiki-following optimization the brief forbids.
Note: Invisible clerks re-randomizing the meta is patch-cycle balancing, not epistemic uncertainty; players will experience it as nerfs, not as a market.

### 311. We Regret to Inform You — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: That rejection is mostly arbitrary, outsourced, and unrelated to merit — internalized by administering it — while your own contracts churn silently for reasons no one will share.
Cheats: The player's own business loop recreates the very unlearnable opacity the game satirizes, so your strategic layer may itself be noise you can't play against.
Note: The perspective inversion is the teaching mechanic and cannot be wiki'd, but it delivers one profound insight once, then risks settling into a triage-job simulator.

### 312. Focus Group of the Damned — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: The Mom Test dynamic — sources answer truthfully but obliquely, and extracting real signal from unreliable interviews is the actual skill of customer validation.
Cheats: You never learn whether your delivered insight worked (clients just silently don't renew), which severs the calibration loop the interview skill is supposed to feed.
Note: Rival-bribed lying ghosts turn data poisoning into an epistemic arms race; silent non-renewal is realistic but skirts feeling like pure RNG punishment.

### 313. Trust Exercise — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: When to quit — the math is public, the panic isn't, and reading humans to time your exit is undegenerable because the uncertainty IS other people.
Cheats: Nothing is built here — it's a speculation sim, exercising timing and crowd-reading without any of the making, selling, or cash-flow pressure that anchor them to founding.
Note: Structurally wiki-proof (no guide can tell you when this particular crowd panics), but it teaches market timing, a cousin of entrepreneurship rather than the thing itself.

### 314. Laurels — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Reputation as collateral — the pay-to-play legibility game where skipping the prestige circuit saves a fortune and quietly makes you unbankable, a lesson real founders recognize with a wince.
Cheats: The satire may eat the lesson — if all awards are openly mintable garbage, the interesting question of which signals lenders actually trust can collapse into pure reflexive fashion.
Note: Player-run lenders haircutting player-minted prestige is a genuine trust-calibration market that can't be solved, only surfed.

### 315. Past Due — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Adverse selection and the winner's curse — blind-auction portfolios salted by known sellers force real due diligence and counterparty-reputation learning.
Cheats: Head office's undisclosed remittance pricing makes your unit economics unknowable at the point of decision, crossing from realistic opacity into decision-theory sabotage.
Note: Learning which sellers salt bundles is properly epistemic and stays fresh because sellers are players; the door-knocking collection loop risks becoming grind.

### 316. Knock Knock — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Cold outreach as it actually feels — most doors never open and the game refuses to tell you why, making silence-versus-absence the central, workable-but-never-resolvable condition.
Cheats: The day-commission roguelike framing can shrink sales to a dialogue minigame with permadeath spice, never teaching pipeline economics or qualification discipline.
Note: Route intel degrading as it is shared and farmed is a structural anti-wiki loop — leads are a depleting commons, exactly like real prospect lists.

### 317. The 41st Floor — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Principal-agent hell — every number you see was prepared by someone whose career depends on what you conclude from it, so the epistemic work is modeling your informants.
Cheats: This is the big-company CEO lesson, not the founder one — no cold outreach, no runway, no customer contact; the fog is organizational rather than market-facing.
Note: Genuinely epistemic (player middle managers have learnable agendas) and ouster-on-fictional-numbers is deliciously real, but it teaches management, not entrepreneurship.

### 318. Feed the Code — TEACH: 3/10
Teaches: That compliance burden grows with every complaint and that community knowledge about regulators is perpetually stale — a one-line insight stretched over a pet-care chore loop.
Cheats: Feeding paperwork to a tamagotchi daily is exactly the attribute-grind the brief forbids wearing a bureaucracy costume, and regulation-as-random-monster is a cartoon of the real thing.
Note: The creature evolving in response to being understood is a clever anti-wiki gimmick that backfires — it makes the system unlearnable by design, which is Sisyphus, not epistemics.

### 319. Post-Mortem — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Hindsight bias made mechanical — even knowing the ending you cannot save the company, because the fatal cause was never the scene you would have bet on.
Cheats: If the causal engine "rarely does what you hope," the delivered lesson is nihilism — nothing you decide matters — which is the opposite of judgment; and the multiplayer archive is a museum, not a market.
Note: One of the truest single insights in the batch (post-mortems overfit narratives) attached to a replay structure that cannot sustain it past a few playthroughs.

### 320. Five Stars, Or Else — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: The reputation-economy protection racket from the inside, with the real kicker that even the racket answers to an opaque platform god enforcing unpublished ban rules.
Cheats: The player is the predator and the prey are other players' actual shops — the "entrepreneurship" taught is extraction, while the victims' side (the interesting side) goes unplayed.
Note: The double-layer opacity is atmospherically right, but the racket's own uncertainty (unpublished fraud detection) is mostly unlearnable dice rather than a system to probe.

### 321. The Stamp — TEACH: 3/10
Teaches: That access to gatekeepers is the scarcest resource — then makes it literally the only resource, at which point the lesson stops and feudal politics begins.
Cheats: One physical bottleneck per server guarantees degeneration into cartel control by the largest guild, locking every actual entrepreneur out of the loop the game is named after.
Note: The periodic "the Stamp may have been counterfeit" announcement is a pure rug-pull with no beliefs to update; nothing here is epistemic and nothing here is a business.

### 322. Pending — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: That prices aggregate hidden information — odds anomalies revealing invisible ministry reorganizations is a genuinely elegant markets-as-sensors mechanic.
Cheats: The player is a handicapper of other people's paperwork, not a founder — the entrepreneurial content is a spectator sport with a betting window.
Note: Betting on your own application is a nice skin-in-the-game signal, but the core loop teaches gambling calibration, and the house-never-answers joke is flavor, not mechanics.

### 323. No-Show — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Making bids pencil out while carrying a hidden, non-negotiable cost structure — unit economics under a corruption tax is a real operator's lesson, sharply posed.
Cheats: "No way to know in advance which is which" is stated as design pride, but an explicitly unlearnable landmine roster is RNG execution — the game punishes action and rewards paralysis.
Note: If protection status leaked through rumor, observation, and social play it would be superbly epistemic; as pitched, the central uncertainty is a coin flip with a body count.

### 324. Going Up — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Rejection with zero explanation, embodied — the investor steps out, the doors close, no note — plus the elite skill of manufacturing "lucky" proximity, which is how warm intros actually work.
Cheats: It reduces entrepreneurship to pitch theater — there is an elevator and an investor but no visible business, customer, or runway to be pitching about.
Note: Player investors and tipped operators keep it un-wikiable, and "everyone pretends the tipping isn't happening" is the truest sentence in the batch about how gatekeeping is actually discussed.

### 325. Audit Siege — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: That your own sloppy early records become late-game breach points — administrative debt compounding into existential risk is real and well-spatialized.
Cheats: Tower-defense grammar means the community publishes optimal paperwork-wall builds within a month, converting an epistemics premise into a wiki-following base-layout meta.
Note: Fog-of-war on what auditors know is fine, but attackers pathing deterministically off your bookkeeping history makes the system fully solvable — learnable once, then dead.
