### 451. Corner Ghost Market
Pitch: You place an AR stall on a real street corner, stock it, and walk away; it sells or starves based on real players who physically pass by, and the game hands you nothing but a bare receipt tape at day's end — never a reason why a spot flopped. Rival stalls appear overnight on your best corners and undercut you without a word, so location lore spreads player-to-player like street gossip.

### 452. Proofing Overnight
Pitch: A bakery run on the real clock: dough proofs for actual hours, so you commit tonight's batch before bed with no idea what tomorrow holds, and wake to either a sold-out counter or racks of stale bread. Demand comes from real players in your neighborhood placing standing orders that get cancelled without explanation. The daily verdict-at-dawn rhythm makes checking your phone each morning genuinely tense.

### 453. Dead Drop Freight
Pitch: A courier network built on geocache-style dead drops — you leave virtual cargo at real locations for strangers to relay onward for a cut, and whether anyone ever picks up your leg is pure silence; a package can sit for days with no way to tell if the route died or is just slow. Reputation forms by rumor, and players blacklist flaky drop points rather than people, so geography itself accumulates a credit history.

### 454. Pardon Our Dust
Pitch: You lease dirt-cheap AR frontage on real construction hoardings and scaffolding, betting on how long the disruption lasts and where the rerouted foot traffic of real players actually flows — the city never tells you when a site will finish, and a project that "wraps next month" can run two years or end tomorrow, orphaning your whole storefront overnight. Veterans develop folk instincts for reading cranes and permit stickers, and sell those instincts to newcomers with absolutely no warranty.

### 455. The Empty Chair
Pitch: Your inventory is your actual calendar: you list bookable real-clock service slots that other players buy — or don't — and an unsold hour expires worthless the instant it passes, the purest perishable good there is. Nobody explains why your Tuesday 3pm never sells while 4pm always does; no-shows burn the slot and say nothing; and every pricing experiment takes a real week to run because the week is real.

### 456. Blockholders
Pitch: Claim a franchise territory over real map neighborhoods, but revenue comes from real players who live or commute there choosing — or ignoring — your services, and demographics are invisible: you learn a block is a student area or a food desert only by losing money on the wrong offering. Borders get renegotiated with rival owners over proximity chat or settled through slow, grinding price wars.

### 457. Skyline Auction House
Pitch: Buy AR ad space anchored to real buildings in sealed-bid weekly auctions where you never learn the other bids or why you lost. Ads convert only when real players physically look at them through the app, and viewer data arrives aggregated and three days late, so campaigns are launched into fog. Player cartels quietly divide skylines and periodically, spectacularly betray each other.

### 458. Meridian Traders
Pitch: A 24-hour global commodity exchange where each real time zone's players wake into whatever market state the previous zones left behind; your edge is being awake when others aren't, but nobody can watch the whole clock. You hire players in other time zones on trust, sight unseen — and some simply stop reporting, a fact you discover only through your balance.

### 459. Flashfront
Pitch: Your pop-up shop exists only while you are physically checked in at a real public place, like an open sign tied to your body — and sometimes nobody comes, so you stand in a plaza tending a stall no one visits, which is the loop's honest cruelty. Regulars are real players whose loyalty transfers only if you show up on a rhythm they feel but never articulate.

### 460. The Sourcing Eye
Pitch: You photograph real-world objects — chairs, plants, storefront tiles, textures — to mint them as product designs and list them in a global player marketplace where trends form from raw player taste and die overnight. Flops earn zero feedback rather than bad reviews, so you never know if the design or the timing killed you. Scouts sell each other trend tips, roughly half of which are lies.

### 461. On Consignment
Pitch: You never run a shop — you place your goods on consignment inside other real players' shops, so your income accrues while you're offline, earned inside strangers' live sessions. Sales reports are self-declared and unverifiable: a host claiming "no buyers this week" might be honest, skimming, or simply never logging in, and your only audit tool is seeding identical goods with rival hosts and comparing the silences. Building a portfolio of shopkeepers whose play schedules cover the clock is the real craft.

### 462. Standing Offer
Pitch: You run the buy side: post standing AR offers at fixed real locations — "paying 40 for scrap gears, corner of 5th" — then wait for passing strangers to decide you're worth a detour. Weeks of nothing could mean your price is insulting, your corner is wrong, or the supply never existed in this district, and the game will never say which. Profit means reselling wholesale to player manufacturers whose demand quietly shifts before your stockpile fills.

### 463. Lanes
Pitch: Your real daily commute, walk, or bus route becomes a shipping lane other players pay to use; break your routine and the lane's reliability rating quietly decays with no notification. Nobody tells you what cargo pays on your geography — you discover your neighborhood is a coffee corridor by expensive trial. Lane owners form rate-fixing unions, and scabs undercut them.

### 464. Dead Air
Pitch: Run a micro radio station broadcasting audio segments at fixed real clock times to players physically inside your transmission radius — but listener counts arrive only as week-old aggregates, so you program into an apparent void. Sponsors, who are other player businesses, pull their money without warning or reason. Growth means guessing when your invisible neighborhood actually listens.

### 465. Gutter Gold
Pitch: Every sale other player businesses make sheds virtual waste into their real neighborhoods, and you bid blind for district collection rights to it — a scrap empire living downstream of economies you cannot see. When the morning haul comes back light, you diagnose from a bare tonnage number: did a big shop upstream die, did a rival poach the route, or was it just a slow Tuesday? Rights auctions reprice quarterly, always one step behind the truth.

### 466. Night Ledger
Pitch: A market that only exists between 9pm and 1am local time, populated by whoever in your city is actually awake; goods bought tonight must sell tomorrow night or spoil. Demand is a small, moody crowd of real night owls whose taste shifts with the actual day of the week, and if you sleep through a night you return to find your spot taken with no record of by whom.

### 467. Field Agents
Pitch: A bounty marketplace where player businesses pay for real-world verifications — photograph the queue at a landmark at 5pm, confirm a rival's AR stall still exists — and you run an agency quoting jobs blind, never knowing whether an agent will actually show up. Clients dispute finished work with no arbiter and no transcript, so the only durable asset is a trust network built one kept promise at a time.

### 468. Ghost Landlord
Pitch: Buy geofenced AR retail zones in real places and lease them to other player businesses, setting rents with no vacancy data and no comparables; tenants vanish without notice, a dead zone stays dead for reasons no dashboard reveals, and a spot booms because a real bus route changed. Rival landlords poach your anchor tenants through handshake deals you never see happen.

### 469. Foot Fleet
Pitch: Your literal step count fuels your delivery fleet, but contracts are bid before you know what your week will physically look like — overcommit and rain, injury, or a lazy Sunday makes you default publicly. Deliver early and nobody says thanks; the client just silently books you again, or doesn't. Couriers pool steps into co-ops with brutal internal politics over who's really walking.

### 470. The Sunrise Clearing
Pitch: Every market clears exactly once per day at local sunrise via sealed bids submitted the evening before, so you always transact blind and wake to results; losing bids receive no information — not the winning price, not how close you came. Because eastern players see outcomes hours before western players can act, selling early-clearing intelligence across longitudes becomes its own shady industry.

### 471. Sticker Empire
Pitch: Your entire storefront is AR stickers slapped on real-world surfaces — a business is a constellation of tags that other players may scan, ignore, or paste over, and the overwhelming default outcome is silence: most stickers are never scanned by anyone, ever, and the game refuses to say which died of bad placement versus bad luck. Turf disputes with rival stickerers play out in archaeological layers of overpasting.

### 472. Press 2 for Prosperity
Pitch: Your company exists only as an automated phone-tree that real players call asynchronously — you design the menus, the hold music, the dead ends — and your sole analytics are where callers hung up, never why. Every redesign is an experiment run on strangers who owe you nothing. The endgame is phone-trees hiring other players' phone-trees as departments, forming baroque corporate switchboards.

### 473. While They Sleep
Pitch: A persistent conquest-market where market share can only be taken during hours when the defending player's city is verifiably asleep, so empire-building means either losing sleep yourself or recruiting overseas partners you will never meet in daylight. Betrayal by a partner is always discovered eight hours too late. Everyone's greatest strategic vulnerability is simply having a life.

### 474. Handshake Protocol
Pitch: Contracts, partnerships, and investments execute only when two players' phones are physically within arm's reach, turning every major deal into a real-world logistics problem — investors ghost meetings, a no-show costs you the trip and tells you nothing, and deals die because someone's bus ran late. Cities organically develop recurring meetup spots that function as player-made stock exchanges.

### 475. Annual Report
Pitch: The game year is the real year — one Christmas rush, one summer slump, one tax season per twelve actual months — so every seasonal bet is a single shot you then live with until the calendar comes back around. Competitors' year-end filings show you only their totals, never their methods. Veterans are simply players who have been through more winters, and it shows.
