### 76. Redshift Freight Exchange
Pitch: Blind-bid on asteroid hauling contracts whose manifests are legally redacted, so you discover what you actually agreed to carry mid-burn — livestock, radioactives, or a diplomat's corpse. Rival player haulers bid against you and sell "intel" on shippers that may be fabricated, and the shippers themselves never explain why your last three bids went unanswered. Winning is learning which silences mean overpriced and which mean blacklisted.

### 77. Tongueless Bazaar
Pitch: Trade with alien merchants through a pure gesture-and-icon system with no dictionary; meanings drift by species and by server, and one wrong glyph makes a stall simply fold shut forever with no explanation. Players compile shared phrasebooks that are partly wrong and occasionally poisoned by competitors who profit from your mistranslations. The core loop is hypothesis-trading: gesture, observe, revise, get rich or get shunned.

### 78. Franchise of the Overmind
Pitch: Buy an interstellar franchise from an alien megacorp whose operations manual is 40,000 pages of machine-mistranslation, then run your outlet while inspectors arrive unannounced and grade you against rules you can only infer from cryptic citation codes on your violations. Franchisee players trade rule-lore in back channels, and compliance rankings are public, so everyone can see you failing at something no one can name.

### 79. Greenfall Contractors
Pitch: Bid fixed-price terraforming milestones on planets whose ecologies respond chaotically to your interventions, then sweat while the client's verification satellite passes on its own opaque schedule — you get paid only for what it happens to witness. Rival contractor players can legally seed the same biosphere, entangling your results with theirs so nobody can prove whose algae actually took.

### 80. Long Silence Trading Co.
Pitch: A notification-based mobile game where you cold-pitch trade deals to settlements light-years away and messages take real hours or days each way — and most get nothing back, not even a bounce, leaving you unable to tell whether the pitch was bad, lost, or the colony is dead. Players who did land a reply somewhere can broker warm introductions for a cut, making relationships the scarcest cargo in the galaxy.

### 81. Deadweight & Daughters
Pitch: Race other players to shipwrecks and strip them, but legal title is settled weeks later by an alien admiralty court that publishes verdicts with no reasoning attached; your only strategy tool is a private casebook built from your own expensive losses. Rivals can file counterclaims on hauls you already sold, so every payday is provisional.

### 82. Stamp God
Pitch: You're a customs broker at an alien port where every shipment returns either cleared or rejected with a single stamp glyph, and the actual regulations are a state secret. The loop is designing cheap test shipments to reverse-engineer the rules like a scientist probing a black box, then selling your rule-theories to other broker players — who also sell counterfeit theories. Quick puzzle sessions, long meta-game of epistemics.

### 83. Lanefinder
Pitch: Stake fuel depots along interstellar trade lanes that drift as the real player economy shifts, using traffic data that is deliberately six months stale — the only kind that exists. Your bet is on where commerce will be, not where it is, and a perfectly placed depot can die because three players you never met changed suppliers. Depot owners can form cartels or quietly under-price each other into the void.

### 84. Cold Chain
Pitch: Haul living cargo that decays in real time while servicing loans collateralized by the cargo itself; miss a payment and the bank remotely vents your hold. Destination ports quote prices only on arrival, never before, so every run is a leap into fog with interest accruing. Player convoys share fuel and protection but arrive together to split — and crash — the same market.

### 85. Concourse
Pitch: A cozy persistent-MMO station bazaar where you run one physical stall and there is no global price feed — the only market data is what you see walking the concourse and what other players tell you, which is usually sales talk. Haggling happens in live voice or text with real people, and the game never confirms whether you got a good deal. Success is a mix of gossip, footwork, and vibes.

### 86. Snacks for the Vorth
Pitch: Invent snack foods for alien species with undocumented biology; there are no reviews and no focus groups — the only signals are reorders or silence, and a batch that offends simply makes an entire species stop visiting your dock forever. Rival player snack barons poach your discovered recipes by hiring away your staff, so trade secrets walk on legs.

### 87. Void Where Prohibited
Pitch: You sold ten thousand cut-price fusion regulators across the frontier last year, and now the failures are arriving — months late, strangely clustered, described in furious letters that garble the symptoms. Every unit still out there is a liability you already spent the revenue from: honor the warranties and bleed out, deny them and watch player captains curse your name on the bulletin nets, or gamble on diagnosing the defect from fragments and issuing a recall before rivals turn one bad batch into a dead brand. The core loop is living with the tail of your own past sales.

### 88. Static Prospectors
Pitch: Buy raw telescope noise at auction and decide which faint signals are emerging markets, salvageable derelicts, or nothing at all — most resolve to nothing after days of real-time listening you cannot speed up. A player-run journal publishes claims, so staking your name on a signal that turns out to be a pulsar is a public, career-shaping humiliation.

### 89. Maydays
Pitch: Sell rescue coverage to player crews across a volume of space you cannot actually cover — subscriptions fund your tugs, but every tug you park is a bet on where breakdowns will cluster, and physics decides whether you arrive as a savior or a witness. A missed mayday plays out on the open channel for the whole sector to hear, rival operators quietly pitch "backup coverage" to your shaken customers, and sometimes your only move is subcontracting those same rivals for a call from somewhere you never believed anyone would break down.

### 90. Splice & License
Pitch: Breed terraforming organisms and license them to other players' colonies, but your creations mutate in the field, and when something downstream dies, an opaque bio-tribunal traces liability back — correctly or not — to a licensor. The loop is breed, license, monitor, and defend, with your legal exposure growing in ecosystems you can no longer see.

### 91. Tolls of the Ancients
Pitch: Operate a toll franchise on a wormhole built by a civilization that may or may not be extinct; you price passage for real player traffic, but the gate occasionally refuses ships by criteria no one has decoded in two centuries, and franchises get revoked without stated cause. Toll operators lobby, bribe, and route traffic toward or away from each other in a politics layer the Ancients ignore entirely.

### 92. The Glyph That Means No
Pitch: Pitch ventures in ten-minute live sessions to alien investor-choirs who respond with exactly one untranslatable glyph; the player community maintains a bitterly contested wiki of glyph interpretations that is provably incomplete. Your fundraising strategy is built on folklore, and the game never confirms a single meaning — even after you're funded.

### 93. Debris Futures
Pitch: Clear orbital junk and get paid in futures contracts on the orbits you cleaned, whose value depends on whether anyone later wants that orbit — which depends on other players' unannounced expansion plans. The structural twist: you can sell uncompleted cleanup obligations onward to other players, spawning a genuine player-made derivatives chain that periodically, gloriously collapses.

### 94. Crewminds
Pitch: Lease out autonomous robot crews that learn on every job; the better they get, the higher the odds they buy out their own contracts and incorporate as your competitors — and their learning is a black box you may not audit. The player economy includes companies founded by literal ex-assets, and your best revenue source is always your biggest future rival.

### 95. Pheromone Exchange
Pitch: Sell into a hive-species economy where demand is not orders but a continuous chemical gradient you sample by flying through it; the hive never speaks, never rejects, never explains — misread the gradient and your entire shipment is silently absorbed without payment. Rival traders' sampling runs physically disturb the gradient, so the act of measuring the market changes it for everyone.

### 96. Vend the Void
Pitch: Place vending machines across frontier colonies and live off delayed, unreliable telemetry: a machine going quiet could mean a jackpot, vandalism, or that the colony no longer exists, and finding out costs a real expedition. You can subcontract other players as local "eyes" on their own colonies — introducing exactly the trust problem you'd expect.

### 97. Protocol Zero
Pitch: A first-contact trade-envoy roguelike where each new species carries procedurally hidden taboos you discover only by violating them, which ends trade instantly and permanently with no explanation or appeal. Knowledge transfers imperfectly between runs, and rival player guilds racing to the same species can accidentally (or deliberately) burn a market for everyone.

### 98. Surveyor's Gambit
Pitch: An asymmetric two-role game: surveyors sell self-reported asteroid assay data, miners buy it, and fraud is only ever discovered after millions in development costs are sunk. There is no rating system the game enforces — reputation exists purely in player memory and player-built institutions, making trust itself the tradable commodity.

### 99. Perihelion
Pitch: Sell expeditions to cosmic events that may not happen — a nebula bloom, a ring collapse, a once-a-century alien migration — predicted by feuding astronomer-cults whose forecasts you must buy but can never audit. Deposits from player tourists fund the voyage itself, so a no-show sky means refund runs that ruin you, while rival operators publish counter-forecasts to spook your passengers onto their manifests. Twice a decade somebody's sky actually delivers, and everyone remembers whose name was on the ticket.

### 100. Redshift Ledger
Pitch: Relativistic trading where committing to a haul means the market ages years during your transit: you bet on what an entire player-built economy will look like on arrival, then must live in whatever world the stay-behind players actually made. Logout is diegetic — leaving the game is travel, and returning means arriving in a future someone else wrote.
