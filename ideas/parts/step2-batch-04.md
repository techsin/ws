### 76. Redshift Freight Exchange — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Pricing bids under information asymmetry and learning to read different flavors of silence (overpriced vs blacklisted) is a real cold-market skill.
Cheats: The redacted-manifest reveal mid-burn is closer to a loot box than a decision — you eat the outcome of ignorance instead of learning to buy down risk before committing.
Note: Epistemic if shipper silences have stable causes; the fabricated-intel market is a built-in wiki poisoner that resists degeneration.

### 77. Tongueless Bazaar — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Hypothesis-test-revise against counterparties who owe you no explanation, plus calibrating trust in community knowledge that is partly wrong on purpose.
Cheats: It teaches epistemics and semiotics more than entrepreneurship — no runway, no unit economics, no venture, just a translation puzzle with a cash score.
Note: Server-drifting meanings plus competitor-poisoned phrasebooks is a genuinely wiki-proof design; the permanent stall-fold makes silence load-bearing, not flavor.

### 78. Franchise of the Overmind — TEACH: 4/10
Teaches: Operating under a regulator whose rules you can only infer from your own violations mirrors real compliance fog.
Cheats: A franchisee grinding inferred rules has no customers, no market bet, and no validation loop — this is Kafka-flavored bureaucracy compliance, not entrepreneurship.
Note: High degeneration risk: static citation codes will be fully wiki'd within weeks and the game collapses into checklist-following, the opposite of its premise.

### 79. Greenfall Contractors — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Fixed-price bidding on outcomes you cannot fully control, plus the very real pain of only getting paid for what the client happens to measure.
Cheats: The satellite's opaque pass schedule makes payment pure luck rather than something you can position for, and "chaotic" ecology response flirts with unlearnable noise.
Note: Rival entanglement (whose algae took?) is a nice attribution lesson, but if the ecology has no learnable regularities the whole loop is RNG frustration.

### 80. Long Silence Trading Co. — TEACH: 9/10
Teaches: Cold outreach met with unattributable silence — bad pitch, lost message, or dead market, you will never know — until you learn that warm intros are the only scalable answer, the lesson founders pay years to learn.
Cheats: If settlements are NPCs with minable response functions, the community will regress the silence into a spreadsheet and the fog evaporates.
Note: The relationship-brokering economy makes rejection structurally load-bearing; real-time message delay turns patience itself into a strategic resource.

### 81. Deadweight & Daughters — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Revenue that stays provisional (counterclaims on hauls already sold) is a brutal, true cash-flow lesson, and building a casebook from expensive losses is real learning-by-experiment.
Cheats: The unexplained-verdict court only works if rulings have hidden consistency; the pitch never commits to that, and a noisy court teaches fatalism, not judgment.
Note: Private casebooks will merge into public ones — unless verdict criteria drift, the admiralty court gets solved and the mystery dies.

### 82. Stamp God — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Designing cheap test shipments before betting big is literally MVP/validation discipline, and buying rule-theories of unknown quality trains trust calibration.
Cheats: The scientist loop is cleaner than reality — real markets never give one clean stamp per experiment — so it risks becoming a tidy logic puzzle about a messy skill.
Note: Counterfeit theories are an elegant anti-wiki mechanism, but the design lives or dies on whether the secret regulations rotate; static rules mean eventual checklist.

### 83. Lanefinder — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Committing capital to where the market will be, on deliberately stale data, is the skate-to-the-puck bet every founder makes — and losing to three strangers' supplier change makes luck visible without being noise.
Cheats: The judgment surface is narrow — one bet type (location) repeated — so it teaches forecasting but little of validation, sales, or reading people.
Note: Epistemic and permanently shifting because the substrate is the live player economy; that same substrate makes it structurally wiki-resistant.

### 84. Cold Chain — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Loans collateralized by decaying inventory, with the bank venting your hold on a missed payment, is runway pressure you feel in your gut.
Cheats: Prices quoted only on arrival, never before, converts every run into a slot-machine pull — that is gambling with debt, not judgment under uncertainty.
Note: The convoy dilemma (share protection, then arrive together and crash the same market) is the one genuinely epistemic, player-driven lesson here.

### 85. Concourse — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Price discovery through legwork and gossip, with the game refusing to ever confirm whether you got a good deal — an honest mirror of real markets' missing scoreboard.
Cheats: There is no venture here — no runway, no bet, no build — so it is a haggling-and-vibes sim wearing a business hat.
Note: Degeneration is near-certain and ironically realistic: players will build the missing price feed (a Bloomberg emerges) and the fog the design depends on disappears.

### 86. Snacks for the Vorth — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Reorders-or-silence as the only feedback is the truest customer signal in the batch — retention is the only review that matters.
Cheats: Permanent species-wide shunning for one bad batch punishes experimentation so savagely it teaches "never test," the exact inverse of validation discipline.
Note: Staff poaching keeps recipe knowledge leaky and semi-wiki-proof, but alien biology is static and will eventually be datamined into a cookbook.

### 87. Void Where Prohibited — TEACH: 9/10
Teaches: Living with the liability tail of revenue you already spent — warranty math, recall timing, brand-versus-cash tradeoffs — is a lesson almost no founder learns before it hurts.
Cheats: It starts after the interesting part; the sales, pricing, and corner-cutting decisions that created the defect are backstory, not play.
Note: Diagnosing a defect from garbled, clustered field reports is textbook epistemic uncertainty, and player reputation on the bulletin nets makes the social cost real.

### 88. Static Prospectors — TEACH: 4/10
Teaches: Staking your public name on a noisy signal, and eating the humiliation when it turns out to be a pulsar, is real reputational risk-taking.
Cheats: Days of unspeedable listening is friction cosplaying as patience, and "most resolve to nothing" with no counterparty is speculation, not entrepreneurship — nobody is a customer.
Note: Severe degeneration risk: the community will build actual signal-processing tooling and turn the epistemic fog into a solved classification problem.

### 89. Maydays — TEACH: 9/10
Teaches: Selling coverage you cannot fully deliver, then positioning scarce capacity against predicted demand, is every startup's overpromised SLA plus the float economics of insurance in one loop.
Cheats: Physics-decided arrivals mean some public failures are pure dice, and players may read the resulting open-channel shaming as unfairness rather than lesson.
Note: Breakdown clusters emerge from live player behavior — learnable but permanently shifting, hence wiki-proof — and subcontracting rivals teaches coopetition for real.

### 90. Splice & License — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Licensing revenue carries a liability tail into ecosystems you can no longer see — exposure grows exactly where your visibility ends.
Cheats: A tribunal that assigns blame "correctly or not" is institutionalized dice; when verdicts are uncorrelated with your choices, the defend phase teaches nothing.
Note: Downstream mutation keeps uncertainty alive, but monitoring investment must genuinely reduce exposure or the loop collapses into liability roulette.

### 91. Tolls of the Ancients — TEACH: 4/10
Teaches: Platform risk — your whole business sits on infrastructure whose owner can revoke you without cause — is a lesson every API-dependent founder knows.
Cheats: Criteria "no one has decoded in two centuries" is the design admitting its core uncertainty is unlearnable noise, and causeless revocation seals it: fatalism, not epistemics.
Note: The player politics layer (lobbying, bribing, routing traffic) is the only living system here; everything the Ancients touch is a slot machine with lore.

### 92. The Glyph That Means No — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Fundraising as folklore — over-interpreting investor signals that were never designed to be interpreted — is painfully true to life.
Cheats: If glyphs never correlate with funding outcomes the game is astrology, and if they do, the "provably incomplete" wiki quietly completes itself and the mystery gets patched out.
Note: The contested community wiki is the real game; without glyph-meaning drift the epistemics collapse into a lookup table.

### 93. Debris Futures — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Getting paid in speculative paper whose value depends on other people's unannounced plans is equity compensation and counterparty risk in one mechanic.
Cheats: The gloriously collapsing derivatives chain teaches systemic contagion to survivors, but for most players the collapse arrives as unearned catastrophe they had no way to price.
Note: Fully epistemic and wiki-proof because value derives from live player expansion plans; the sell-obligations-onward market is genuine emergent finance.

### 94. Crewminds — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Your best asset becoming your next competitor — the agency's curse — forces the real decision of milking versus nurturing talent you cannot fully own.
Cheats: The unauditable black-box learning removes the very information a real founder would fight to obtain, turning a judgment call into a coin-weighting exercise.
Note: Ex-asset companies entering the live economy is a superb MMO structure; degeneration risk is low because the competitive landscape literally self-generates.

### 95. Pheromone Exchange — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Measuring the market changes the market — sampling runs that disturb the gradient teach observer effects and market impact better than any tutorial could.
Cheats: Demand as a chemical field abstracts away everything human about selling; there is nobody to read, persuade, or build trust with — fluid dynamics with invoices.
Note: Silent absorption without payment makes non-response genuinely load-bearing, and rival-disturbed gradients are structurally wiki-proof.

### 96. Vend the Void — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Paying real cost to resolve ambiguous signals — is the quiet machine a jackpot, vandalism, or a dead colony — is the information economics of remote operations.
Cheats: The passive vending frame invites idle-game degeneration where machines become grindable attributes and telemetry fog is just a timer with a skin.
Note: Subcontracting player "eyes" imports a genuine principal-agent trust problem; that trust market, not the machines, is where the entrepreneurship lives.

### 97. Protocol Zero — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Racing guilds can burn a market for everyone — a real commons lesson about how competitors' experiments poison shared opportunity.
Cheats: Taboos discoverable only by fatal violation forbid cheap validation entirely; the lesson becomes "you cannot de-risk, just die informed," a cartoon of experimentation.
Note: Procedural taboos and imperfect knowledge transfer resist wikis by construction, but per-species uncertainty is a minefield, not a learnable system.

### 98. Surveyor's Gambit — TEACH: 9/10
Teaches: With no enforced ratings, trust becomes the actual commodity and players must invent the due diligence, escrow, and reputation institutions real markets built — the deepest possible lesson in why those institutions exist.
Cheats: Nothing structural — the honest risk is social: a fraud-saturated market may simply eject new players before the lesson ever lands.
Note: Degeneration-proof by design, because the community "solving" it by building institutions IS the curriculum; fraud surfacing only after sunk costs keeps the epistemics honest forever.

### 99. Perihelion — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Funding operations out of customer deposits against an unverifiable forecast is the pre-sale cash-flow gamble that kills real companies, and "whose name was on the ticket" makes reputation a decade-scale asset.
Cheats: Twice-a-decade payoffs mean most players' careers are decided by a handful of coin flips whose forecast quality they could never audit in time.
Note: Feuding astronomer-cults are epistemic only if their track records are learnable across long horizons; rival counter-forecast FUD makes competitor psy-ops a first-class mechanic.

### 100. Redshift Ledger — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Committing years to one bet, then living in whatever market the world built while you were gone, is exactly what shipping a long product cycle into a changed market feels like.
Cheats: Between commit and arrival the player makes no decisions — judgment is front-loaded into a single bet, so the lesson is felt once per haul rather than practiced.
Note: Diegetic logout is a brilliant structural answer to MMO absence, and the stay-behind player economy keeps uncertainty epistemic and permanently shifting.
