### 351. Scar Capital
Pitch: Every bankruptcy wipes your company, cash, and product, but your Rolodex persists across lifetimes: every NPC and real player you ever charmed, stiffed, or ghosted remembers you in the next run. No UI shows which bridges are burned — you find out only when a cold call goes strangely quiet or a door that opened last life stays shut. The Rolodex is mostly real players whose own runs determine whether they greet you as customer, investor, or creditor with a grudge.

### 352. Ninety Days
Pitch: A run is exactly ninety days of runway on a hard clock, and each day you get one big action: build, ship, collect, or cold-email. Outreach goes to real players who are mid-run themselves and usually never open it — the game deliberately refuses to show delivered-versus-ignored, so you burn days guessing whether to follow up. When a player hits day 87 with empty pockets, their final three days broadcast as a spectator "deathwatch" where strangers can wire a last-second lifeline, or just watch.

### 353. The Post-Mortem Forge
Pitch: When your company dies you must write its post-mortem by assembling evidence fragments from the wreckage, and the finished document is forged into an equippable relic whose powers derive from what you blamed. The game never confirms your diagnosis — equip a lesson built on the wrong cause of death and it quietly sabotages your next run. Other players can read, buy, and publicly contest your post-mortems, and a successfully contested lesson transfers its power to the challenger.

### 354. Ghost Rival
Pitch: Death here is founder burnout, not bankruptcy: when you collapse, your company survives without you and is handed to the market simulation or a hired real player as its new CEO. Your next run frequently lands in the same sector, where your old firm now outcompetes you using strategies you taught it — and you no longer get to see inside it. The community endgame is founders acquiring or destroying their own ghost companies while other players earn a living captaining them.

### 355. Personal Guarantee
Pitch: Mid-run loans exist, but the only collateral lenders accept is your permanent layer: pledge a contact from your lifetime Rolodex, a hard-won scar, or your standing with a player faction, and default hands it over for good. The lenders are real players who shop for distressed founders specifically to strip the relationships they covet, and their offered terms quietly tell you how doomed they think you are. Every cash crunch thus forces the roguelike's cruelest question — which piece of everything you have ever built do you feed to this run to keep it alive one more month.

### 356. Rejection Deck
Pitch: A deck-builder where the only cards you keep are the rejections you collect: every "no" becomes a card whose power scales with how badly it stung, and you pitch procedurally generated gatekeepers specifically to harvest better refusals. Pure silence mints blank cards with effects that stay hidden until the moment you play them, so your deck is partly unknowable to you. Players trade rejections on an open market, and a scathing refusal from a famous player-run VC is a chase legendary.

### 357. The Ten-Minute Term Sheet
Pitch: An entire run is a single ten-minute investor meeting structured like a dungeon: dialogue rooms, doors as topics, traps as taboo subjects, and a procedurally rolled hidden psychology behind the desk. Death is being walked to the elevator with a warm smile and no explanation, and nothing persists between runs except your own read on human tells, since the same investor archetypes recur in remixed disguises. Some sessions the investor is secretly a live player who only profits if their portfolio picks survive.

### 358. Overdraft
Pitch: A side-scrolling platformer where the floor is literally your bank balance — revenue raises the ground under your feet, burn erodes it, and dipping below zero is the pit. Invoices dive-bomb you, payroll day is a recurring spike wall, and an unexplained seasonal wind shoves everyone without warning or pattern notes. All players' businesses scroll on parallel tracks in the same sky, and aggregate spending shifts the shared interest-rate gravity for the whole server each day.

### 359. Night Market
Pitch: An MMO night bazaar where your whole company is one physical stall and your lease is your permadeath timer, paid nightly out of cash flow. Foot traffic is real players, and the market's layout reshuffles by algorithmic rules nobody is told — a golden corner one night is a dead alley the next, and the whisper network trading tomorrow's layout rumors is at least half planted lies. Alliances form row by row, and rows can pool rent to save a dying neighbor or quietly let them fold to claim the spot.

### 360. The Creditors Remember
Pitch: Every new run begins with the debts of your old ones personified: collectors from past bankruptcies physically hunt you across the map while you try to build the next thing. You can pay, flee, or renegotiate, but their patience rules are hidden and their arrival timing is random, so a knock on the door mid-product-launch is always possible. Other players can buy your old debt on the secondary market and become your literal pursuers.

### 361. Cosigned
Pitch: You cannot incorporate a new run until a living player signs your charter, staking a slice of their own standing on your survival — and nobody has to explain a refusal, or respond to your request at all. Your failure history determines who will even open your file, so first runs are begged into existence from strangers while veterans become choosy godparents curating portfolios of protégés. When you go under, everyone who ever vouched for you takes the hit, and the web of who-signed-for-whom becomes the game's true map of trust, obligation, and quiet grudges.

### 362. The Gatekeeper Spire
Pitch: A dungeon crawler where floors are funding stages and the monsters never attack — they ignore you: gatekeepers drift through the halls and your limited signal items (warm intros, demos, stunts) are your only way to engineer their attention before your runway torch gutters out. Most attempts produce nothing at all, and the run ends in darkness rather than combat. Players leave Souls-style graffiti on the walls — "this one answers flattery," "door is fake" — and some of it is sabotage.

### 363. The Understudy
Pitch: Nothing you learn survives a bankruptcy unless your understudy — a shadow character you drag through the run — personally witnesses it, and they can only stand in one room at a time. Each day you choose whether they sit in on the pitch, the factory floor, or the brutal creditor call, and your next founder wakes up remembering only those scenes, misreadings and all. Understudies can also be rented out to other players' runs, so some of the sharpest next-lives are built on tuition paid by watching strangers fail.

### 364. Same Week, Different Market
Pitch: A time-loop roguelike where you relive the same launch week, and each loop you know more — but the week itself keeps shifting because other players' loops interleave with yours and their actions bleed into your market. The cruelty is attribution: you can never cleanly tell whether the customer who vanished this loop was your mistake or some stranger's ripple. Death is exhausting your founder's attention budget, and post-run you can compare loop diaries with the players who were secretly warping your week.

### 365. Ghost Franchise
Pitch: If your company dies with any brand recognition at all, it becomes a franchise template that other players can license and operate, paying you ghost royalties that bankroll your future runs — so one viable strategy is build fast, die famous. You cannot control or even fully observe what franchisees do under your name, and a scandal in their run poisons your royalty stream without warning. The leaderboard tracks living empires and dead brands side by side, and some of the richest players haven't run a live company in months.

### 366. Founders' Rest
Pitch: A persistent graveyard-town hub where every headstone is a real player's dead company; between runs you wander it, and tending a stranger's grave unlocks that founder's recorded final week as a playable memory. The memories are first-person, biased, and incomplete — the dead founder never knew why their biggest customer went silent either — so extracting the real lesson is your problem. Wisdom currency is earned only through remembrance, creating a quiet economy where the most-visited graves belong to the most instructive failures.

### 367. The Only Employee
Pitch: Each run you may hire exactly one other real player, negotiating salary, title, and duties in freeform chat; they can carry your company, quietly coast, or walk out with your customer list. You see their outputs but never their effort, and firing on suspicion costs severance you can't afford. Meta-progression is your boss reputation on the in-world review board, which silently determines who will ever agree to work for you again.

### 368. Control Group
Pitch: Every run spawns your company as identical twins in sealed parallel markets, sharing one cash pool and one founder's attention that you split however you dare. Diverge their decisions to learn what actually matters — but demand noise differs between the twins by hidden amounts, so when one thrives you still cannot prove it was your choice and not the coin flip. When the shared pool runs dry you must merge and keep only one, and the community's endless argument over everyone's published twin data is the closest thing this world has to a business school.

### 369. Seed of the Day
Pitch: A daily-challenge roguelike where every player on earth gets the identical market seed — same customers, same trend curve, same hidden shock on day four — and exactly one attempt. Nothing about the seed is announced; its crucial variables reveal themselves only through play, so you commit capital before you understand the world. At midnight all runs unlock side by side, and the daily ritual is watching a thousand founders make a thousand different fatal reads of the exact same reality.

### 370. Non-Compete
Pitch: Every bankruptcy permanently bars you from the industry that killed you, so your hard-won expertise is always stranded one door behind you and each new run must be built in territory you do not yet understand. Choosing where you can afford to fail becomes the real strategy, and grizzled players are walking maps of forbidden sectors. The only way to monetize a banned mastery is consulting for real players still active there — who must decide whether advice from someone who died in that market is wisdom or the reason they will join you.

### 371. Sunk Years
Pitch: Your founder has one lifetime, and every run spends it: years tick off for each venture, each pivot, each stubborn month you refuse to fold, and when the clock runs out your account retires for good. Waiting for better market timing burns the same irreplaceable currency as building, so the core agony is never knowing whether to quit fast or dig in — with luck deciding, in hindsight, which was right. Young founders have energy and nothing to lose, old ones have networks and dwindling chances, and the server's economy is a churn of age cohorts trading what the other lacks.

### 372. Board Purgatory
Pitch: Bankruptcy does not send you to a menu — it sends you to purgatory as a board director assigned to living players' companies, and governance is the only way to earn your next seed check. Founders must answer to a boardroom of the recently failed, each pushing agendas warped by their own deaths, and a board that loses faith can vote you out of the company you started. Conduct follows you in both directions: tyrant directors find nobody will fund their comeback, and founders who ignored every warning arrive in purgatory to face the very directors they snubbed.

### 373. Scar Tissue
Pitch: Every failure inscribes a permanent, visible mark on your founder's body — a repossession brand, an eviction scar, a lawsuit tattoo — and scars are the only progression: certain districts, investors, and player factions open exclusively to people bearing specific marks. You cannot choose what a failure leaves on you, and some coveted doors require scars you'd have to earn the hard way. Scars are un-fakeable, so reading a stranger's forearms tells you their whole career before they say a word.

### 374. Estate Sale Sunday
Pitch: A short, cozy interstitial game: your startup just died, creditors are emptying the office in real time, and you have one hour to pack a single box — a contact, a prototype, a grudge, a habit, a chair. Whatever fits in the box is your entire inheritance for the next run, but nothing indicates future usefulness at packing time, and the movers won't wait while you deliberate. Everything you leave behind ships into other players' foreclosure crates, so your discards become strangers' mystery loot.

### 375. The Number You Have Reached
Pitch: An SMS alternate-reality game where the business texts you on your actual phone at realistic, inconvenient hours — investors, customers, suppliers, scammers — and your company lives or dies by what you reply and how fast. Most threads simply go dead without explanation, and you will never learn which silence was a test, a lost deal, or a bot; some numbers are other players on burner identities. Permadeath is your number being marked "out of business" and burned forever: a new run means a new number, and nobody you knew has it.
