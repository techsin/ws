### 201. Dead Air Dispatch
Pitch: You play a fledgling freight broker working two phones, matching real-player shippers with real-player carriers before loads go stale on a live board. Most of your cold calls and load offers land in literal dead air — the game renders silence as an in-game resource drain, and you never learn whether a carrier passed on your rate or never saw it. Reputation is whispered between players in a rumor channel you can't read until someone finally trusts you with a load.

### 202. Golden Sample
Pitch: A sealed-bid sourcing game where you send RFQs for your product to player-run factories and get back quotes that vary 10x with no explanation — quality, honesty, and capacity are hidden until your first shipment arrives and you crack open the crate. The core loop is betting real working capital on suppliers you can only judge by fragments: a photo, a quote, a delivery date they may or may not hit. Factory players build long-game reputations by deciding which customers get their good line and which get the night shift.

### 203. Preorder Purgatory
Pitch: You crowdfund a gadget by promising specs to a live audience of player-backers, then discover what it actually costs to build — tooling quotes, BOM drift, and certification surprises arrive after the money is committed. The loop is writing public update posts that keep backers from refunding while you privately triage which promise to break, and the game never tells you which update tone works. Backers can organize, leak your DMs, or double down and become evangelists.

### 204. Demurrage
Pitch: An asynchronous mobile game where your containers sit in player-governed ports accruing real-time storage fees, and the loop is bidding for berth slots, gate appointments, and chassis in murky micro-auctions whose clearing rules each port authority player sets and never publishes. Uncertainty lives in the queue itself: you can pay to jump it, but you can't see it. Alliances of importers form to share intel on which ports are quietly broken this week.

### 205. Scrap Rate
Pitch: A production-line roguelike: each run you assemble a factory from salvaged machines whose defect distributions are hidden, and the only instrument you have is the scrap bin — you infer which station is killing you from the pattern of ruined batches. Runs end when a customer rejects a lot, and death teaches you statistics, not lore. Between runs you trade machine "folklore" with other players, half of which is wrong.

### 206. Keystone
Pitch: A slow multiplayer strategy game where every player builds a supply chain, unaware that deep upstream many of them share the same single-source supplier — a mine, a chip fab, a family-run plating shop. The core loop is mapping your own dependency graph before a cascade proves you never knew it; the game only reveals shared dependencies through failures. Players who discover a keystone early can quietly secure capacity or sell the secret.

### 207. Cross-Dock
Pitch: A real-time spatial game where you run a 3PL warehouse and inbound freight from other players' businesses arrives in unpredictable surges you must slot, stack, and turn around before dock doors jam. Client acquisition is the opaque part: prospects tour your warehouse, say "looks great," and ghost, and the game gives you no signal about why. Your one lever is that other players' delivery performance publicly depends on your floor.

### 208. Winner's Curse
Pitch: A tense bidding game where manufacturing contracts go to reverse auction against other player shops, the specs are deliberately incomplete, and winning usually means you missed something everyone else priced in. The loop is deciding what a drawing doesn't say — tolerances, packaging, liability — and the buyer never explains why you lost or, worse, why you won. Veterans profit by seeding the bidder pool with misleading questions in the public Q&A thread.

### 209. Lost in Translation Mfg. Co.
Pitch: You develop a physical product with an overseas factory, but every message you send passes through a lossy translation layer that literally garbles your specs in ways you only discover when samples arrive weeks (real hours) later. The core loop is designing communication that survives corruption — drawings, jigs, reference objects — rather than designing the product itself. Players who've cracked a factory's dialect sell "translation memory" to newcomers, accuracy not guaranteed.

### 210. Pull
Pitch: A structurally weird economy game with no money: the only currency is kanban cards flowing upstream from end customers, and you can produce exactly nothing until demand pulls it from you. The loop is positioning yourself in other players' value streams and starving or flooding rivals by how you release cards; opacity lives in never seeing demand beyond your immediate neighbor. Entire player chains die of a bullwhip nobody in them caused.

### 211. Lot 4471
Pitch: A forensic thriller in reverse: 10,000 of your units are in the field and the first defect reports trickle in, so the loop is tracing symptoms back through lots, shifts, and player-run subsuppliers before regulators or the press force a full recall. Suppliers may quietly amend records, and the game never confirms root cause — you act on a theory and eat the consequences. Other players short your brand in a public market while you investigate.

### 212. Last Time Buy
Pitch: A chip deep in your product's BOM gets an end-of-life notice: one final production run, order now or never again. The loop is betting years of working capital on lifetime buys against demand nobody can forecast — buy too little and your product dies mid-life, buy too much and the pallets entomb your cash. Guess low and your only recourse is the gray market, where player brokers offer reels of possibly authentic, possibly re-marked stock, and every unit you sacrifice to verify their claims is a unit you can never buy again; speculator players who cornered the last run quietly set the ransom.

### 213. Line Down
Pitch: A 20-minute crisis sprint game: a customer's assembly line is down, penalty clauses are ticking per minute, and you're the expediter working a phone tree where half the numbers — some played by real players — ring out into nothing. The loop is triaging who to wake up, what favors to burn, and when to charter a jet for a $4 part. Your call history becomes a public scoreboard of who answers for you at 3 a.m.

### 214. Upstream
Pitch: You start as a garage assembler and climb the ladder by acquiring the player-run businesses that supply you — but every acquisition is a mystery box, because seller books in this world are unaudited fiction. The core loop is due diligence with no due diligence: plant visits, gut reads, and discovering the real liabilities after the keys change hands. Sellers craft their own deception, and burned buyers' war stories are the game's true content.

### 215. MOQ
Pitch: A push-your-luck gambling game where minimum order quantities are the ante: to play a product at all you must commit to 5,000 units of cash you don't have against demand nobody knows. The loop is poker with inventory as chips — raise (reorder), fold (liquidate), or bluff (fake sell-through to spook competitors) — while shelf space at player-run retailers is auctioned each season. The house never shows you true demand, only your own sell-through.

### 216. Ghost Shift
Pitch: A structurally weird factory game you can only *not* play: your plant runs exclusively while you're logged off, executing the standing orders, incentive schemes, and escalation rules you left behind, and you log in each day to consequences, grievances, and a night-shift culture that evolved without you. Opacity is total — you see outcomes, never the shift itself. Rival players can poach your foreman or bribe your ghost shift to run their parts.

### 217. Tariff Weather
Pitch: Trade policy behaves like weather — fronts of tariffs and export bans roll across the map with rumors preceding them — but the twist is that the "weather" is endogenously generated from aggregate player behavior, so the herd's panic buying literally causes the storms. The loop is positioning inventory and rerouting lanes on rumor, knowing the rumor mill is also player-made. Nobody, including the developers' dashboard, can tell you what next season holds.

### 218. Rolodex
Pitch: A relationship deck-builder where your contacts are cards representing real opted-in players — a customs fixer, a mold maker, a produce buyer — and cards decay if you only call when you need something. The loop is spending scarce attention keeping relationships warm, and the opacity is human: cards can simply stop answering with no status effect explaining why. Your deck is your business; a poached card can gut it overnight.

### 219. Bill of Monsters
Pitch: A dungeon crawler where the dungeon is literally your product's bill of materials: each component is a room, sub-assemblies are branching corridors, and single-source suppliers are bosses you cannot route around. The loop is descending the BOM tree to "clear" (source, qualify, contract) every node before your launch date, while lead-time traps re-lock rooms you thought were done. Other parties raid the same dungeons, and a boss slain by a rival means their exclusive supply deal is your dead end.

### 220. Co-Packer Speed Dating
Pitch: A scheduled live event game: food and beverage founders get eight four-minute video-booth pitches to player-run contract manufacturers whose capacity slots are brutally scarce, and most of the room leaves unmatched with zero feedback. The loop across weeks is refining a pitch against pure silence — no scores, no notes, just matched or not. Co-packer players develop cult reputations, and intel on "what Hillside Foods actually wants" trades at a premium.

### 221. Box Diaspora
Pitch: An asset game with a weird structural core: the shipping containers you lease out physically travel through *other players' game sessions* — appearing in their ports, warehouses, and mishaps — and may return late, damaged, converted into someone's site office, or never. The loop is pricing leases against a tracking system that is canonically unreliable. Recovering a lost box means hiring player skip-tracers in regions you'll never visit.

### 222. The Wrong Map
Pitch: A routing game where the official map is guaranteed inaccurate — bridges out, weight limits unposted, warehouse hours fictional — and the true network state exists only in crowd-annotations left by player drivers, some stale, some sabotage. The loop is quoting delivery promises against a world you can't verify, then eating the difference. Route knowledge becomes tradeable property, and cartographer-players sell subscription corrections of varying honesty.

### 223. Pilot Purgatory
Pitch: You sell industrial hardware into factories run by player corporate buyers with hidden org charts, and every enthusiastic contact offers you a "pilot" — the loop is diagnosing which champions have real budget authority and which are using your demo to look innovative, with no mechanic that ever labels them. Deals die in silence after months of warm meetings. Sales veterans compile scouting reports on buyer players like fantasy football stats.

### 224. Net-90
Pitch: A rhythm game about cash flow: revenue and expenses land as beats on a timeline, but customer payments arrive 60-90 days behind the work while suppliers demand cash up front, so you're playing a song where the melody is time-shifted against the bassline. Missing a payroll beat isn't a combo break, it's insolvency; factoring invoices lets you drag beats earlier at a punishing discount. Multiplayer chains sync tempos — your customer's late beat becomes yours.

### 225. Teardown
Pitch: An espionage-lite crafting game where you buy rival players' actual in-game products off the shelf and physically tear them down at a workbench to infer their BOM, suppliers, and margins — the game shows you parts, never answers. The loop is turning teardown intelligence into cost advantage while seeding your own products with decoy components to poison rivals' analysis. Public teardown reports become content, propaganda, and occasionally libel.
