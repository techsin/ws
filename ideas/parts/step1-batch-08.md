### 176. The Org Chart Is the Dungeon
Pitch: Crawl a fog-of-war map of a Fortune 500 buyer where rooms are departments and stakeholders are encounters; every meeting you earn reveals a little more of the map, but surprise re-orgs redraw the corridors mid-run. The economic buyer's chamber is hidden, and some doors (blockers) never open no matter what you bring. Rival vendor players crawl the same org simultaneously and can lock doors behind them.

### 177. Musical Chairs
Pitch: In enterprise sales the account is a fiction — the person is the asset — so this game throws out territories and makes you invest in individual buyers who hop jobs across a live, player-driven career graph. A champion who bought from you at one company resurfaces eighteen months later with budget at another, or vanishes into a role with no purchasing power, and you never know which until the music stops. Contacts are real players climbing their own corporate-ladder game, so cultivating someone junior today is a genuine bet on an unknowable future org chart.

### 178. Wired
Pitch: Sealed-bid RFP poker: you receive a 40-page RFP with subtle clues about whether it was pre-wired for a rival, and must decide bid/no-bid, price blind, and how many column-fodder hours to burn. Losers hear nothing but "we went in another direction." Competing players see each other's public wins in a shared vendor lounge, breeding paranoia, table-talk, and grudges.

### 179. Expo Hall
Pitch: A short-session MMO where hundreds of vendor players work booths on a live convention floor, pitching passersby (real players) in 30-second windows and scanning badges. Lead quality is unknowable for weeks — the great conversation goes nowhere while the guy who only grabbed a stress ball signs six figures a month later. Booth placement auctions and after-party invite lists form the social metagame.

### 180. Feed Your Champion
Pitch: A creature-care game where your inside champion is a fragile pet you feed with decks, business cases, and small political wins; starve them of ammunition and they go quiet, overfeed them and they burn credibility internally. They can change jobs overnight and orphan your whole account. Champions are real players climbing their own corporate-ladder game, so their incentives are genuinely misaligned with yours.

### 181. Stage 9 of 10
Pitch: A procurement roguelike: each run pushes one deal through a procedurally generated gauntlet of security review, legal redlines, vendor portals, and budget re-approvals, with permadeath at any stage and no explanation beyond "finance froze new vendors." Meta-progression is pattern knowledge in your head, not stats. Ghosts of other players' dead runs litter the stages as cryptic warnings.

### 182. The Committee
Pitch: Asymmetric hidden-agenda multiplayer: five players form a buying committee, each holding secret personal objectives (empire-building, CYA, revenge on IT), while two or three vendor players pitch into it. The published evaluation rubric is fiction; deals close only when a vendor skillfully or accidentally satisfies enough hidden agendas. Post-game reveals are the water-cooler moment everyone plays for.

### 183. Rootbound
Pitch: A garden sim where deals are plants whose health lives in roots you cannot see, and watering (touchpoints) can drown them as easily as save them. Each week you must commit a forecast number to your VP — a real player — and the invisible roots decide whether you look like a sandbagger or a fantasist. Your sales pod shares one greenhouse and one quota.

### 184. Bottle Mail, Inc.
Pitch: Outbound-as-ocean: craft cold sequences and launch them into a live player sea where 97% sink without a ripple, and your only data is drift patterns. Recipients are other players who owe you nothing — no ratings, no rejections, mostly nothing at all. Sending costs a reputation currency you can only earn by replying thoughtfully to other players' bottles, making spam self-extinguishing.

### 185. Margin Vassals
Pitch: A feudal channel empire: recruit real-player resellers as vassals who sell your product for margin, but you never hear what they tell customers or whether they secretly carry a rival lord's banner. Deal-registration disputes resolve through an arbitration trial-by-combat mini-game. It plays at empire scale, but betrayal is the core resource being managed.

### 186. The Portal
Pitch: A co-op escape room where your already-closed-won deal is trapped inside the buyer's vendor-onboarding portal, and you and your deal-desk teammates must solve its hostile UI: W-9 uploads that fail silently, an insurance-certificate riddle, a supplier-diversity form that resets at step nine. The room's rules change without patch notes. Speedrun leaderboards exist for each procurement-platform-inspired dungeon.

### 187. FOLLOW UP
Pitch: A one-button game: the entire interface is a single button and a deal that has gone silent — press too soon and you're needy, too late and it's gone, and the game never says which, revealing only closed/lost weeks later. A daily-puzzle format gives everyone on earth the same silent account. Players compare their timing graves in a shared graveyard feed.

### 188. The Quarter
Pitch: A legacy game played in weekly 20-minute sessions across a real 13-week quarter, where decisions leave permanent marks — a bridge burned in week two is still ash in week eleven. Between sessions the world moves without you: champions quit, budgets freeze, a competitor announces. Your live sales pod shares one legacy board and argues over every irreversible move.

### 189. Whale Team Six
Pitch: Heist-structured co-op: assemble a crew — AE, sales engineer, exec sponsor, lawyer — to land one whale account, each seat filled by a real player with a role, a cut to negotiate, and a hidden personal agenda. The blueprint is an account plan drawn from incomplete recon, and the alarms are procurement tripwires nobody mapped. One crew member might be interviewing at the target company.

### 190. Bake-Off Royale
Pitch: A live proof-of-concept battle royale: eight vendor teams build against a buyer's vague success criteria in a shared arena while the buyer refuses to clarify requirements and scores on a hidden, partly irrational rubric. Elimination announcements carry zero feedback. Spectators bet on outcomes, and eliminated teams can linger in chat spreading FUD.

### 191. Use It or Lose It
Pitch: Every buyer org runs on a hidden fiscal calendar — budget flush windows, freeze months, re-approval cliffs — and deals only close when your push lands inside a window you were never told exists. Your craft is timing archaeology: dating an org's fiscal year from stray clues like hiring bursts, conference no-shows, and the week their whole team went quiet. Buyers are real players managing actual use-it-or-lose-it budgets in their own game, so a perfect pitch in the wrong month dies unexplained while a mediocre one in flush week sails through.

### 192. The Steak Dinner
Pitch: Social deduction over a one-hour dinner party: among six buyer guests sit one true economic buyer, one saboteur quietly championing your rival, and four people who just love free steak. Conversation cards, seating choices, and who grabs the check are your only moves, and you must commit your proposal to one person by dessert. A wrong read earns a polite thank-you note and no explanation.

### 193. Redline
Pitch: Clause-by-clause tug-of-war across a contract-shaped map against a real player cast as opposing counsel — who is paid by the hour and literally scores points for delay itself. Each hard pull risks waking the sleeping giant of GC escalation. You never learn which clauses the buyer actually cares about versus reflexive lawyer territory-marking.

### 194. Commit
Pitch: Liar's-dice forecasting up a live management chain: reps commit numbers to manager players, who aggregate and commit upward to a VP player, who commits to a board. Underlying deals resolve from real buyer-player behavior, so every layer is bluffing on partial truth. Getting caught sandbagging and getting caught with happy ears carry different, asymmetric punishments.

### 195. Cold Case: Enterprise
Pitch: A noir detective game where every case is a stalled deal: interview witnesses, pull CRM records, hunt for the re-org memo or the budget freeze. The structural twist is that some cases are genuinely unsolvable — the game generates stalls with no discoverable cause, and mastery includes knowing when to close the file unsolved. Players publish case files that others can reopen and contest.

### 196. Switchboard
Pitch: Arcade cold-calling: you get eight seconds of attention, and every good conversational beat buys five more, against gatekeepers who are real players scoring points for protecting their executive's calendar. Hang-ups are adversarial and never explained. Dial lists are roguelite runs, and the leaderboard ranks meetings booked per hundred dials, not talk time.

### 197. Land & Expand
Pitch: Territory strategy on live shared org-maps: enter an enterprise through one tiny $4k crack, then spread across business units via renewals and internal referrals while rival vendor players expand from other corners of the same map. Fog of war hides budget cycles, and M&A events can suddenly merge two players' maps into one bloodbath. Co-sell alliances form and break mid-campaign.

### 198. Before the RFP
Pitch: You play the game before the game exists: months ahead of any RFP, you seed requirements, brief analyst NPCs, and ghost-write "neutral" evaluation criteria through your contacts. When the RFP finally publishes to all vendor players at once, everyone scans it for whose fingerprints survived the drafting. You never learn which seeds took root until the document drops.

### 199. Kriegspiel Deal
Pitch: Blind-chess selling: you and a rival vendor maneuver on a board where neither sees the other's pieces, and the buyer acts as referee, reporting only vague and occasionally false signals like "there's interest in your direction." Moves are meetings, content plays, and pricing gambits. The referee is a third player whose incompetence or private agenda you must model along with your opponent.

### 200. The Rumor Ledger
Pitch: An intelligence marketplace where players buy and sell account intel — org charts, budget whispers, "the CIO hates your competitor" — with no accuracy guarantees, no provenance, and no refunds, so stale and planted intel circulates for months. Your deals live or die on what you paid to believe. Counter-intelligence, including seeding false rumors about your own pipeline, is a fully legal move.
