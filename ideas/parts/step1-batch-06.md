### 126. Pallet of Fog
Pitch: You bid on sealed import pallets at a customs-overflow auction using only sensory clues — weight, rattle, water stains, a half-torn manifest in a language you don't read. The uncertainty is total: contents are unknown until you've paid, and resale value is set entirely by what real players happen to want that week in the shared flea market. Rival bidders can plant fake "insider tips" about pallets, so even your information is a market.

### 127. The Corner Is Yours (Until It Isn't)
Pitch: You claim sidewalk pitches for your cart in a persistent city where no spot has legal title — claims are held by daily presence, favors to neighboring vendors, and small payments to people whose authority is never verified. Evictions arrive as unannounced "beautification sweeps" with zero warning or explanation, and you can never learn the schedule, only rumors of it. The social game is a fragile web of real-player vendor alliances that decide whether your corner is defended or quietly sold out.

### 128. Ninety Days of Rain
Pitch: A run-based street food cart survival game: each dawn you pick a location, prep quantity, and one menu tweak, and each dusk you count what sold and what rots. Demand is a hidden distribution you can only infer from sellouts and waste — the game never shows you why a day flopped. Other players' carts occupy the same city and silently siphon or generate foot traffic, so your best spot can die because someone you never met parked two blocks upstream.

### 129. Say Nothing, Sell Everything
Pitch: A ranked 1v1 haggling duel with hidden reservation prices, where silence is a playable card: stay quiet and the other side may crack, but every beat of silence burns your day's remaining selling time. There's no matchmaking rating shown and no explanation when opponents walk away mid-deal — you just watch them leave. Five-minute matches against real players who bring real tells, bluffs, and grudges.

### 130. The Bale
Pitch: You buy sealed 45kg bales of secondhand clothing graded with letters that turn out to mean different things from different suppliers, then sort, price, and hawk the contents at a player-populated market. Supplier honesty is learnable only through repeated ruinous purchases, and the "A-grade" stamp is a lie exactly often enough to keep you guessing. What's fashionable — and therefore valuable — emerges from real players' aggregate buying, so a bale of dead stock can become gold if the meta shifts.

### 131. Tontine
Pitch: You join rotating savings circles with real strangers: everyone pays in weekly, one member takes the whole pot, and the turn rotates — but anyone who has already collected can simply vanish. There is no escrow, no enforcement, and no recourse; the only security is social memory, vouching chains, and the reputation whisper-network players build themselves. The game is deciding who to let in, when to take your turn, and whether this is the week your circle's quiet member stops answering.

### 132. Cold Calls at the Kiosk
Pitch: An asynchronous sourcing game played through an in-game messaging app: you cold-message wholesalers (a mix of NPCs and real players) to stock your kiosk, and most messages get read receipts and then nothing, forever. The core skill is crafting openers that earn replies and reading meaning into different flavors of silence. Real players can play the supplier side, where profitably ignoring lowballers is itself a strategy — so the wall you're pounding on has a person behind it.

### 133. Inspector Rotation
Pitch: You run an unlicensed stall under an enforcement regime whose rules are real, consistent — and never published, changing entirely whenever officers rotate. You reconstruct the current rulebook only from fines, confiscations, and which neighbors got shut down. Vendors share intel in player-run channels, but tips can be stale or deliberately poisoned by rivals who want your spot raided.

### 134. The Fixer
Pitch: You sell nothing but introductions: player buyers and player sellers who can't find each other pay you to connect them, and your contact book is your entire capital. Any two parties can cut you out after the first deal, and you'll never be told — the referrals just stop coming. Your reputation exists only in what other players say about you in rooms you're not in.

### 135. Suitcase Economy
Pitch: You fly between two server regions with a strict kilogram limit, buying what's cheap here to sell where it's scarce there — but scarcity is only discoverable by being on the ground or trusting other players' reports. Customs is a genuine dice roll that can eat a whole trip, uninsured. A live intel bazaar lets players sell "what's hot in Region B" lists that are sometimes accurate, sometimes last month's news, sometimes bait.

### 136. Dawn at the Mandi
Pitch: A ten-minute real-time session game: the wholesale produce market opens at 4am, fifty players surge in, and prices exist only as open-outcry bids while today's actual supply is unknown until the trucks are unloaded. Hesitate and the good crates are gone; overcommit and you're stuck hawking wilting greens all day at a loss. Bulk lots force on-the-spot split alliances with strangers who may stiff you on their share.

### 137. No Stars for the Cart
Pitch: You run a food cart with a reputation score you are never allowed to see — you only observe its effects: queue length, whether regulars return, whether suppliers offer credit. The structural twist is that other players CAN see your score, and selling reputation reports to blind vendors is itself a viable in-game business. You're forced to buy a mirror from people with every incentive to distort it.

### 138. The Ledger of Aunt Rosa
Pitch: You inherit a dead relative's market stall as nothing but a cryptic handwritten ledger — nicknames, shorthand debts, suppliers marked with symbols nobody alive can explain. Gameplay is archaeology-by-experiment: you test entries against the live player economy, discovering which contacts are real, which debts are owed to you, and which entries were coded warnings. Some of "Aunt Rosa's" counterparties are real players running their own inherited ledgers, holding the other half of your mysteries.

### 139. Matatu Kings
Pitch: You run informal minibus routes in a city with no schedules and no licenses — route "ownership" is a fiction enforced by player cartels, and your gameplay is positioning vehicles, poaching passengers, and reading which turf wars you can survive. Revenue depends on unknowable daily rider flows, and the cartel politics happen in channels you have to earn your way into. Loud, fast, and one bad alliance from losing your fleet.

### 140. Scrap Cartography
Pitch: A walking game where valuable scrap and e-waste spawn along city routes, and your only capital is route knowledge that decays as the city changes under you. The novel mechanic: players sell hand-annotated maps to newcomers — and every map is either an honest inheritance, a half-truth, or a deliberate decoy leading buyers away from the seller's best routes. There is no way to verify a map except to walk it.

### 141. Two Currencies
Pitch: A border-town trading game where every price the UI displays is the "official" rate — and the official rate is a lie. The real black-market exchange rate exists only in player conversations, coded phrases, and back-room deals, so the game's entire interface is systematically wrong and knowing HOW wrong is the skill. Newcomers get fleeced by the screen itself until another player takes pity or takes advantage.

### 142. Grandmother's Recipe Is Wrong
Pitch: You inherit a beloved street food recipe that's slowly losing customers, and you may change exactly one variable per day — but customers never explain anything; they just stop coming, or quietly come back. Attribution is agony: was it the chili, the price, the new spot, or the rival stall that opened Tuesday? Taste trends drift based on what real players across the city are actually eating, so the target moves while you're aiming.

### 143. The Queue
Pitch: You are a professional line-stander in a bureaucracy where permits, subsidized goods, and license windows are scarce and the rules for which queue actually leads anywhere are undocumented folklore. You speculate on queue positions — standing, holding, and selling your place to other players — while offices close early, requirements change unannounced, and window 4 turns out to have never processed anything. The market for positions is entirely player-to-player, complete with panics and bubbles.

### 144. Genny Row
Pitch: You own a wheezing secondhand generator in a market district where grid power dies without schedule or explanation, and you sell extension-cord subscriptions to surrounding stalls run by real players. Your true capacity is finite and invisible to customers — oversell it and one blown fuse kills every freezer on your line at once, torching trust you can't buy back. Illegal taps siphon your load undetectably except as creeping fuel costs, diesel arrives through player suppliers at rumor-driven prices, and when the grid inexplicably works for a whole week, your entire business quietly stops existing.

### 145. Festival Chasers
Pitch: You run a nomadic vendor caravan choosing which fairs, festivals, and match days to chase each week on limited fuel, where attendance forecasts are secondhand rumors and a "guaranteed" event can be rained out or cancelled with your pitch fee unrefunded. ### 146. The Apprentice Who Sweeps
Pitch: A bazaar apprenticeship game with a structural rule: there is no tutorial, no tooltip, and no mentor dialogue that explains anything — you sweep floors and carry tea, and your only teacher is watching the actual live transactions of master traders (including real high-level players) without commentary. ### 147. Rent Is Due Friday

Other players are your lenders of last resort, at whatever rates desperation lets them set.

### 148. Counterfeit Alley
Pitch: A social-deduction bazaar where goods are genuine or fake, only the seller knows which, and both honest dealing and counterfeiting are fully supported career paths. Buyers get no authenticity indicator — only wear-over-time reveals, word of mouth, and grudges — so trust is built and torched across real player relationships. ### 149. The Barker's Cut
Pitch: You sell foot traffic itself — hand-painted signs, shouted pitches at the market gate, tourists steered by the elbow — to real player stallholders, and neither you nor your clients can ever prove whether any of it worked. Your only defense is theater — conspicuous effort, staged crowds, testimonial regulars you may or may not be paying — because in a business with unmeasurable output, looking effective and being effective pay exactly the same.

### 150. Umbrella Men
Pitch: Five-minute mobile rounds of micro-speculation: buy umbrellas cheap in sunshine, sell dear in rain — except the weather is genuinely stochastic and the forecasts are a paid product sold by other players whose track records are self-reported. Fortunes turn on whether you bought the right liar's forecast. Top forecast-sellers become server celebrities right up until the storm they missed.
