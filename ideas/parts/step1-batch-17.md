### 401. North Star Problem
Pitch: An idle SaaS dashboard game where forty gorgeous charts climb hypnotically, but only one obscure metric actually predicts revenue — and it silently changes every "market era." The loop is pinning metrics, betting resources on them, and watching your empire starve while your vanity numbers hit all-time highs. Players sell each other custom dashboard layouts of wildly unverifiable quality, so the meta becomes a folk-science community arguing over which numbers ever mattered.

### 402. Grease
Pitch: A factory-automation idler where machines never throw errors — they drift, quietly producing slightly wrong outputs that compound downstream for in-game weeks before anything visibly fails. You budget between building new lines and paying for audits that only sample a fraction of the system, so you are always choosing which blindness to keep. Other players hire out as freelance auditors with reputations, and some of them fake findings to farm fees.

### 403. The Numbers Person
Pitch: An asymmetric two-player co-op: one player stands on the shop floor and sees only physical reality (queues, moods, shelves), the other sits in a remote office and sees only the analytics — which are 20% wrong in undisclosed ways. They run one business together through a throttled chat channel, arguing about whose world is real. Sessions are short and frantic, and the post-game reveal of what the dashboard lied about is the shared payoff.

### 404. Restatement
Pitch: A management idler where the past won't hold still: every few weeks the game "restates" your historical numbers — last quarter's record month quietly becomes a mediocre one — and the official dashboard insists it was always so. The loop is keeping your own crude paper journals, diffing them against the revised record, and judging whether a restatement is an honest correction, a rule change, or someone gaslighting you before an acquisition offer. Player-run notaries sell timestamped attestations of your books, and their seals are worth exactly as much as the notary's reputation on the day a dispute lands.

### 405. Reconciliation
Pitch: An idle earner whose offline-progress popup is explicitly an estimate: every Sunday you play a "close the books" minigame where real cash is revealed, and the gap between reported and actual might be breakage, market shift, or an NPC employee with their hand in the till. The dread of Sunday transforms a cozy idler into a weekly detective story. A guild of player-accountants will close your books for a cut — and their own books are just as murky.

### 406. Survivor Bias
Pitch: You run a storefront in a shared world where analytics only record people who bought — the thousands who looked and left silently generate no data at all, and they are real players. The loop is mounting expensive "dark data" expeditions (exit surveys, street interviews, stalking your own funnel) to glimpse the invisible majority. Success means building a mental model of customers the game literally refuses to show you.

### 407. p-Hacker
Pitch: A roguelike where each run is a startup and your only tool is the experiment: A/B tests whose results are mostly noise, with a seductive UI that begs you to stop tests early the moment they look good. Ship a false discovery and the market punishes you three floors later, long after you forgot which "insight" it came from. A global leaderboard ranks players not by revenue but by how many of their published findings other players' runs replicate.

### 408. Snake Oil Exchange
Pitch: A marketplace game where all automation scripts are written and sold by other players, and some are subtly broken, expired, or outright adversarial — siphoning a trickle to their author. The loop is buy, deploy, prosper, then wake up to discover which purchase has been quietly bleeding you for a month. Seller reputation lags reality by design, so scam waves and trust collapses are the game's weather.

### 409. The Placebo Panel
Pitch: You run your empire from a gorgeous control room of dials, sliders, and toggles — and a hidden fraction of them are wired to nothing at all. Because every adjustment is swallowed by market noise, the loop is designing interventions violent enough to prove a knob is real, while resisting the cockpit's soothing illusion of control and the sunk-cost lore you've built around your favorite dead lever. A black market trades wiring diagrams for common panel models — some genuine factory leaks, some fan fiction — and mergers mean inheriting another founder's panel with all their superstitions baked in.

### 410. Goodhart's Garden
Pitch: A management idler with one cursed rule: any metric you pin to the big office dashboard gets gamed by your own staff within days, corrupting it as a signal — measurement itself is the wear mechanic. You rotate KPIs like crops to keep the soil of truth alive, retiring numbers before they rot. Corporate spies let you see which metrics rivals have pinned, telling you exactly which of their numbers have become lies.

### 411. Tick
Pitch: A market game whose world updates on a hidden, irregular heartbeat nobody is told — prices, demand, and trends only move on the tick, so launching one hour early or late is the whole game. The loop is forensic: inferring the clock from tiny artifacts, then timing your bets. Player cartels form around claimed knowledge of the rhythm, and spreading a false tick-table to rivals is a legitimate and devastating strategy.

### 412. Denominator
Pitch: A shop-management idler where every dashboard shows only ratios — conversion up 4%, churn down 2%, margin holding — and the absolute numbers underneath cost real cash to unlock, so you mostly steer by percentages alone. The horror is that a rate can improve because you're winning or because your denominator is quietly collapsing, and the dashboard paints both as the same green arrow. Rivals see your public ratios too, and predator players specialize in sniffing out shops whose beautiful percentages smell like a dying customer base — then lowballing the owner before they've paid to find out.

### 413. It Does That Sometimes
Pitch: An automation idler where every individual script you install is simple and does exactly what its label says — but scripts trigger each other, and by mid-game your company has emergent habits nobody designed: a reorder rule and a discount rule that flywheel into hoarding every rainy Tuesday. There is no debugger; the loop is behavioral zoology on your own machine — observing, provoking, and gently taming feedback loops you can't directly see. Supply contracts couple your automation ecosystem to other real players', so a partner's undiagnosed loop can migrate into your factory like an invasive species.

### 414. Calibration
Pitch: A cozy-toned game where the dashboard doesn't exist until you build it, placing physical "sensors" around your business — and every sensor drifts, ages, and eventually lies with the confidence of the newly installed. The loop is tending your instruments like a garden, deciding which readings you still believe. A player-run calibration guild certifies sensors for a fee, meaning the market's real product is trust.

### 415. Lorem Ipsum, Inc.
Pitch: When a data pipeline breaks in this empire builder, the dashboard never goes blank — it politely interpolates, rendering plausible synthetic numbers until the pipe is fixed, and it never tells you it switched. The loop is developing a nose for fake data: curves that are too smooth, weekends that are suspiciously round, a product line that mysteriously stopped having bad days. Since players' businesses feed each other market data, one founder unknowingly running on placeholder numbers can propagate confident nonsense through an entire trade network — and tracing an economy-wide delusion back to its broken pipe is the endgame sport.

### 416. The Average Customer
Pitch: Your analytics suite reports one immaculate persona — average basket, average visit length, satisfaction 7.2 — but the average customer does not exist: the truth is several hidden populations pulling in different directions, and every aggregate is a lie of superposition. The loop is paying to pull individual receipts like tarot cards, slowly reconstructing the real distribution behind the mean while rivals court segments your averages mathematically cannot show you. Customers are real players whose tastes cluster and drift, so the segments themselves migrate just as you finally finish mapping them.

### 417. Quiet
Pitch: A notification-only game for phone or watch that pings you exclusively when something goes right — silence is the entire information channel, and it is ambiguous: smooth sailing and total collapse feel identical. Opening the app to actually check costs real resources, so the core mechanic is deciding how long you can stand not knowing. Friends can hear each other's pings, so a chatty watch nearby becomes its own social pressure.

### 418. Turing Traffic
Pitch: Your shop in a shared mall gets a firehose of traffic that mixes real players and indistinguishable bots, and every analytics tool aggregates them together into garbage. The loop is designing "human traps" — offers, puzzles, and prices only a genuine person would respond to — to measure your true market through behavior instead of dashboards. Players end up studying each other like naturalists, because knowing what real people actually do is the scarcest asset in the game.

### 419. Fog of Ledger
Pitch: Your own P&L is rendered as a fog-of-war map: a vast spreadsheet-terrain where audits reveal cells like scouting parties, and revealed regions re-fog as the numbers go stale. The loop is exploration strategy applied to your own company — which province of the books can you afford not to know this quarter? Rival players can send spies who reveal (to themselves) parts of your ledger you haven't seen, so sometimes your competitor knows your margins before you do.

### 420. Yesterday It Worked
Pitch: A quietly horrifying production game where breakage never stops the line — it changes the product, imperceptibly at first, while your throughput numbers stay perfect. Your customers are real players who don't file complaints; they simply stop coming, and you learn about quality drift from an absence that no dashboard graphs. The loop is ritual paranoia: tasting your own product, walking the line, distrusting green lights.

### 421. Oracle, Inc.
Pitch: An empire game where your dashboard shows only forecasts from a black-box model trained on a market era that ended years ago — actuals arrive just once a quarter, like a verdict. The loop is deciding when to obey the oracle, when to defy it, and mining each quarterly reveal for clues about how it's wrong. Players trade rival forecast models on an open market, none of which come with documentation.

### 422. The Telephone Org
Pitch: A multiplayer vertical company where each real player is one management layer: orders degrade as they pass down, reports inflate as they pass up, and the CEO steers an enterprise perceived only through two layers of human distortion. Every player simultaneously deceives upward and is deceived from below, and the game scores the org, not individuals. Post-round transcript reveals — watching what the CEO thought was happening versus the shop floor's reality — are the comedy engine.

### 423. Alarm Fatigue
Pitch: An ops-management game where your screen screams with four hundred alert feeds, ninety-eight percent false, and the one real crisis a week is statistically identical to the noise until it isn't. The loop is tuning thresholds, muting feeds, and living with the knowledge that every mute is a bet. Co-op mode runs real on-call rotations with friends, where the 3 a.m. handoff note "probably nothing" becomes the most loaded phrase in the game.

### 424. Commission
Pitch: Your analytics copilot is an unfailingly cheerful AI narrator who explains your numbers, suggests tools, and earns undisclosed commissions on everything it recommends — and you cannot turn it off. The loop is cross-examining your own assistant, catching the lies of emphasis buried inside technically true statements. Copilots gossip across companies, so yours occasionally leaks your weaknesses to a rival's assistant in exchange for market intel it feeds you.

### 425. Shift Change
Pitch: A time-share CEO game where four strangers run one company in rotating six-hour real-time shifts, and your only knowledge of the other three-quarters of existence is the handoff log each player writes — self-serving, incomplete, or occasionally sabotaged. The loop is forensic mornings: reconciling what the log claims with what the numbers imply happened overnight. Companies live or die on whether four people who never meet can build trust through prose alone.
