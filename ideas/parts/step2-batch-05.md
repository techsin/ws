### 101. Ravenpost & Rue — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Cold outreach where silence is the default outcome and you iterate on pitch, targeting, and cadence without ever receiving attributable feedback — the emotional and strategic core of early sales.
Cheats: With replies this rare, players cannot distinguish a better letter from a luckier week, so the celebrated "folklore about what works" is unfalsifiable superstition the game risks rewarding as if it were learning.
Note: Epistemic only insofar as real guild-players behave with readable texture; the true degeneration risk is boredom-quit before the lesson lands, not wikis — you cannot wiki other people's inboxes.

### 102. Offal & Ichor — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Buying sight-unseen in a market with a private language teaches information asymmetry and paying real tuition for domain literacy.
Cheats: The glossary is pure wiki-bait — the day "wyrm-wet" is documented on Discord, the entire literacy edge evaporates and this becomes bid-sniping with a cheat sheet.
Note: Survives only if the slang is genuinely player-generated and drifting; rejections-without-reason from alchemists are good, but static slang makes this a solvable puzzle, not a market.

### 103. The Last Lantern — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Irreversible location bets against a demand curve made of other people — commitment under uncertainty with the pivot option deliberately amputated.
Cheats: One bet per season followed by months of spectating is gambling with an inn skin; the player makes almost no decisions between commitment and outcome, so nothing is practiced except rumor-reading.
Note: Reflexivity saves it from wikis (forecasting the meta moves the meta), but the loop cadence is far too slow to teach through iteration — this teaches regret, not judgment.

### 104. Underwriter of the Deep — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Pricing risk on gut with sample sizes too small to ever exonerate your model — the authentic founder experience of never knowing whether you were wrong or just unlucky.
Cheats: Community death-stat spreadsheets will reconstruct the actuarial tables the game withholds, quietly converting gut-pricing into table-lookup for everyone in the Discord.
Note: The adverse-selection layer (applicants privately know their own recklessness) is genuinely epistemic and player-driven, so the lemons dynamic outlives the wiki even after the base rates leak.

### 105. The Identifier's Dilemma — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Reputation as inventory, and the impossibility of cleanly attributing a bad review to your error versus their malice — a real trust-calibration problem.
Cheats: The appraisal core dies the moment an item database exists, and sanctioned review-griefing ("lie to tank you and buy your shop") teaches paranoia about players, not judgment about customers.
Note: Half the uncertainty is wiki-mortal (fixed item stats) and half is pure adversarial noise; the learnable middle band is uncomfortably thin.

### 106. Guildcharter — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Losing sealed bids with zero feedback while slowly mapping an institution's unwritten preferences — then taking the seat and discovering the system was never coherent, the truest gatekeeper lesson in the batch.
Cheats: It teaches regulatory and political navigation more than building anything — you can master this game without ever touching a customer, a product, or a unit economic.
Note: Rotating real players through council seats keeps the opacity permanently shifting and un-wikiable; the epistemic loop is structural even if the entrepreneurship is adjacent.

### 107. Sevenday — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Hypothesis velocity — the winner is whoever runs the most cheap, disconfirming experiments per week, which is the actual engine of validation.
Cheats: Weekly wipes amputate runway fear and ruin, teaching the scientist half of founding while anesthetizing the half that makes founders sweat — failure costing "only days" is exactly what failure never costs.
Note: Demand regenerated from last week's aggregate player behavior is the rare structurally wiki-proof design — the system under study is made of people and moves when you touch it.

### 108. Mimic, Incorporated — TEACH: 3/10
Teaches: Selling into a category presumed fraudulent, where trust must be purchased by absorbing abuse — a real cold-start problem for pariah industries.
Cheats: Mechanically it is an instinct-meter self-control minigame with a reputation bar stapled on — the "rejection" is adventurers poking you, flavor animation on a stat check rather than a strategic force.
Note: One-slip-resets-everything is punishment theater, not learning; there is nothing epistemic to investigate, just a willpower gauge to grind — a cartoon of stigma, cute for a week.

### 109. Gone Below — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Credit policy under silence — a non-returning counterparty could be dead, delayed, or defrauding you, and you must set deposits, write-offs, and collections with real money walking into the dark.
Cheats: Deposits and blacklists have a comfortable equilibrium the community will converge on, sanding the terror down into actuarial routine faster than the pitch admits.
Note: Degeneration here recapitulates financial history (shared blacklists become a credit bureau), which is the lesson continuing rather than dying; uncertainty stays epistemic because counterparties are people.

### 110. Contract of the Brass Djinn — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Your own language is your risk surface — deals fail at the clause you did not know was ambiguous, and you learn drafting by bleeding.
Cheats: Community boilerplate solves it within a month, and free-text "literal" execution is either an exploitable LLM or a hidden parser — in which case losses feel like parser-lottery RNG, not epistemic failure.
Note: The arms race of players attacking standard templates could keep it alive, but "never told which clause failed" crosses from opacity into unlearnability unless adjudication is rigidly consistent.

### 111. Bard & Banner — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: The agency owner's curse — attribution is unknowable, clients churn silently, and you sell a portfolio of unprovable wins on belief.
Cheats: If causality is genuinely unrecoverable even in aggregate, the game contains no signal, and "skill" collapses into pure social salesmanship — which the design then rewards as though it were competence.
Note: Dangerously close to pure noise; it needs a faint, expensive-to-detect true effect or it teaches fatalism plus BS artistry — an honest lesson about marketing, perhaps, but a corrosive one.

### 112. Waystone & Toll — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Pricing in the dark where the only market signal is your own traffic silently evaporating — demand discovery via loss.
Cheats: The decision surface is a toll slider and a prayer; between rare price moves it is an idle spreadsheet, so the sunk-capital agony is felt but no judgment is actually practiced.
Note: Out-of-game price-sharing turns it into a cartel sim (realistic, but it deletes the intended blindness); epistemic in principle, tedious in execution.

### 113. Hoardkeepers — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Serving whale clients with no mandate, capricious reversals, and catastrophic key-account concentration — reading one rich human is the entire business.
Cheats: The whole design leans on the server's richest veterans role-playing engaged, mercurial LPs for your benefit; when they get bored, the game's uncertainty engine simply logs off.
Note: Public office-scorching is memorable, load-bearing rejection, but this is closer to a job with a terrifying boss than to founding anything.

### 114. Spawnwrights — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Shipping into confounded, season-delayed feedback where you can never isolate which decision mattered — the ecosystem-play and brand-building experience.
Cheats: "Hopelessly confounded" is the design confessing the player cannot learn; a season-long loop with unattributable outcomes is noise with agricultural flavor.
Note: Species-interaction tables are wiki-mortal; the shifting adventurer meta is the only durable uncertainty, and it is precisely the part the player can least act on.

### 115. Necropolis Staffing — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Operating in a regulatory gray zone where rules are unpublished, shifting, and discovered by watching who gets raided — genuine fintech/cannabis founder texture.
Cheats: Enforcement-by-surprise-raid is punishment without a hypothesis loop unless edicts follow a consistent internal logic, and the pitch never commits to one — arbitrary inquisitors are just a random tax.
Note: Compliance gossip as a tradeable-but-unreliable good is a nice trust-calibration market; the skeleton-labor layer is filler drifting toward attribute management.

### 116. Caravanserai — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Buying protection you cannot test until it fails — credence goods, counterparty risk, and trust networks built with the people best positioned to rob you.
Cheats: Route knowledge consolidates fast into faction-aligned information brokers, after which most players follow someone's map instead of reading survivor silence themselves.
Note: "The mercenaries may be the bandits" keeps uncertainty epistemic and permanently shifting because the threat model is other players' incentives, not a spawn table.

### 117. The Cursed Goods Exchange — TEACH: 4/10
Teaches: Portfolio thinking over tail-risk liabilities — some inventory is a claim against you, not an asset.
Cheats: Severity undetectable before purchase plus warehouse misfortune you "can't measure" makes the core buy decision a scratch-off ticket; there is no belief to form and test, only variance to endure.
Note: The single epistemic layer is evaluating curse-breaker players' skill — a decent vendor-selection subgame trapped inside a slot machine.

### 118. Familiar & Co. — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Churn without exit interviews — the product walks home wordless and you must infer the failure from portfolio patterns, which is exactly what silent SaaS cancellation feels like.
Cheats: If matching runs on a hidden compatibility matrix, the wiki solves it and the window of returned familiars empties forever — "infer from nothing" only teaches if there is actually something.
Note: The public shame display (returns visible in your shopfront) is a genuinely load-bearing reputation mechanic; epistemic depth depends entirely on clients being humanly inconsistent.

### 119. Chokepoint Concessions — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Paying for placement against demand you cannot inspect — and eating the fee when a flawless party walks past without a word, silence in its purest retail form.
Cheats: Player agency ends at the bid; afterward you are a spectator to someone else's competence, so it trains bankroll management for a betting game more than any operating judgment.
Note: Parties auctioning their choke rights is a lovely emergent two-sided market, but reputation data on parties will make bidding actuarial within weeks.

### 120. The Prophecy Pit — TEACH: 4/10
Teaches: Keynesian beauty-contest reading — you profit by predicting the crowd's interpretation rather than the event, a real lesson about narrative and timing.
Cheats: It is a speculation game wearing a business hat — no customers, no product, no payroll, no unit economics; mastery produces trading-floor instincts, not founding judgment.
Note: Double-interpretive settlement (jury atop oracle) is epistemic and human-shifting, so it will not wiki-collapse — it simply is not entrepreneurship.

### 121. Both Banners — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Political exposure as a ledger you cannot read — pricing neutrality by inferring invisible tolerances from silences, "lost" shipments, and retroactive punishment.
Cheats: If faction players are capricious rather than consistent, crossed lines become uninferable and the game slides from opacity into arbitrary ruin — the design bets everything on humans being readably self-interested.
Note: The quartermaster-stops-answering mechanic is silence doing real strategic work; wiki-proof because the ledger is other players' grudges.

### 122. The Quest Board — TEACH: 9/10
Teaches: The marketplace founder's structural tragedy — you float both sides' dishonesty on your own cash while your best matches disintermediate you forever, so success at matching is failure at retention.
Cheats: Very little — the harshest note is that veteran board-owners will converge on standard vetting heuristics and fee structures, though lying counterparties and leakage pressure are people and never stop.
Note: The rare case where the lesson (platform leakage, trust arbitrage, commission cash-flow) is the win condition itself and cannot be patched out by any wiki.

### 123. Patch Eve Outfitters — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Committing inventory ahead of a regime change on a diet of leaks, lies, and cope — source calibration and forecast humility under platform risk.
Cheats: The patch is dev-authored dice; if rebalances are deliberately anti-inductive, the tea-leaf reading is a lottery with extra homework, and the game quietly rewards paying the right leak-Discord over judgment.
Note: Leak markets centralizing is realistic (expert networks exist) but concentrates the lesson in a handful of insiders while everyone else just gets whipsawed.

### 124. The Effluvium Cartel — TEACH: 4/10
Teaches: In young industries, safety knowledge is a competitive moat hoarded one drop above the legal minimum.
Cheats: Fixed detonation chemistry is the most wiki-mortal core in the batch — one shared spreadsheet of combinations kills the entire experimental-learning premise, leaving a hauling logistics sim.
Note: Public city-block-cratering failure is a great reputation stake, but the epistemic uncertainty has a hard expiry date measured in weeks.

### 125. The Silent Partner — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Trust calibration from the money's side — funding on spin-heavy letters and pattern-matched heartbreak, learning why silence at the wrong moment reprices everything.
Cheats: The playable surface is thin (read letter, wire or don't), and with no books and no recourse the rational meta is treating every founder as a fraud — which teaches cynicism faster than judgment.
Note: Uncertainty is fully epistemic and human (real players spinning you), so it cannot be wikied — but the game must give investors something to do between heartbreaks or it is an inbox simulator.
