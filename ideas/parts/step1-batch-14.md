### 326. Best and Final
Pitch: A negotiation game built entirely on unverifiable leverage: every claim of "I have another offer" may be true, invented, or expired an hour ago, and the only way to test it is to walk away and see whether the other side lets you go. Since real players juggle several slow-burning deals at once, even the bluffer often doesn't know if their alternative still exists when they invoke it. The game teaches that your negotiating position is literally your alternatives — by making you lose deals you tried to win with imaginary ones.

### 327. The Gentlemen's Cartel
Pitch: A darkly comic commodity game where honest competition grinds everyone to zero margin, and the only path to profit is a price-fixing handshake struck in a private lounge with rival players — with no contracts and no enforcement. When the market price suddenly craters, the game never tells you who broke the fix; you deduce the defector from shipment patterns and body language in chat, then decide whether to punish, forgive, or quietly defect yourself. Cartels rise, rot, and reform every season.

### 328. Blacklist
Pitch: A noir MMO-lite where you never see your own reputation: after every deal, counterparties rate you in secret, and those whispers silently determine which markets, rooms, and clients even render on your screen. You don't get banned — doors just quietly stop opening, and you must infer what you did wrong from the shape of the silence. Rehabilitation means finding one player willing to deal with a ghost and slowly rebuilding through them.

### 329. Zombie Fund
Pitch: A bluffing game where founders pitch five investor players for a round while burning real runway each meeting — but some funds are secretly out of money and must keep taking meetings to look alive to their own LPs. Founders play social deduction on who can actually wire; investors play poker with their own insolvency. The cruelest losses come from spending your last three weeks courting a corpse.

### 330. Locked In
Pitch: You win the big contract only by retooling your whole operation around one buyer — custom specs, dedicated capacity, their logo painted on your loading dock — and the moment that investment is sunk, they know it, and renegotiation season begins. The social-deduction layer is reading which partner players will honor the spirit of the original handshake and which are patiently waiting until you're too committed to walk before demanding new terms. Veterans learn to price the holdup into the deal, keep a second buyer secretly warm, or refuse to sign unless the buyer sinks something irreversible too.

### 331. Vesting
Pitch: A tense 2-4 player couch/tabletop-style game about co-founding: each round, players secretly allocate their hours between the shared company and private side hustles, with equity vesting on cliffs and anyone able to trigger a buyout fight. Nobody can prove who's coasting — you deduce commitment from output and confront or bluff accordingly. Sessions end in triumphant IPOs, acrimonious splits, or one founder quietly owning everything.

### 332. The Unexplained No
Pitch: A sales game where buyer players are procedurally assigned hidden constraints — budget freezes, internal politics, a boss who hates your category — and are mechanically forbidden from explaining themselves; they may only say yes or no. Sellers must reverse-engineer the invisible objection landscape across dozens of attempts, building folk theories that are sometimes wrong. Buyers, meanwhile, are playing their own game of surviving their constraints.

### 333. Word of Mouth
Pitch: A structurally weird trading world with no ledger at all: deals exist only in what the two players remember and choose to honor, and there is no game record to appeal to. Disputes go before a jury of uninvolved players who never saw the agreement either, ruling purely on credibility, reputation, and storytelling. The game quietly teaches why contracts, witnesses, and paper trails were invented — by making you live without them.

### 334. Floor Pass
Pitch: A 15-minute real-time proximity-voice game set on a trade show floor: work the booths, qualify leads fast, and close before the doors shut — except some eager "buyers" are competitor plants harvesting your pricing and roadmap. You must sell openly enough to win deals while deducing who's wearing a fake badge. Post-round reveals show exactly which secrets you gave away and to whom.

### 335. Assay
Pitch: You build a certification mark — a seal other players pay to stamp on their goods — and its value rests on diligence nobody can observe: you privately choose how hard to actually inspect, and cutting corners looks identical to rigor for a suspiciously long time. One scandal poisons every product currently carrying your mark, so honest sellers vet certifiers the way investors vet founders, from tea-leaf signals and old gossip. The game's thesis is that trust can be manufactured, licensed, and scaled like any product — and destroyed faster than any of them.

### 336. Counterparty
Pitch: A market where the tradeable asset is other people's integrity: buy and sell "trust bonds" that pay out only if a named third player honors their deals this season. Before partnering with anyone, you check — and move — the market's live price on their trustworthiness, and whales can manipulate a rival's trust price to strangle their deal flow. Being reliably boring becomes a lucrative personal brand.

### 337. Earnout
Pitch: Sell your company to another player and half the price arrives only as an earnout tied to future performance — performance the buyer now controls. Sellers must judge, before signing, whether this acquirer will feed the business or quietly starve it just below every milestone; buyers weigh the profit in sandbagging one earnout against a market that remembers forever. Diligence happens over dinners, reference whispers, and gut reads, because the contract's numbers matter less than the person now holding the levers behind them.

### 338. First in Line
Pitch: Supply is short and every supplier is a real player who has promised "priority allocation" to more customers than they can possibly serve — courtship, favors, and loyalty theater determine your true rank in the queue, which you only discover the day the shortage actually bites. The deduction runs both ways: buyers probe for proof they're really first, while suppliers oversell their queue because commitments cost nothing until the crunch makes them all due at once. It teaches the unlisted truth that in a squeeze, allocation is a relationship, not a price.

### 339. The Mole in the Pipeline
Pitch: A B2B sales campaign where one account in your pipeline is secretly a competitor's plant, feeding your roadmap and pricing back to them — but churn signals are noisy and honest customers act suspicious all the time. Cut the wrong account and you've burned real revenue plus your reputation for paranoia; cut nobody and you bleed secrets. Competitors are real players choosing where to place their moles.

### 340. The Licensing Office
Pitch: A structurally strange game where the regulator gating your industry is an anonymous rotating player with private, unwritten approval criteria they invent within loose bounds. Applicants deduce the rules from the pattern of who gets approved, trade half-true folklore about "what the office likes," and risk lobbying attempts that can backfire spectacularly. When the regulator rotates, all your hard-won institutional knowledge silently expires.

### 341. Off the Record
Pitch: A Discord-native M&A game where acquisition talks happen in chat rooms that self-delete every 24 hours, and only terms both sides explicitly re-affirm survive into the final closing document. Misremembering what was agreed — or strategically "misremembering" — is a legal move, so negotiators develop rituals of repetition, summaries, and witnesses. Deals collapse over what one side swears was promised on a message that no longer exists.

### 342. Boardroom Knives
Pitch: A social-deduction governance game played across a cap table: investors and executives secretly pool votes and proxies to oust the founder, while the founder reads calendar patterns, sudden friendliness, and meeting exclusions to sniff out the coup before the board convenes. Every ally is one term sheet away from flipping. Sessions climax in a single live board vote where the whip counts meet reality.

### 343. Power of Attorney
Pitch: The rooms where your deals close are rooms you can't enter, so you hire another player to negotiate on your behalf — and you see only the outcome, never the conversation. Agents build books of rival clients while fielding constant quiet offers to tilt a deal for the other side, and principals must infer loyalty from a string of results that bad luck and betrayal explain equally well. The endgame skill is structuring incentives so your agent only wins when you do — and discovering firsthand why that alignment is nearly impossible to write down.

### 344. Reference Call
Pitch: Before hiring or partnering with another player, you get up to three live reference calls — but the references are real players with their own grudges, debts, and side deals, possibly coached by the candidate an hour earlier. The core skill is asking questions that make polite people accidentally tell the truth. Everyone eventually plays all three roles: chooser, candidate, and the reference deciding what they owe to whom.

### 345. The Long Silence
Pitch: A deliberately slow daily game, Wordle-paced but months long: you send exactly one cold pitch per day into a global pool of real players, and "seen" receipts drift back on delays of days to weeks, with replies rarer still. The metagame is iterating your pitch across weeks of near-total quiet and learning to read microscopic signals without spiraling. Its weird structural bet is that the silence itself — genuinely enduring it — is the content.

### 346. Lemon Syndicate
Pitch: An angel-investing game where lead players present deals and backers commit capital on the lead's word — but some deals are procedurally rotten, and the lead may or may not know which. Backers play adverse-selection detective on both the deal and the lead's honesty, while leads compound a track record that becomes their real product. One knowingly-passed lemon can end a five-season reputation overnight.

### 347. Texas Shootout
Pitch: Every partnership in this game is signed with a shotgun clause: at any moment your partner can name a single price for the business, and you must immediately either sell your half at that number or buy theirs at the same one. Naming a price when you don't know which side of it you'll land on is the purest valuation test ever devised — and it turns cash reserves, bluffed poverty, and reading your partner's liquidity into weapons. Players enter every venture already negotiating, silently, over how it might end.

### 348. Shelf Space
Pitch: Your product lives or dies on distribution, and distribution is owned by gatekeeper players who take "listing fees" and favors — then can pocket the fee and stock your rival anyway, with zero recourse beyond reputation warfare. Makers learn to structure staged payments, hostages, and alliances that make betrayal unprofitable rather than impossible. Gatekeepers learn how much extraction a market tolerates before makers organize against them.

### 349. The Consortium
Pitch: The richest contracts are too big for any single player, so bitter rivals must form temporary consortiums — splitting the fee up front, delivering the work afterward, with each member's true effort hidden inside the joint result. When the project underdelivers, was it sabotage, shirking, or honest failure? You litigate that question in the court of gossip while yesterday's co-bidders become tomorrow's competitors again. A live lesson in moral hazard: every partnership where credit is shared but effort is private.

### 350. Port of No Return
Pitch: A cross-server trade game engineered so most exchanges are one-shot deals with strangers you will statistically never meet again — no escrow, no arbitration, and reputation passports that can be forged for the right price. The design question it forces on players is when cooperation can survive without repeated play, and the emergent answer is player-built institutions: guilds, bonded couriers, and paper trails. Scamming is legal; being unable to trade with anyone afterward is the sentence.
