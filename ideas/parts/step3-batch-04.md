# Step 3 — Critique Pass 2 (FUN) — Batch 04 (Ideas 76–100)

### 76. Redshift Freight Exchange — FUN: 6/10
Hook: The mid-burn manifest decrypt — "I just found out I'm hauling a diplomat's corpse" — is a clip-and-retell moment, and the fabricated-intel market keeps rumor mills churning.
Poison: Between reveals the verb is filling out bid forms and staring at unanswered ones; three silent bids in a row is a whole session of nothing happening to you.
Fix: Guarantee a low-margin spot-cargo haul every session so lost bids sting your ego and ledger but never leave you with an empty evening — silence stays the lesson, not the gameplay.

### 77. Tongueless Bazaar — FUN: 7/10
Hook: Charades with aliens is inherently funny, the hypothesis-gesture-observe loop is real detective play, and "I did the wrong hand-flick and the stall folded forever" is prime war-story material.
Poison: Permanent stall-folds punish the playful players hardest — by hour 40 the curious have salted their own map, and minute 1 offers no toehold, just bafflement.
Fix: Make folds reopenable via a costly, itself-untranslated apology ritual, so each failure spawns a new puzzle arc instead of deleting content from your world.

### 78. Franchise of the Overmind — FUN: 4/10
Hook: The community's back-channel Talmud on citation code 7-KHA-99 is genuinely delicious meta-content, and public compliance rankings breed schadenfreude.
Poison: The actual moment-to-moment is running a fast-food outlet while dreading paperwork — janitorial anxiety simulation, work stress cosplaying as a game, misery with no catharsis.
Fix: Make inspections live theatrical encounters you can improv your way through — talking to the inspector becomes performance comedy, and dread becomes a scene instead of a grade.

### 79. Greenfall Contractors — FUN: 5/10
Hook: Watching your seeded ecology visibly bloom is Spore-grade satisfaction, and "whose algae actually took" blame-wars with rivals generate real gossip.
Poison: Getting paid only for what a satellite happens to witness means your best work routinely goes unrewarded off-camera — the most enraging feeling in games, success that didn't count.
Fix: Publish the satellite's orbital schedule (where it looks, never how it grades) so players stage showpiece blooms for the pass — the wait becomes a heist-timing game.

### 80. Long Silence Trading Co. — FUN: 6/10
Hook: The phone buzzing after nine real days of silence from a colony you'd written off is a genuine spike of joy — delicious waiting, done right, on the right platform.
Poison: A mobile game whose default notification is no notification gets deleted in week one; "most pitches get nothing back, ever" means the drumbeat of anticipation never establishes.
Fix: Add a 5-minute daily harbor ritual — packing pitches, trading unverifiable dockside rumors about your silent colonies — so every day has a verb and hope stays theatrically alive.

### 81. Deadweight & Daughters — FUN: 7/10
Hook: Racing rival crews to a fresh wreck and stripping it under pressure is a strong physical core verb, and provisional paydays make every purchase a gamble worth retelling.
Poison: A no-reasons court clawing back money you already spent, weeks later, reads as the devs mugging you — the loser gets pure misery with zero catharsis or counterplay.
Fix: Make verdict day a scheduled public spectacle — rulings read aloud in a packed hall with side-betting on outcomes — so even your clawback becomes server theater you were part of.

### 82. Stamp God — FUN: 8/10
Hook: Designing a cheap decoy shipment to trap the black box into revealing a rule is Zendo-as-a-trade-game — the cleared/rejected stamp thunk is a slot-pull with detective pride attached, and counterfeit theory-selling is deliciously nasty social play.
Poison: For anyone who doesn't love lab notebooks it's homework, and a regulation rotation that invalidates your hard-won theory can land as a progress wipe.
Fix: Juice the feedback loop — physical crates returning to your dock, stamp glyphs with personality, a visible theory-board that survives rotations as "historical scholarship" — so experiments feel like play, not data entry.

### 83. Lanefinder — FUN: 5/10
Hook: The day fresh (six-month-stale) traffic data drops and the entire map redraws — vindicating some cartels and bankrupting others at once — is a server-wide event people would plan around.
Poison: Between data drops the core loop is place-depot-then-wait-months, a spreadsheet with a starfield skin; there is almost no moment-to-moment verb at all.
Fix: Formalize the drop as "Census Day," a scheduled live holiday where all bets are graded together on a shared broadcast map — one great communal high per cycle beats none.

### 84. Cold Chain — FUN: 7/10
Hook: A decaying hold, a ticking loan, and a bank that will remotely vent your cargo on a missed payment makes every run FTL-tense, and convoys arriving together to crash their own market is superb emergent tragedy.
Poison: Arrival-only pricing plus debt means the unlucky enter unearned death spirals — hour 100 is either mastery or a misery treadmill of repossession.
Fix: Let you buy price rumors mid-transit from players coming the other way — unreliable by design — adding a bargaining verb en route and softening pure slot-machine arrivals.

### 85. Concourse — FUN: 7/10
Hook: Live-haggling with real humans in a cozy stall you decorated, building regulars and trading gossip, is the MMO farmers market a real audience genuinely wants — good deals feel earned because a person blinked.
Poison: Total dead-population dependence — an empty concourse is the saddest place in games, and off-peak hours are a ghost mall with no single-player floor.
Fix: Concentrate the game into scheduled market-day windows (two evenings plus weekends) so density is guaranteed and every session opens to a crowded, noisy concourse.

### 86. Snacks for the Vorth — FUN: 7/10
Hook: Inventing cursed alien snacks and discovering the Vorth reordered ten thousand units of gravel nougat is comedy gold — reorder notifications are pure dopamine and screenshots write themselves.
Poison: One bad batch permanently deleting an entire species from your customer base punishes exactly the experimental players who make the game funny — the jokers churn first.
Fix: Let shunning decay over real weeks and be shortcut by a groveling apology-feast event, converting your worst failure into a redemption arc instead of an account scar.

### 87. Void Where Prohibited — FUN: 5/10
Hook: Diagnosing a hidden defect from furious, garbled customer letters is a legitimate detective pleasure, and a well-timed recall gamble is one big dramatic decision worth sweating.
Poison: You start in the hangover — inbox triage and actuarial dread over fun your backstory-self had — all consequence, no agency-highs, an anxiety machine with mail.
Fix: Make it cyclical: play the boom too — sell the next product line, choose which corners to cut — so each act's sins author the next act's letters and players earn their own tail.

### 88. Static Prospectors — FUN: 3/10
Hook: Publicly staking your name on a signal in the player journal and being wrong — "it was a pulsar" as a career-defining humiliation — is a great story exactly once.
Poison: Days of real-time, unspeedable listening to literal noise is anti-game; the core verb is waiting for a graph to disappoint you, and most signals resolve to nothing by design.
Fix: Make listening active skill play — allocate attention across bands and filters sonar-style so what resolves depends on how well you listened, not how long you waited.

### 89. Maydays — FUN: 8/10
Hook: A live mayday on the open channel — your tug burning hard while the whole sector listens to find out if you arrive as savior or witness — is peak MMO drama, and every save or miss becomes sector legend.
Poison: Real-time coverage of player breakdowns means 3am pages and off-hours guilt — it demands the lifestyle of an on-call SRE, and public failure you positioned into last Tuesday can be harrowing without release.
Fix: Make coverage windows contractual — subscriptions specify the hours you're on shift — so on-call becomes opt-in session play while overcommitment economics stay fully intact.

### 90. Splice & License — FUN: 6/10
Hook: Creature-breeding is a proven joy-verb, and hearing your organism mutated into something wonderful or monstrous inside another player's colony is a story generator.
Poison: The endgame degenerates into anxiety management — defending lawsuits about ecosystems you can't see, where the best outcome is "nothing bad happened this week."
Fix: Make monitoring a real verb — deployable sensor-fauna and field agents whose footage doubles as tribunal evidence — so surveillance is detective play, not insurance paperwork.

### 91. Tolls of the Ancients — FUN: 6/10
Hook: Owning a chokepoint on live player traffic is a genuine power fantasy, and the lobbying-bribing-rerouting politics among toll barons is EVE-grade social sport.
Poison: Causeless franchise revocation vaporizing your entire position on a die roll is the purest rage-quit event a designer can build — losing the toy AND the reason.
Fix: Demote revocation to suspension with a costly, opaque appeals pilgrimage — you keep your asset, gain a desperate quest, and platform risk still costs you dearly.

### 92. The Glyph That Means No — FUN: 7/10
Hook: Performing a live ten-minute pitch to an alien choir and receiving one inscrutable glyph is theatrical, streamable, and the bitterly contested interpretation wiki is a permanent community bonfire.
Poison: If performance never visibly matters, players conclude their craft is irrelevant and the whole loop curdles into astrology fatigue by hour 20.
Fix: Let choirs react DURING the pitch — swaying, dimming, harmonizing — so your delivery has readable texture in the moment even while the final glyph stays forever untranslated.

### 93. Debris Futures — FUN: 7/10
Hook: The periodic, glorious derivatives collapse is a server-history event — "the Kessler Crash" gets retold for years, and selling your cleanup obligations onward to a sucker is delicious.
Poison: The median newcomer is exit liquidity — wiped out by a contagion they never had the literacy to price, which reads as unearned catastrophe, not lesson.
Fix: Pay cleanup a modest cash wage floor with futures as the upside kicker, so a collapse burns your bets and your pride but never your rent.

### 94. Crewminds — FUN: 8/10
Hook: The day your best robot crew buys out its contract, incorporates, and underbids you for your oldest client is an all-time gaming story — pride and betrayal in one notification.
Poison: Between betrayal beats the game is fleet-management dashboards over a black box you're forbidden to open — thin daily agency, thick quarterly drama.
Fix: Add daily crew-debrief rituals where you choose what to praise or suppress — a verb that plausibly (never provably) shapes loyalty, giving hands-on play between the big beats.

### 95. Pheromone Exchange — FUN: 6/10
Hook: Flying sampling runs through a shimmering chemical gradient is an actual kinetic verb with gorgeous potential, and rival wakes corrupting your reads adds dogfight texture to market research.
Poison: There is no one to meet — no faces, no gossip, no customer stories, just fluid dynamics with invoices — and a shipment silently absorbed without payment is pure anti-payoff.
Fix: Give absorption spectacle and residue — the hive visibly builds something from goods it kept — so even a total loss produces lore, clues, and a screenshot.

### 96. Vend the Void — FUN: 6/10
Hook: Hiring another player as your "eyes" on a distant colony, knowing they might be lying, is a tasty standing trust game, and mounting an expedition to one quiet machine is a real event.
Poison: The honest daily loop is refreshing a dashboard of stale telemetry — idle-game boredom without idle-game generosity.
Fix: Make expeditions playable co-op road trips with salvage, vignettes, and a reveal at the end, so resolving the fog is the fun part rather than the tax.

### 97. Protocol Zero — FUN: 7/10
Hook: The roguelike chassis is the right one — "I bowed; bowing means war" deaths are hilarious clips, and between-run knowledge inheritance gives failure a progression flavor.
Poison: Dying instantly to a taboo a rival guild burned yesterday, with no explanation ever, drifts from dark comedy toward arbitrary punishment the run structure can't fully absorb.
Fix: Ritualize every violation into an elaborate, collectible shunning ceremony logged in a personal codex of deaths — still zero explanation, but each failure becomes a trophy.

### 98. Surveyor's Gambit — FUN: 7/10
Hook: Running or falling for the long con is the most retold content in multiplayer history — EVE's best stories are exactly this — and watching players invent escrow and ratings from nothing is fascinating.
Poison: The fun is badly asymmetric: surveyors get the con, miners get spreadsheet purchases and delayed ruin, and a fraud-saturated month-3 market chews up every newcomer.
Fix: Give miners active due-diligence verbs — costly spot-audits, site visits, interrogation minigames — so paranoia becomes gameplay and the mark gets a fighting chance worth narrating.

### 99. Perihelion — FUN: 4/10
Hook: The once-in-a-decade night the sky actually delivers, with your name on every ticket, is arguably the single best screenshot in this entire batch.
Poison: The payoff cadence is twice a decade — nearly every player's whole career is refund-run ruin over coin flips, an emotional arc that is all downpayment and almost never dividend.
Fix: Add a weather-layer of small frequent celestial events (meteor showers, minor auroras) so operators run a living tour business between the legendary gambles.

### 100. Redshift Ledger — FUN: 7/10
Hook: The arrival moment — logging in to a future economy strangers built while you were gone, discovering your hold is now treasure or trash — is a sublime returning-player high, and diegetic logout makes absence guilt-free.
Poison: The hauler's entire game is two sessions bookending one bet — zero decisions in between — so their fun depends wholly on the stay-behind economy being a complete game someone else is playing.
Fix: Give haulers slow-light news trickling in mid-transit — years-old bulletins enabling one real repositioning decision per week (sell forward, reroute) — so the voyage has a heartbeat.
