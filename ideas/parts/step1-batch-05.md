### 101. Ravenpost & Rue
Pitch: You run a backwater potion wholesaler whose only sales channel is letters carried by ravens to adventuring guilds run by real players, and the core loop is drafting pitches, choosing which guilds to court, and waiting days of real time for replies that mostly never come. The silence is total and mechanical: unanswered ravens simply vanish from your outbox, and you never learn if the guild read you, laughed at you, or died in a dungeon. An async mobile game where the rare reply — even a rude one — feels like a jackpot, and players trade folklore about what letter styles "work."

### 102. Offal & Ichor
Pitch: A monster-parts middleman game where returning raid parties auction off carcass lots sight-unseen at the docks, described only in adventurer slang ("two crates wyrm-wet, one sour basilisk, some jangle") that you must learn to decode through expensive mistakes. The uncertainty lives in the lot descriptions — there is no appraisal tool, only your growing private glossary — and rival buyers (real players) bid against you while alchemist customers reject spoiled goods without saying why. Your edge is literacy in a language the game never teaches.

### 103. The Last Lantern
Pitch: You sink your life savings into building an inn at the mouth of whichever dungeon you bet will be next season's hotspot, but dungeon popularity is driven entirely by the shifting meta of real adventurer players you can't poll. The loop is scouting foot-traffic rumors, committing to immovable real estate, and either riding a boom or watching an empty taproom while a rival inn two valleys over overflows. Multiplayer towns emerge organically around good bets, and ghost towns around bad ones stand as public monuments to failed founders.

### 104. Underwriter of the Deep
Pitch: You sell life insurance to real adventurers with zero actuarial data — the game publishes no death statistics, so you price policies on gut, gossip, and the look in a barbarian's eyes. The loop is interviewing applicants, setting premiums, and sweating as their real dungeon runs resolve; a run of bad luck bankrupts you and nobody can tell you if your pricing model was wrong or you were just unlucky. Rival underwriters poach your clients by undercutting prices that may or may not be suicidal.

### 105. The Identifier's Dilemma
Pitch: An appraisal-shop game built on asymmetric information: players bring you unidentified loot, and you choose to appraise it for a fee or buy it blind and flip it. Your reputation is a public wall of chalk marks left by customers — some of whom lie to tank you and buy your foreclosed shop — and you can never fully verify whether a bad review reflects your error or their malice. The whole market is a lemons problem where trust is the only real inventory.

### 106. Guildcharter
Pitch: A politics game where every trade in the city requires a charter granted by a council of elder merchant players and inscrutable NPC magistrates, awarded by sealed bid with no feedback on losing offers. The loop is drafting petitions, cultivating patrons over tavern dinners, losing charters for unstated reasons, and slowly mapping the council's unwritten preferences. Council seats rotate to successful players, so the opaque system you cursed becomes the one you administer — and you discover it was never coherent from the inside either.

### 107. Sevenday
Pitch: The market town of Sevenday resets every real-world week — your shop, stock, and coin wiped — but your notebook of observations carries over, so the loop is running cheap, aggressive experiments (weird products, absurd prices, strange partnerships) because failure costs only days. The town's hidden demand structure is regenerated each week from the aggregate behavior of all players in the previous loop, so the system you're reverse-engineering is made of other people and never fully stabilizes. A structurally weird game about hypothesis velocity: the best players run ten experiments a week and burn their own businesses down on Sixday for data.

### 108. Mimic, Incorporated
Pitch: You are a mimic who has decided to go legitimate and run an actual shop, fighting your own instinct meter that builds toward eating customers mid-transaction. Real adventurer players arrive expecting a trap, poke you with sticks, and leave without buying — the rejection mechanic is that your entire product category (you) is presumed fraudulent, and building trust means letting people hurt you profitably. Word-of-mouth among real players is the only marketing that works, and one slip (one eaten customer) resets years of reputation.

### 109. Gone Below
Pitch: You run a rental outfitter at the dungeon gate — mules, rope, lanterns, climbing rigs — and your working capital walks into the dark every morning strapped to real player parties who may or may not come back. Non-return is the only signal and it never explains itself: a missing party could be dead on floor six, merely delayed, or selling your gear in the next town, so the loop is deciding when to write an asset off, when to hire a repo crew (also players) to go down after it, and how big a deposit you can demand before renters walk to the cheaper stall. Your real inventory is a private blacklist of names and hunches — tradeable, sellable, and sometimes catastrophically wrong.

### 110. Contract of the Brass Djinn
Pitch: All commerce in this world runs on free-text contracts executed with djinn-like literalism by the game itself: you write supply deals, employment terms, and partnership agreements in your own words, and counterparty players hunt for exploitable ambiguity in your phrasing. The uncertainty lives in your own language — you learn contract craft by being burned, and the game never tells you which clause failed you. Courts exist but are slow, expensive, and staffed by player judges with visible biases.

### 111. Bard & Banner
Pitch: You run a marketing agency composing hype campaigns — ditties, planted rumors, banner processions — for other real players' fantasy businesses, and the central torment is attribution: sales data arrives noisy and late, so neither you nor your client ever knows whether your campaign caused the bump. Clients churn silently, just not renewing, and you're left guessing whether your last ballad was genius or garbage. The loop is pitching, producing, and building a portfolio of claimed (unprovable) wins that other players can choose to believe.

### 112. Waystone & Toll
Pitch: An infrastructure game where you finance, build, and maintain a teleportation waystone, betting that player traffic will flow your way — but traffic follows other players' quests, wars, and fads that you can't see coming. You set toll prices in the dark: competitor waystone pricing is invisible until your traffic silently evaporates, which is your only market signal. Long-horizon sessions, spreadsheet-adjacent interface, and the special agony of sunk capital in a fixed location.

### 113. Hoardkeepers
Pitch: You pitch dragon clients — played by the server's wealthiest veteran players — to let you manage their hoards, and dragons give no investment mandate, change their minds without notice, and formally reserve the right to eat managers who lose principal. The loop is prospecting whales, surviving capricious client demands, and making allocation calls (fund this player's caravan venture? that one's mine?) with your neck on the line. Rejection here has teeth: a dropped client publicly scorches your office as a signal to the market.

### 114. Spawnwrights
Pitch: When a dungeon gets farmed empty, the town around it economically dies — so player town councils hire your firm to restock it: sourcing breeding pairs of monsters, seeding floors, and praying an ecosystem takes. Feedback arrives a full season later and hopelessly confounded — adventurer traffic depends on a shifting player meta you can't poll, your introduced species interact in undocumented ways, and one escaped wyvern eating livestock erases three successful contracts' worth of reputation. It's attraction design for an audience you can't survey, using living inventory that has its own opinions about the plan.

### 115. Necropolis Staffing
Pitch: You run an undead temp-labor agency, raising skeletons for other players' farms, shops, and construction sites, under a Church regulatory regime whose edicts are shifting, unpublished, and enforced by inquisitors who arrive without warning. The loop is winning staffing contracts from player businesses, managing a workforce that occasionally wanders off, and probing the boundaries of legality by seeing who gets raided. Compliance knowledge becomes tradeable gossip between agency owners — none of it reliable.

### 116. Caravanserai
Pitch: A logistics game where you schedule trade caravans through territories controlled by player factions, negotiating protection with mercenary companies who may quietly also be the bandits. Route risk is never quantified — you learn which passes are safe this month from survivor reports and the absence of returning drivers — and a protection deal's real value is unknowable until it fails. Multiplayer trust networks form, betray, and reform as the map's politics shift under your shipping lanes.

### 117. The Cursed Goods Exchange
Pitch: You flip cursed items: buy them cheap from desperate players, contract curse-breakers (also players, of wildly uneven skill) to cleanse them, and resell — but curse severity is undetectable before purchase, and some curses transfer to the shopkeeper. The loop is portfolio management over unknowable liabilities, with your warehouse itself accumulating ambient misfortune you can't measure. Curse-breakers won't explain their failures, and the best ones have waitlists you can't buy your way up.

### 118. Familiar & Co.
Pitch: You breed and place familiars matched to wizard clients' personalities, but clients describe their needs badly and the true test happens after sale: a mismatched familiar simply walks back to your shop on its own, wordless, sometimes weeks later. The returning familiar is the whole rejection mechanic — no complaint, no explanation, just a cat on your doorstep and a refund owed — and you must infer what went wrong from nothing. Real players are the clients, and your placement success rate is publicly visible as the crowd of returned familiars living in your window.

### 119. Chokepoint Concessions
Pitch: A structurally weird game where you bid for exclusive vending rights inside other players' live dungeon runs, setting up a supply stall at a chokepoint to sell bandages, torches, and antivenom mid-delve. You bid blind — you don't know the party's build, prep level, or whether they'll even reach your floor — and a flawless party walks past your stall without a word, leaving you to eat the concession fee. Sessions are short and parasitic on other people's adventures, and parties learn to auction their choke rights as a pre-run revenue stream.

### 120. The Prophecy Pit
Pitch: A futures-market game where oracles (part procedural, part player-submitted) issue deliberately vague prophecies ("the river will forget its name"), and you place business bets on interpretations — stockpiling boats, shorting fisheries — before ambiguous world events settle the contracts. Settlement itself is contested: a player adjudication jury rules on what the prophecy "meant," so you're speculating on interpretation twice over. Fortunes are made by reading crowds, not futures.

### 121. Both Banners
Pitch: Two player guild-alliances slide toward war and you outfit both, because that's where the margin is — but every sale writes to a political ledger you can't read, and factions never warn you before acting on it. There is no standing meter: you learn you've crossed an invisible line when a quartermaster stops answering your letters, a shipment goes "lost" at a checkpoint the faction controls, or an embargo lands that is somehow retroactive to last month's invoices. The game is inferring unwritten tolerances from silences and punishments, pricing what neutrality is actually worth, and discovering both sides keep files on you that neither will ever show.

### 122. The Quest Board
Pitch: You own the town's quest board, a two-sided marketplace where player patrons post jobs and player adventurers take them — patrons understate the danger, adventurers overstate their competence, and every failed quest is somehow the board's fault. Your commission only pays on completion, so you float the whole market's dishonesty on your own cash, and your best matches are your worst outcomes: once a patron and a party learn to trust each other, they cut you out and deal direct, forever. The loop is pricing listings, vetting liars with no verification tools, and fighting the slow disintermediation that your own success causes.

### 123. Patch Eve Outfitters
Pitch: You run an adventurer outfitter that must commit its seasonal inventory orders before the world's "patch" drops — a periodic rebalancing of monsters and magic that the game announces exists but never documents. The loop is reading tea leaves (playtest rumors, cryptic developer-oracle pronouncements, smug hints from connected players), placing blind orders, then living with a stockroom full of suddenly-worthless fire resistance gear when the new dungeons turn out to be ice. Real-player customers return merchandise angrily, and the merchants' guild forum is an economy of leaks, lies, and cope.

### 124. The Effluvium Cartel
Pitch: A hazardous-waste business: every potion shop in the city (run by real players) produces volatile alchemical byproducts, and you haul, store, and neutralize them — except nobody, including the game, documents which byproduct combinations detonate. Learning is purely experimental and failures are public: a mishandled load craters a city block, and your reputation with every generator in the zone craters with it. The social layer is a cartel of rival haulers who share just enough safety knowledge to keep the industry legal, and never one drop more.

### 125. The Silent Partner
Pitch: You play the money: an investor staking other real players' fantasy ventures — a new smithy, a monster ranch, a heretical church — based on their pitches, with no right to see their books afterward. Founders send sporadic, spin-heavy update letters, go quiet for weeks at exactly the wrong moments, and your follow-on funding decisions run on vibes, scraps, and pattern-matching across your portfolio of prior heartbreaks. It teaches entrepreneurship from the flip side: the opacity founders inflict, and why the money gets so weird about silence.
