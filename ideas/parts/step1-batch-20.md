### 476. Overflight
Pitch: Your only market intelligence is a blurry aerial photograph of the entire valley, taken once a week by a balloon you don't control — you read demand from smoke plumes, queue shadows, wagon ruts, and the size of rivals' woodpiles. Ground truth is never published, so the metagame becomes trace warfare: players stage fake queues, burn decoy smoke, and rake false ruts to poison each other's photo-reading. The best operators become forensic photographers of an economy nobody can see directly.

### 477. The Fog Exchange
Pitch: A trading floor where no prices, volumes, or order books are ever displayed — you only see your own fills, and you infer the invisible market from how fast your offers clear or rot. The fog is the market: a bid that sits untouched for an hour is your only data point, and it might mean anything. Hundreds of real players trade in the same fog, so the thing you're groping toward is other people groping back.

### 478. No Clocks
Pitch: There are no clocks, calendars, or timers anywhere in the interface — rent, loan calls, seasonal demand shifts, and trend deaths all arrive on schedules you can only infer from ambient cues: light through the shop window, birdsong, the day the regulars start wearing coats. Veterans keep private tally-mark almanacs and argue about how long a "month" even is on this server, and the game never settles it. Getting blindsided by a due date you should have felt coming is the core wound the game teaches you to scar over.

### 479. The Museum of Winners
Pitch: You wander a gorgeous museum where every exhibit is a triumphant business — audio guides, founder statues, glass-cased first dollars — and you copy strategies from exhibits to run your own venture outside. The trick: the basement archive holding ten thousand failed businesses that did the exact same things is locked, and excavating one failure file costs real in-game money most players won't spend. Players who fund basement digs can publish (or hoard, or sell) the base rates the museum hides.

### 480. Guru Bazaar
Pitch: A marketplace where real players sell strategy advice to each other for in-game currency, and every seller's track record is self-reported and unverifiable by design. The core loop is buying advice, betting your venture on it, and slowly learning which vendors are skilled, which are lucky, and which are confident frauds — while the game deliberately never tells you which you just got. Your own path to riches might be selling advice about selling advice.

### 481. The Luck Ledger
Pitch: Every decision you make in your delivery-startup runs is resolved by hidden dice — but after each quarter, the game opens the ledger and shows you the exact rolls, so you see precisely how much of your success was judgment and how much was weather. The leaderboard ranks players by decision quality (choices with the best odds at the time) rather than outcomes, so the richest player on the server is often ranked 400th. Watching a rival's ledger replay is the game's favorite spectator sport.

### 482. Hands & Eyes
Pitch: Two strangers run one company: the Eyes sees every number, contract, and rival move but cannot touch anything; the Hands controls everything but plays completely blind — and the only channel between them is one ten-word note per in-game day. Strategy becomes compression: what do you tell a blind partner when words are the scarcest resource in the firm? Partnerships develop private codes over months, and losing a longtime partner to churn feels like losing a language.

### 483. Vitrine
Pitch: Your shop is mechanically mute — no chat, no ads, no listings — and your entire outbound communication is a single street-facing display window you dress with objects, colors, and arrangements that real passersby players interpret however they will. You learn semiotics by till receipt: did sales jump because of the blue cloth, or in spite of it? Rival windows evolve into a silent visual argument up and down the street, legible to everyone and provable to no one.

### 484. No Second Run
Pitch: A permadeath startup roguelike with a deliberate, advertised absence of meta-progression — no unlocks, no upgrades, no persistent perks, nothing carries between runs except what you personally learned. The world's rules are deep and knowable but the game will never acknowledge your prior deaths, which makes shared player notebooks (in-game, annotatable, inheritable when you die) the only real progression system. Reading a dead stranger's notebook and deciding whether to trust it is the central act.

### 485. Whisper Street
Pitch: A market simulation that publishes no numbers whatsoever — no prices, no indices, no news — so the only market data in existence is what players tell each other in taverns, letters, and rumors. Information is the true commodity, and it can be stale, honestly mistaken, or deliberately poisoned by someone positioning against you. The endgame isn't being rich; it's becoming a node other players trust, then deciding what that trust is worth.

### 486. The Failure Number
Pitch: Floating over every business is a single, brutally honest number — the true probability it dies this year — updated live with every decision you make, and the game never explains a single tick of it. Players run superstitious rituals and careful self-experiments to learn what moves their Number, while investors, landlords, and partners all price you off it, which itself moves it. It's the only honest oracle in the genre, and it still tells you nothing.

### 487. The Fan of Fates
Pitch: You play one focused business run, and at the end the game renders the Fan: a thousand parallel yous who made your identical choices under different dice, spread into a glowing distribution from bankrupt to billionaire, with your actual timeline highlighted as one thin thread. The whole design exists to make luck visceral — you might see that your triumphant run was a 94th-percentile fluke, or your bankruptcy was the modal outcome of a genuinely good strategy. Players share and compare Fans, and the community's status symbol is a fat, right-shifted distribution rather than a lucky thread.

### 488. The Herd
Pitch: Working capital is literally a herd: revenue arrives as animals, payroll eats them monthly, and paying a distant supplier means driving part of the herd overland for days through weather, disease, and rustlers who are real players. A cash crunch isn't a red number — it's thin, lowing animals you can hear from the office. Credit means borrowing someone's breeding stock on a handshake, and a market panic is stampede season.

### 489. Wire Deals
Pitch: All deals happen over an anonymous text wire where you can never learn who your counterpart is — a whale, a broke newbie, a syndicate of twelve players, or a bot — and every claim they make about themselves is unverifiable by design. Negotiation becomes a pure reading-the-text skill under identity fog, and the game never resolves the fog even after the deal closes. Long-term players develop styles recognizable enough that "I think I've traded with this one before" becomes its own currency.

### 490. The Lying Manual
Pitch: The game ships with a lavish, beautifully produced 200-page in-fiction tutorial written by a celebrity guru whose advice is confidently, specifically wrong about a third of the time — and finding out which third is the actual game. Following the manual faithfully produces a mediocre doomed business; the fun is the dawning, paranoid re-reading after your first collapse. The community endgame is a shared annotated edition, which itself accumulates errors, because the guru's estate issues revised editions that re-poison settled knowledge.

### 491. Strata
Pitch: You dig through geological layers of a marketplace where the customers are recorded ghosts — actual purchasing behavior of real players from past seasons — and build businesses to serve the patterns you excavate. The trap is regime change: deeper strata are richer and more legible, but the further back you dig, the more likely that demand no longer exists up on the surface. Current players' live behavior becomes the next stratum, so everyone is simultaneously an archaeologist and a fossil.

### 492. Burn to Speak
Pitch: The only way to communicate anything about your product's quality is to publicly burn resources — money, inventory, your own time-in-queue — in the town square; words, ads, and chat about quality are mechanically nonexistent. Customers must decide what your bonfire means, con artists burn big on garbage products, and honest newcomers can't afford to say anything at all. The market becomes a brutal, legible-yet-ambiguous theater of costly signals that players endlessly try to counterfeit.

### 493. Anecdata
Pitch: Your entire analytics stack is one anecdote: each day the game replays you the complete first-person experience of a single randomly chosen customer — a real player — and shows you no aggregates, ever. The discipline is statistical humility: yesterday's furious sample might be your only unhappy customer or the tip of an exodus, and overcorrecting to an N of one is how most ventures die. Players beg, trade, and lie about their daily anecdotes, assembling a folk census nobody can verify.

### 494. Eminent Domain
Pitch: Your storefront is permanent but the city isn't: every season a council of randomly conscripted real players votes on petitions to reroute roads, move the market square, and bridge or dam the river — silently redrawing whose door the city walks past. Half the game is commerce; the other half is lobbying strangers, reading council politics, and hedging against a map that might abandon you. The best players build businesses that survive being left on the wrong side of a bridge that didn't exist last quarter.

### 495. Control Group
Pitch: Every business ships with a shadow twin: a frozen autopilot copy of your company that keeps doing exactly what you did last quarter, forever — and your only dashboard is the live difference between you and it. The design makes causality visceral: when both twins crater, that was the market; when only you do, that was you. Except the counterfactual drifts noisier every month, and deciding how much to keep trusting your own control group becomes the quiet endgame.

### 496. The Seeded Deck
Pitch: Every venture draws weekly events from a deck you can never inspect — and a portion of your deck is secretly seeded by your competitors, who paid to slip disasters (and occasionally poisoned gifts) into your future. You can pay to burn cards from your deck unseen, or study the discard pile of your past, but never peek ahead. The social metagame is deducing who seeded the strike that ruined you, and whether to retaliate or ally with them.

### 497. Honesty Box
Pitch: You run unattended honesty-box stalls across a countryside — stock them, price nothing, and come back to find goods gone and whatever coins strangers (real players) chose to leave. Theft, generosity, spoilage, and weather all arrive as one merged anonymous residue, so attribution is pure inference: was the empty box a robbery or your best day ever? Scaling means placing boxes in territory you'll visit even less, buying more revenue with less knowledge — the game's whole thesis in a tin box.

### 498. Séance Round
Pitch: Your advisory board is a ouija circle of dead founders who answer questions one letter at a time, agonizingly slowly, and whose knowledge froze on the day their business died — the 1998 mail-order queen gives brilliant advice for a world that no longer exists. The tension is triage: séance time is scarce, ghosts ramble, and you must decide which fragments of stale wisdom still transfer. Ghosts are voiced by players whose own ventures died, haunting the living as a post-bankruptcy game mode.

### 499. Same Product, Different Day
Pitch: Every player in the season sells the literally identical product — same specs, same cost, same quality — and the only decisions are when and where to launch, scale, and exit, against hidden regime clocks that make the same move brilliant in March and fatal in May. By stripping out product differences, the game isolates timing, and the season-end reveal shows the full launch-date-versus-outcome scatter plot: a public monument to how much of everyone's fate was calendar luck. Players write manifestos claiming they read the regime; the scatter plot quietly disagrees.

### 500. Shopkeeper in Someone Else's Game
Pitch: You run a business embedded as an "NPC vendor" inside a live fantasy MMO whose adventurer players mostly don't know which shopkeepers are humans — you set prices, stock inventory, spread quest rumors to steer foot traffic, and survive rent in a world whose patch notes, metas, and gold-farming economies you don't control and were never explained. Your market shocks are another game's balance changes, arriving without warning or apology. The deepest uncertainty is social: adventurers who suspect you're human start testing you, and being unmasked changes everything about how they haggle.
