### 351. Scar Capital — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Reputation is a compounding, invisible ledger — you learn who you burned only from the temperature of the next conversation, which is exactly how real networks punish you.
Cheats: The mechanic only fires with massive population density and repeat encounters; in a big MMO most cold calls hit strangers with no memory of you, so the load-bearing silence goes quiet for the wrong reason.
Note: Uncertainty is properly epistemic (you can reconstruct who hates you from behavioral evidence), and players keeping private grudge-CRMs is not degeneration — it is the lesson.

### 352. Ninety Days — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Runway converts every follow-up email into a real opportunity-cost decision — burning a day on maybe-ignored outreach with no delivery receipt is the truest rendering of founder time allocation here.
Cheats: One-big-action-per-day flattens company-building into a slot puzzle whose optimal opening sequences the community will solve within a month, leaving only the player-silence layer unsolved.
Note: The deathwatch is brilliant spectacle economics, but watch that lifelines don't become a popularity-contest bailout that teaches audience-farming over business.

### 353. The Post-Mortem Forge — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Misattributing your own failure is the deadliest founder error, and carrying a wrong lesson into the next venture quietly kills it — mechanically delivered, not narrated.
Cheats: It presumes the game knows the one true cause of death, granting markets a legible causality real founders never get, and forging lessons into stat-relics drifts toward exactly the attribute-grinding the brief forbids.
Note: The contest market is genuine peer review, but expect meta-gaming where players write deliberately contestable post-mortems to farm challenges.

### 354. Ghost Rival — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: You are not your company, and your strategies commoditize the moment other people can execute them — a lesson founders learn only by watching their playbook used against them.
Cheats: The entire premise hangs on the sim or hired player running your old firm competently; if they don't, it is haunted-house flavor text, and the player learns spite, not judgment.
Note: More dramatic-irony engine than entrepreneurship teacher — the acquiring-your-own-ghost endgame is compelling content but the day-to-day loop is unspecified.

### 355. Personal Guarantee — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Offered terms are information — predatory pricing from real player-lenders tells you exactly how doomed the market thinks you are, training the term-sheet-reading instinct.
Cheats: It is a collateral mechanic wearing a game's clothes; without the underlying company sim specified, it risks being pure loss-aversion torture that teaches players to fear debt rather than price it.
Note: Real-player lenders shopping for distressed founders is unsolvable by wiki, which makes the adversarial pricing permanently epistemic.

### 356. Rejection Deck — TEACH: 3/10
Teaches: Rejection has residual value and thick skin is an asset — about the only true thing surviving the loot-ification.
Cheats: It inverts the actual lesson: rewarding rejections with card power makes players farm "no" as a grind resource, teaching rejection-seeking as optimization instead of adaptation, and the deck IS character attributes.
Note: This is the batch's clearest business-themed-game failure — silence minting hidden-effect blanks is arbitrary RNG, not epistemic, and the whole thing wikis into a tier list.

### 357. The Ten-Minute Term Sheet — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: The warm smile and the walk to the elevator with zero explanation is the most honest rejection in the batch, and storing progression only in the player's own read of human tells is the purest meta-progression design here.
Cheats: Recurring archetypes "in remixed disguises" is a taxonomy waiting for a community spreadsheet — six months in, players pattern-match the disguise in room two and the psychology becomes a lookup table.
Note: Also scope-narrow: it teaches pitching, one organ of entrepreneurship, with no cash flow, building, or customers anywhere.

### 358. Overdraft — TEACH: 3/10
Teaches: Cash balance as literal ground underfoot makes burn viscerally legible for about ten minutes.
Cheats: An "unexplained seasonal wind" with explicitly no learnable pattern is pure RNG frustration by design, and the skill being tested is platformer twitch, not one second of business judgment.
Note: The shared interest-rate gravity is a genuinely nice macro-economy touch bolted onto a game that is a metaphor, not a market — nothing here survives contact with the teach test.

### 359. Night Market — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Operating on rumor when half the whisper network is planted lies trains real information-market skepticism — who to trust, what a tip costs, when the consensus is bait.
Cheats: The hidden layout algorithm is a datamining target; the day the reshuffle rules hit a wiki, the game's central mystery becomes a solved traffic model unless the devs keep shifting it forever.
Note: Rent-as-permadeath plus row-politics (save the neighbor or take the spot) delivers cash pressure and coopetition with real players — the uncertainty is epistemic and socially regenerating.

### 360. The Creditors Remember — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Debt outlives the company that incurred it, and the player-owned secondary debt market is a legitimately sharp lesson in how obligations become other people's assets.
Cheats: Hidden patience rules plus explicitly random arrival timing means the collectors are jump-scare RNG, not a system you can form beliefs about, and the core loop teaches evasion — flee mechanics, not financial judgment.
Note: Punishment theater; the knock-during-launch moment is great drama the first time and pure frustration the fifth.

### 361. Cosigned — TEACH: 9/10
Teaches: You cannot even begin without social capital, unexplained refusal and outright silence gate the game's front door, and the vouching web makes trust, obligation, and reference-risk the literal map of the world.
Cheats: The cold-start problem is brutal by design — a new player begging strangers with no track record may just churn before the lesson lands, and sign-for-pay cartels will emerge.
Note: The cartels are the defense, not the flaw: vouching markets, accelerator-godparents, and grudge webs are real entrepreneurship, so the degeneration path IS the curriculum — this cannot be patched out by a wiki.

### 362. The Gatekeeper Spire — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: The world does not reject you, it fails to notice you — monsters that ignore you and runs that end in darkness rather than combat is the truest mechanical rendering of cold outreach in the pool.
Cheats: Funding stages as dungeon floors is a cartoon ladder implying entrepreneurship is a linear gauntlet with a top, and the signal-item meta will be tier-listed fast if gatekeeper psychology doesn't drift.
Note: Souls-graffiti-with-sabotage is a perfect model of founder advice culture — the community itself keeps the epistemic uncertainty alive by lying in it.

### 363. The Understudy — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Attention is the scarcest founder resource, and what you learn from a run is partial, biased, and sometimes wrong — carrying forward misreadings as if they were wisdom is devastatingly true.
Cheats: The game must codify what a "witnessed scene" teaches, and that codification becomes a wiki page of optimal room-sitting rotations, converting hard-won judgment into a scheduling exercise.
Note: Renting understudies into strangers' failing runs — tuition paid by watching others die — is the standout mechanic and genuinely MMO-native.

### 364. Same Week, Different Market — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: You never get a clean counterfactual — the inability to tell your mistake from a stranger's ripple is the deepest epistemic truth about markets any game in this batch attempts.
Cheats: Time loops promise mastery, and deliberately denying attribution risks players experiencing designed unfairness as bugs — the whole lesson rides on the post-run loop-diary comparison actually making the interference legible.
Note: Player-interleaved loops mean the week never fully solves, so it resists wiki-death structurally; the danger is frustration churn before the diary payoff, not degeneration.

### 365. Ghost Franchise — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Brand is an asset that outlives the operator, and the franchisee you cannot observe is a clean principal-agent lesson with real players as the agents.
Cheats: "Build fast, die famous" as a leaderboard-viable strategy teaches hype-then-exit as an optimization target — the game rewards manufacturing recognition over building anything, which is a cartoon with real-world victims.
Note: Rich players who haven't run a company in months is honest (rentiers exist), but expect the meta to collapse into scandal-insurance and brand-pump degenerate play.

### 366. Founders' Rest — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Post-mortems are unreliable narrators — the dead founder never knew why the customer went silent either, so extracting signal from biased testimony is genuinely your problem.
Cheats: It is a museum, not a game: no cash pressure, no bets, no consequences for misreading a memory, so the critical-interpretation lesson is decorative rather than load-bearing.
Note: Wisdom-through-remembrance currency turns grave-tending into a daily-quest grind within weeks, and the most-visited graves become a wiki's greatest-hits list.

### 367. The Only Employee — TEACH: 9/10
Teaches: Hiring is trust calibration with real stakes — you see outputs but never effort, firing costs money you don't have, and a human who can coast or walk out with your customer list cannot be solved, only judged.
Cheats: The two-body scope means the "company" around the relationship is thin, and collusion or smurf-alt exploits could let players fake references and gut the review board's signal.
Note: Because the uncertainty is another live human's character, it is permanently epistemic and structurally wiki-proof — the meta-progression being your boss reputation is the right asset to persist.

### 368. Control Group — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Even with the cleanest A/B test any founder will ever get, hidden noise means you still cannot prove your decision mattered — causal humility delivered as a core mechanic.
Cheats: Sealed twin markets are lab apparatus no real founder ever has, so the fiction is artificial, and the forced merge risks being a stats comparison rather than a judgment call.
Note: The community arguing forever over published twin data is the point — the game manufactures the replication crisis of business advice, and that argument cannot degenerate because the noise is resampled every run.

### 369. Seed of the Day — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: A thousand founders making a thousand different fatal reads of the identical reality is the cleanest luck-versus-judgment separator ever proposed — one attempt forces real commitment before understanding.
Cheats: Unless every run is literally simultaneous, the 00:01 player leaks the day-four shock and everyone after plays a spoiled seed — the single-attempt epistemic gamble dies to Discord within one news cycle.
Note: Great as a mode bolted onto a bigger game, thin as the game itself; the daily-reveal ritual is the actual product.

### 370. Non-Compete — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Choosing where you can afford to fail is an underrated strategic skill, and the is-dead-founder-advice-wisdom-or-poison consulting market is a sharp trust question.
Cheats: It structurally punishes the most realistic founder move — retrying the same market with scar tissue — so its central rule is a variety-forcing design constraint cosplaying as a lesson real founders would call backwards.
Note: Grizzled players as walking maps of forbidden sectors is flavorful, but the ban mechanic teaches breadth-by-fiat, not judgment.

### 371. Sunk Years — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Time is the only truly non-renewable founder resource — waiting costs the same as building, quit-versus-dig-in has no formula, and luck decides in hindsight which was right, exactly as in life.
Cheats: Account-level retirement is so punishing that the market response is alt accounts, and the moment alts are viable the entire mortality economy is a polite fiction.
Note: If retirement genuinely holds, this is one of the few designs whose lesson is structural and unwikiable; the age-cohort economy trading energy for networks is honest and MMO-native.

### 372. Board Purgatory — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Governance is people with agendas warped by their own histories, reputation runs in both directions across the table, and the directors you snubbed will be seated at your judgment — all mechanically real because they are real players.
Cheats: Earning your next seed check through board service inverts actual incentive flows in a way no founder would recognize, and boardroom gameplay risks devolving into faction-politics grind divorced from any company's reality.
Note: The two-sided conduct memory (tyrant directors go unfunded) is the load-bearing part and is epistemic — you learn people, not systems.

### 373. Scar Tissue — TEACH: 4/10
Teaches: Failure is an unfakeable credential and experienced people wear their history — VCs really do fund the scarred.
Cheats: Making scars the ONLY progression with specific doors requiring specific marks guarantees players speedrun deliberate bankruptcies to farm the right tattoos — the design literally becomes grinding character attributes via engineered failure.
Note: Reading a stranger's forearms is lovely social UI, but the degeneration is total and arrives on day one of the wiki listing which scar opens which door.

### 374. Estate Sale Sunday — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Deciding what to carry out of a failure under time pressure, with no price tags on the future, is a real triage-and-sunk-cost moment rendered with genuine tenderness.
Cheats: "Nothing indicates future usefulness" collapses toward blind lottery on early runs, and as an interstitial minigame it has no economy, no outreach, no market — it decorates entrepreneurship rather than teaching it.
Note: Discards shipping into strangers' foreclosure crates is a charming MMO touch; across many runs players do learn what tends to matter, which nudges the uncertainty from RNG toward epistemic.

### 375. The Number You Have Reached — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Most threads just go dead and you never learn which silence was a test, a lost deal, or a bot — delivered on your actual phone at actually inconvenient hours, this is the founder's phenomenology with no gamified anesthesia.
Cheats: If there is no learnable causal system beneath the silences, it shades into pure noise theater, and reply-speed pressure risks teaching notification compliance rather than judgment about which threads deserve you.
Note: Burning the number forever is real permadeath of a network — structurally unwikiable since threads are ephemeral and some counterparties are live players on burners; the design's whole fate rests on the hidden system actually existing.
