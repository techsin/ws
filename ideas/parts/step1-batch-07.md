### 151. Forecast: Feed
Pitch: You run a small streaming channel under a platform algorithm that behaves like procedurally generated weather — fronts of reach, droughts of impressions — which you can never see directly, only forecast. The loop is planning a week of content against your own folk-meteorology, publishing, then reading noisy aftermath data that may or may not confirm your theory. Multiplayer lives in a shared almanac where players trade forecasts and superstitions, some of which are confidently, contagiously wrong.

### 152. Dead Air
Pitch: A podcast-network builder played almost entirely inside a fake email client: you cold-pitch guests, sponsors, and cross-promo partners, and the core mechanic is that most threads simply never get a reply — no rejection, just an inbox that stays quiet while your production calendar burns. You learn to read tiny tells (opened-but-unanswered, a reply from an assistant) and to keep twenty pitches alive at once. Other players' networks are poaching the same finite pool of NPC guests, and you can see when a guest you pitched appears on a rival's show instead.

### 153. Demo Pile
Pitch: You run an indie label with a nightly pile of demo tapes — some procedurally generated, some actual 30-second loops uploaded by other players — and you must sign acts with cash advances before rivals do. The market's taste drifts invisibly between seasons, so the sound you overpaid for in March is landfill by June, and nobody sends you a memo about it. Signed player-artists become real collaborators who can also walk out on you for a better deal.

### 154. The Algorithm Is a Player
Pitch: Asymmetric multiplayer where one hidden player each season literally IS the recommendation algorithm: they write secret distribution rules, and the twenty creator-players must infer those rules purely from which uploads live or die. Creators experiment, share (or hoard) theories, and chase reach; the Algorithm player scores by keeping engagement high without being decoded. Mid-season the platform "ships an update" — the Algorithm may quietly rewrite one rule, torching everyone's hard-won folklore.

### 155. Churn Garden
Pitch: A cozy-sinister game where your audience is an underground garden you can never look at directly — you only get sensor readouts (opens, likes, watch time) that are noisy proxies for what's actually growing or rotting down there. The loop is planting content seeds, reading crooked instruments, and discovering too late that a whole bed of subscribers quietly died in the dark. Neighboring players' gardens share a water table, so a viral bloom next door can drain your soil without either of you understanding why.

### 156. Clipfarm
Pitch: A fast twitchy multiplayer race where a shared live "event stream" (an absurd generated broadcast) plays for everyone at once, and players compete to clip, caption, and post the same moments within seconds of each other. Virality is decided by a hidden-weight lottery, so a technically identical clip posted three seconds later can do 100x the numbers, and you never learn why. Short sessions, leaderboards per event, and a gray market where big accounts (players) buy clips from farmers.

### 157. Roster
Pitch: You run an influencer talent agency repping a roster of creators — some NPC, some other real players — and your job is pitching them into brand deals with black-box corporate buyers. Deals die silently at any pipeline stage: verbal yes, then contracts "in legal" forever, then nothing, and you must decide when to stop chasing and when a ghost is actually still alive. Player-creators can fire you for a rival agency, and you never find out what the other agent promised.

### 158. Deprecated
Pitch: A survival-strategy game about platform risk: you build audiences across several platforms, and periodically one changes its rules overnight or dies entirely with no warning and no appeal process. The loop is hedging — how much do you invest in the booming platform versus the boring owned-audience email list — and then executing a panicked migration when the extinction event hits. Every player is struck by the same collapse simultaneously, triggering a server-wide land grab on whatever platform is next.

### 159. Parasocial
Pitch: You manage the relationship between a creator and a fandom that has its own simulated (and partly player-driven) inner politics: pander and the fandom's respect decays, ignore them and they bleed away, and either way they never tell you which line you crossed. The loop is choosing what to share of "yourself" — each disclosure is an irreversible card played into the fandom's collective memory. Other players can join your fandom incognito, including rivals seeding discontent.

### 160. Demonetized
Pitch: Your channel's income sits at the mercy of an automated enforcement system that hands out strikes, age-restrictions, and demonetizations with no stated reason, and an appeal button that leads into a queue of unknown length and unknowable criteria. The loop is producing under paranoia — building folk theories about which words, sounds, or thumbnail pixels trip the machine, diversifying income so a three-strike week doesn't end you — while the enforcement rules silently change under your feet. Other players can file copyright claims against you (legitimate or predatory), spawning a gray economy of claim-trolls, strike-brokers, and "reinstatement consultants" who may know nothing at all.

### 161. Ghost
Pitch: You are a ghostwriter for bigger creators — real players — writing their scripts and tweets for pay while your name appears nowhere. The loop is balancing income against the slow build of a shadow reputation that only circulates by word of mouth between clients you're contractually forbidden to name. The endgame question is when, or whether, to burn your whole client book by going public as yourself, with no way to know in advance if your invisible reputation was ever real.

### 162. Drop Friday
Pitch: A cash-flow pressure cooker about creator merch: you commit to inventory orders months of game-time in advance, against demand you can only guess at, then all players' drops go live in the same synchronized Friday window competing for a finite pool of fan wallets. Overorder and you're liquidating hoodies at a loss; underorder and the hype you paid for evaporates unspent. The weekly synchronous drop event is the social heartbeat — everyone watching everyone's sellout timers.

### 163. Trend Futures
Pitch: A trading-floor game where you buy and sell futures contracts on microgenres and aesthetics ("goblincore audio dramas, Q3") before they blow up or die. The trend engine is opaque and partially reflexive — heavy player buying can itself ignite a trend or curse it — so you're never sure if you're predicting the wave or accidentally being it. Public order books mean everyone trades against everyone, and the richest players start manufacturing trends just to dump them.

### 164. The Collab Web
Pitch: Growth in this game comes only through collaborations, and collabs only happen by cold-DMing other real players — most of whom will leave you on read, because answering small accounts costs them status. The game board is the visible network graph of who has collabed with whom, making everyone's social capital brutally public. Strategy lives in ladder-climbing: which slightly-bigger node might actually reply, and what can you offer that costs you nothing but reads as valuable.

### 165. Crisis Retainer
Pitch: You run a reputation-crisis agency for creators: clients (NPC and player) pay retainers against the day an opaque outrage simulator decides it's their turn. When a firestorm ignites, you play a tense real-time hand of intervention cards — apologize, wait, lawyer up, counter-narrative — with no reliable model of which move feeds the flames, because the outrage engine's rules shift with the culture. Rival agencies can quietly amplify your client's crisis to prove you're bad at your job.

### 166. Big in Veldova
Pitch: Overnight, ninety percent of your views come from a country you have never thought about — the comments arrive mangled by machine translation, the fan memes reference things you don't understand, and nobody can tell you why they love you or what would make them stop. The loop is betting real money on a market you cannot read: hiring player-run translators and local fixers whose interpretations of your own audience contradict each other, licensing merch through partners you can't vet, booking a tour in cities you can't pronounce. Rival creators are courting the same inexplicable audience, and the one insight you need most — what it is they actually see in you — is the one thing no amount of spend can buy.

### 167. Open Mic Purgatory
Pitch: A comedy-circuit ladder game where you actually write short bits (a few lines of text) and perform them into rooms of real players acting as the audience, whose laughter — or devastating silence — is delivered asynchronously over hours. Bookers are NPC gatekeepers who watch silently, never give notes, and simply do or don't call, weeks later. The loop is bombing, dissecting the silence with zero data, rewriting, and going back up.

### 168. The Backlog
Pitch: You inherit a dead channel with 400 forgotten videos, and the algorithm randomly resurrects old content years after upload — so your core resource is your own archaeological past, not new production. The loop is excavating the backlog, re-editing, making sequels to whatever the platform inexplicably exhumed this week, while never knowing which grave will open next. Other players trade in dead channels like real estate, speculating on archives that might one day erupt.

### 169. Signal in the Static
Pitch: A slow-burn newsletter game where feedback is sparse, delayed, and unexplained: open rates arrive weeks late, unsubscribes come with no reason attached, and an A/B test takes ten in-game days to mature while your runway shrinks. Subscribers are drawn from a shared attention pool, so every reader you win was quietly pried from another player's list. Sessions are ten contemplative minutes a day; the tension is entirely in what the numbers refuse to tell you.

### 170. Second Chances Records
Pitch: You run a label that only signs acts other players' labels have dropped — every artist arrives with a hidden reason for their failure (bad timing, sabotage, addiction, wrong market, or the original label's incompetence) that you must diagnose through gameplay, not a stat sheet. Engineering a comeback means betting real money on your theory of why they flopped. The former label watches your progress, and public vindication (or humiliation) flows between players.

### 171. The Green Room
Pitch: A social-deduction party game set at an endless industry mixer where some attendees are real gatekeepers — players holding actual commissioning budgets in the game economy — and the rest are pretenders performing importance. You get a few pitches per night, pitching a fake burns your reputation, and gatekeepers gain by staying camouflaged. Reading status signals, name-drops, and who's drifting toward whom IS the game.

### 172. Treadmill
Pitch: A notification-based game that runs on real-world time: your channel's audience decays across actual days unless you publish on the schedule you promised, so the game's true subject is the content treadmill and your own relationship to commitment. Skipping a real weekend has consequences your subscribers never explain; taking a deliberate hiatus is a terrifying, data-free gamble. Players form pods that can cover each other's slots — a mutual-aid mechanic where flaking damages real relationships.

### 173. Playlist Politics
Pitch: Your artists live or die by placement on playlists controlled by anonymous curator-players, whose tastes and prices are undocumented and whose DMs mostly go unanswered. The loop is courtship under fog: gifts, favors, borderline payola, all without ever being told what the curator actually wants — while curators themselves are ranked by an opaque platform score that punishes them for bad picks. Every relationship is two black boxes negotiating.

### 174. Pivot or Die
Pitch: A roguelike where each run is a channel, and every time growth stalls you must pivot niches — keeping only an unpredictable fraction of your audience, since which segments survive which pivots is hidden knowledge that persists across runs as your only meta-progression. There are no stat upgrades between runs, just scar tissue and pattern recognition. A shared graveyard shows every player's dead channels and their final pivots, a public museum of failed theories.

### 175. Conglomerate
Pitch: The empire-scale endgame: you acquire channels, newsletters, and podcasts actually built by other players in the game's smaller modes, doing due diligence through fog — sellers can dress up rotting audiences, botted metrics, and burned-out hosts to look like healthy assets. The loop is deal flow, valuation under deception, and post-acquisition discovery of what you really bought. Every sale is a genuine two-player negotiation where the seller knows things you never will.
