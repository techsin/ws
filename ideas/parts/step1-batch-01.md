### 1. Reply-All Ghosts
Pitch: The entire game is a fake email client: you research leads, craft cold outreach, hit send, and then live with the silence — no fail state, no rejection screen, just an open-tracking pixel that fires ambiguously or not at all. Deliverability, subject-line fatigue, and burned domains are hidden systems you can only infer from patterns across hundreds of sends. Other players' founder-personas are among your recipients, and a gray market exists where players sell each other lead lists of dubious freshness.

### 2. The Dashboard Lies
Pitch: Your product's true product-market fit is a hidden variable you never see; you only get noisy, sampled, occasionally broken analytics dashboards, and every feature you ship perturbs the real number invisibly. The core loop is spending scarce cash on either better instrumentation or actual product work, then making bets on data you half-trust. A shared industry-benchmark report is aggregated from real players' submitted metrics — and players can (and do) inflate theirs.

### 3. Ghost Round
Pitch: An asymmetric MMO where founders pitch and a separate population of players runs VC funds with their own portfolio-construction game whose incentives founders can't see — passing on you silently is often the VC player's optimal move, so ghosting emerges from real strategy rather than a script. Founders piece together fund theses from crumbs: who they funded, what they liked at demo day, secondhand gossip in the game's forum. Reputation flows both ways, and founder players keep a whisper network on which funds actually respond.

### 4. Runway (a fundraise roguelike)
Pitch: A deck-building roguelike where your cards are pitch slides, traction claims, and warm anecdotes, and each run is one fundraise across a gauntlet of partner meetings before the money clock hits zero. Each investor boss has a hidden thesis drawn from a live meta that quietly shifts based on what the whole playerbase has been pitching lately — last month's winning deck is this month's pattern-matched pass. Between runs, players publish (or sell) their read on the current meta, which may already be stale.

### 5. Cap Table
Pitch: A tense one-on-one negotiation duel where a founder and an investor construct a term sheet clause by clause — valuation, liquidation preference, board seats, pro rata — like a chess opening with money. The founder can't see the investor's fund vintage and reserve pressure; the investor can't see the founder's real pipeline of competing offers, and both may be bluffing about time pressure. Ranked ladder play, with famous historically brutal term sheets as puzzle scenarios.

### 6. Churnyard
Pitch: You run a subscription tool inside a persistent world where your users are real players who genuinely rely on it for their own in-game businesses — and who can silently cancel at any moment. The loop is shipping features, reading cohort curves, and running exit surveys that almost nobody answers. The horror is that churn arrives with no explanation, and win-back campaigns land in the same void your cold emails do.

### 7. API Wars
Pitch: A dev-tools MMO where your product is an API and revenue only exists when other players actually wire it into their own in-game products — you see error rates and usage graphs, never the reason an integration was abandoned. Shipping a breaking change ripples through the whole dependent economy and can nuke your reputation overnight. Endgame is platform politics: deprecating rivals, courting the biggest integrator guilds, and surviving the day a whale drops you without a word.

### 8. Cold Start
Pitch: A two-sided marketplace sim about the chicken-and-egg problem: seed drivers or riders, sellers or buyers, in a living city where every subsidy and fake-it-till-liquidity trick has an elasticity you can only discover by burning money on it. The map never tells you why a neighborhood didn't ignite. Rival marketplaces are other players who can poach your painstakingly recruited supply side with a better take rate.

### 9. Standup
Pitch: A daily-cadence game (Wordle energy) where you make exactly one founder decision per real day and consequences surface days later, making attribution genuinely foggy — did Tuesday's pricing change cause Friday's dip, or was it the market? Sessions are five minutes; companies live or die over real-world months. Players share their "Day 47: died to a payroll miss" grids, and you can run your company alongside friends' ghost companies facing the identical hidden market.

### 10. Post-Mortem
Pitch: A roguelite where your startup will almost certainly die, but each death forensically reveals one verified true fact about the hidden market model ("mid-market buyers never self-serve"), which gets written into a codex shared by the entire playerbase. The twist: the simulated economy drifts over real weeks, so codex entries silently rot, and the community must constantly re-test its own received wisdom. Progress is epistemic, not stat-based — you get smarter, not stronger.

### 11. The Feed
Pitch: Every player sells apps into a store ranked by an actual black-box recommender model whose weights are secret and periodically retrained on the whole playerbase's behavior — so any discovered growth hack destroys itself as players pile in. The loop is shipping variants, watching rank twitch, and deciding whether a spike was your change or the algorithm's mood. Guilds form around competing folk theories of the algorithm, most of which are wrong.

### 12. Voicemail
Pitch: A voice-interface game played like a phone: you cold-call into simulated enterprises, talking your way past LLM-driven receptionists, procurement gatekeepers, and the eternal "she's in a meeting," with most calls dead-ending in voicemail purgatory you never hear back from. Org charts are hidden; the champion who loves you may have no budget, and you find out three weeks of calls later. Players trade hand-annotated org-chart intel on target accounts in a shared map that's part wiki, part rumor mill.

### 13. Burn
Pitch: A survival-horror take on cash flow: your office at night is lit only by your remaining runway, payroll day stalks the calendar like a recurring monster, and choosing which invoice to pay first is the combat system. Revenue arrives late, disputed, or not at all, and you can't see which. Two-player co-op casts cofounders with split authority over the bank account, which is where the real horror lives.

### 14. Term Sheet Poker
Pitch: A fast party/tabletop-style digital game where founders and investors sit at one table betting with hidden information: founders' traction cards may be real or vapor, and investors bet via term sheet structures where liquidation preferences are the kicker that reveals who actually believed. Bluffing your metrics is legal; getting caught craters your reputation for future hands. Best played with voice chat and four friends who will never trust each other again.

### 15. Fake Door
Pitch: You never build a product — the whole game is landing pages, ad micro-budgets, and waitlists, hunting for demand signals in conversion noise where the truth shifts weekly. Validated demand is itself the commodity: you sell proven concepts on an in-game exchange to builder-type players who bear the execution risk. The dread mechanic is that a great signal can simply evaporate between validation and sale, and no one will tell you why.

### 16. Null Result
Pitch: A puzzle game set in a world that never gives negative feedback: every action either visibly succeeds or produces absolutely nothing, and the game is constructing experiments — control groups, staggered launches, channel isolation — to distinguish "no" from "not yet" from "wrong audience." Levels are markets; the solution is a valid inference, not a product. Players publish their experimental designs, and elegant ones get reused by the community like speedrun routes.

### 17. Warm Intro
Pitch: An MMO where cold access to capital literally does not exist as a verb — you can only reach investors through chains of real-player introductions, and every introducer stakes reputation that burns if you flame out in the meeting. The social graph is entirely player-built, so gatekeeping, favor-trading, and kingmaker nodes emerge naturally. New players start outside the graph entirely and must earn their first edge, which is the game's brutal thesis statement.

### 18. Ticket Queue
Pitch: You experience your entire company through a single window: the support inbox. Product quality, market shifts, a competitor's launch, a billing bug — everything must be inferred from the stream of tickets, some of which are exaggerated, confused, or planted by rival players running sabotage campaigns. The loop is triage-as-detective-work: which three tickets this hour are the real signal about what's killing you?

### 19. Soft Landing
Pitch: You never found a company in this game — you inherit one that's already dying, procedurally generated with nine months of runway, a demoralized team, and books full of surprises, and the goal is any exit that isn't zero: acquihire, fire-sale, miracle turnaround. Corp-dev counterparties are other players with hidden mandates who go quiet for weeks mid-diligence because their internal politics shifted. A pure endgame chess problem where dignity is the score.

### 20. Replumb
Pitch: A Factorio-style machine game where your startup is literal live plumbing — leads flow in pipes, features are pumps, revenue drips out the far end — and a pivot means re-plumbing the machine while it's running and cash pressure is dropping. Market demand is a fluid whose composition you can only sample crudely at a few gauges. Players can publish blueprints of their machines, but a blueprint that printed money in last quarter's demand mix quietly fails in this one's.

### 21. Cofounder
Pitch: You're matched with a real stranger and must split equity, choose vesting, and divide control before you know their skill, honesty, or staying power — then run a company together for real weeks. The game engineers asymmetric temptations: solo acquisition offers, secret side deals, the option to just quietly stop showing up. It's a prisoner's dilemma stretched across a month, and the postmortem conversations are the real content.

### 22. Compliance
Pitch: You're a fintech founder and the regulator is an undocumented parser: submit filings, receive cryptic rejection codes, or — worse — ninety days of silence that might mean approval pending or application lost. The community collectively reverse-engineers the rule grammar like a TIS-100 forum, but the rules get silently patched, invalidating hard-won folklore. Competing players must decide whether to share regulatory discoveries or weaponize a rival's non-compliance by reporting them.

### 23. Out of Office
Pitch: A notification-native game that lives in your phone's lock screen: the sim runs in true real time, and your company contacts you via push notifications styled as Slack DMs — an engineer threatening to quit, a customer escalating, an investor finally replying at 11pm. You answer with short replies within a time window or the situation resolves without you, for better or worse. Friends' companies exist in the same world, so their crises sometimes ping you for help, mid-dinner.

### 24. Board Deck
Pitch: An information-control game where the sim mostly runs itself and your real gameplay is deciding, once a month, which metrics to show your board and how to frame them — while an invisible "narrative debt" meter compounds every time reality and your story diverge. The board (a mix of AI directors and real investor-players) controls follow-on funding and can fire you. Truth is always an available move; the game is about discovering how expensive it becomes over time.

### 25. Fork Threat
Pitch: An open-source maintainer game where your repo accrues stars, issues, and entitled users from a mix of simulated devs and real players, and the central tension is monetizing without triggering the community — because other players can genuinely fork your project and take your contributors with them. The loop is triage, licensing gambits, and shipping a paid cloud tier while reading the community's mood from noisy proxies. Nobody announces they're leaving; your download graph just quietly bends.
