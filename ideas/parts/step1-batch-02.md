### 26. Dead Corner
Pitch: You scout storefronts on a persistent shared city map where foot traffic is generated by other players' avatars actually running their errands, then sign a lease and find out what the corner is really worth. Traffic data before signing is rumor-grade, corners die for reasons the game never states (a bus route quietly rerouted, a rival opened two blocks over), and your only feedback is the till. Rival players bid on the same leases, and vacated corners carry visible ghost signage of everyone who failed there before you.

### 27. Form 27-B
Pitch: A bureaucratic dungeon crawler where opening your shop requires navigating the Permit Office, a literal maze of counters, waiting rooms, and forms that reference other forms. Requirements change mid-application, rejections arrive as bare stamp codes with no explanation, and the clerks are other players moonlighting for in-game income with their own quotas and moods. Bribing, befriending, or simply outlasting a player-clerk becomes its own social metagame.

### 28. Fade
Pitch: You run a barbershop where cutting hair is a tactile skill mini-game, but the real game is regulars: actual players book chairs, sit through your cut, and either come back or silently never do. No-shows give no notice, word-of-mouth reputation spreads through channels you cannot read, and a dead Tuesday tells you nothing about why. Your Discord-style waiting room chat is where customers gossip about you, sometimes within earshot.

### 29. Quarters
Pitch: A laundromat game where cash flow is literal: coin trays fill in real time, machines depreciate and jam, and rent day demands you physically consolidate enough quarters to pay. The uncertainty lives in breakdowns you can't predict, a coin-op distributor who stops answering, and demand swings nobody explains. Scale up into a multi-location route and hire other players as collection runners who may or may not skim.

### 30. The Landlord's Table
Pitch: An asymmetric negotiation game where one player owns a strip of storefronts and everyone else wants a lease; the landlord holds hidden competing offers and silence is a legal move — your proposal can simply expire unanswered. Tenants never learn why they lost a space or what the winning terms were. Long-term, landlords develop reputations in player-run forums that may themselves be planted misinformation.

### 31. Footfall Futures
Pitch: Before committing to a location, you can buy traffic reports — but every report is authored and sold by another player acting as a data broker, with no accuracy guarantee. Ground truth only reveals itself after you've signed a twelve-month lease, at which point the broker may have vanished or rebranded. Broker reputation, fraud, and revenge reviews form the emergent social economy.

### 32. Ghosted Gym
Pitch: You run a gym where members never quit loudly — they just stop showing up, and the game refuses to tell you why. Your only tool is experimentation: change the music, the hours, the pricing, the smell, and read noisy, confounded retention curves like tea leaves. Leagues of gym-owner players compare churn graphs, swap unverified theories, and openly poach each other's lapsed members.

### 33. Bench Fee
Pitch: A repair shop game where every broken item is a physical puzzle-box submitted by another player, and you must quote a price before you're allowed to open it up. Underquote and the repair eats your week; overquote and the customer walks without a word. Submitters earn from stumping you, creating an arms race between devious breakers and diagnosticians who learn to read external clues.

### 34. Ninety Days
Pitch: A single-run roguelike: ninety days of runway to open a shop in a city whose ordinances, inspection triggers, and customer tastes are re-rolled every run and documented nowhere — you learn the rules by receiving violation notices. Death of the business is permanent and often arrives from a rule you never saw. Everyone plays the same weekly seed, so the leaderboard doubles as a frantic community effort to reverse-engineer this week's hidden city.

### 35. Endcap
Pitch: You curate the shelves of a cramped corner store where every SKU slot is contested real estate: player-suppliers cold-pitch you their products while you cold-call distributors who mostly never write back. Products live or die silently on the shelf, and the register never tells you whether the failure was price, placement, or the neighborhood. Supplier relationships persist across seasons, and burning one gets around.

### 36. Hold Music
Pitch: A game played entirely through a phone interface — dial pads, hold queues, voicemails — as you try to open a shop by cold-calling wholesalers, inspectors, contractors, and the gas company. Eighty percent of your calls go to voicemail and are never returned; occasionally a callback lands days later in real time, and you'd better pick up. Some numbers secretly connect to other real players' businesses, and figuring out who's human is half the game.

### 37. The Strip
Pitch: Twelve storefronts on one shared street, each run by a real player, and your foot traffic depends on the tenant mix — an anchor tenant lifts everyone, a vape shop next door quietly kills your salon — but the synergy formula is never shown. The loop is running your own shop while politicking over who gets the vacant unit. Street-improvement votes, joint promotions, and passive-aggressive window signage are the social core.

### 38. Inspection Day
Pitch: Health and safety inspectors are other players, each authoring a private checklist within loose official bounds, and you never see any checklist — only a pass, or a citation code with no elaboration. The loop is running your shop day-to-day while hardening it against audits whose criteria you can only infer from accumulated scar tissue. Inspector players build reputations as hardliners or pushovers, and shop owners trade contradictory folklore about what each one cares about.

### 39. Change of Hands
Pitch: An exquisite-corpse business: every real-world week, your shop rotates to a new player-owner, and you inherit the previous owners' ledgers, half-finished supplier deals, debts, and cryptic margin notes. The central skill is archaeology — deciding which inherited commitments were genius and which were traps, with no one obligated to explain themselves. An alumni chat of past owners watches your stewardship and gossips.

### 40. Till Tape
Pitch: You experience your corner store exclusively through the register tape and the cash drawer count — no shop floor view, no camera, ever. Shoplifting, weather, a hot new product, or a clerk skimming all arrive as anomalies in a stream of receipts, and your only actions are terse written instructions left for staff you never see. Guilds of tape-readers share pattern libraries, and staff behavior is seeded from other players' management styles.

### 41. Variance
Pitch: To expand, renovate, or put up a bigger sign, you must appear at Zoning Board Night — a scheduled live event where you get three minutes to plead before a rotating board of player jurors. Verdicts are final and delivered without reasons, and the board's composition is announced only at the door. The theater of it — pre-hearing lobbying, packing the room with supportive players, whisper campaigns — is the game.

### 42. The Playbook
Pitch: A franchising game where franchisor players write real operating manuals — actual documents — and franchisee players stake their savings on following one. You discover whether the playbook is brilliance or garbage only after months of compliance, and franchisors owe you no explanation for the parts that don't work. Royalty flows, franchise trees, and public blowups when a chain collapses make the economy socially combustible.

### 43. Open Up
Pitch: A five-minute daily ritual game: raise the grate, count the float, stock the case, set out the sandwich board — while the street outside changes subtly each morning. Scaffolding going up, a new parking sign, a competitor's window papered over: the game never flags these details, but they are the only warning you get of shifts that will hit your till in a week. Neighboring shops on your street belong to other players whose morning rituals you can watch for cues.

### 44. Store Hours
Pitch: Your shop is open only during real-world hours you commit to, and walk-ins arrive as push notifications you must answer live — miss one and that customer is gone forever, identity unknown. The core tension is scheduling your fictional shop around your actual life while inferring, from silence, when the neighborhood wanted you open. Customers are real players running cross-shop errands, so a street of players ends up negotiating de facto opening rhythms.

### 45. Word on the Street
Pitch: A small-business sim that is deliberately undocumented: no tooltips, no stable wiki, and core mechanics that quietly rotate every season, so the only reliable knowledge is gossip traded in the barbershop chairs and back booths of other players' shops. Information is a decaying, tradable resource — and some of it is planted by rivals. The loop is running your shop while constantly deciding whose word to buy, believe, or poison.

### 46. As-Is
Pitch: A live auction game for dead businesses: you bid against other players seeing only a photograph of the storefront and one self-reported number from the departing owner — who is a real player with every incentive to have lied. The turnaround phase is discovering what you actually bought: the loyal customer list, or the mold. Auction-floor bluffing and post-sale grudges carry across seasons.

### 47. The Regulars
Pitch: Your shop survives on exactly twelve named regulars, each with needs they never state and a social graph you can't see — when one drifts away, their whole cluster follows in silence weeks later. The loop is noticing micro-signals (shorter visits, changed orders) and tending relationships before the quiet cascade. Regulars can be courted by rival player shops, and a poached regular takes friends.

### 48. Across the Street
Pitch: A 1v1 fog-of-war rivalry: another live player runs the shop directly across from yours, and your only intelligence is what's visible through your front window — their signage, the length of their line, what's in their dumpster. Copying what you see risks copying their mistakes; their thriving look may be a bluff staged for your benefit. Matches run for a real-time week, and the street's foot traffic is a shared pool one of you will starve the other out of.

### 49. Small Fires
Pitch: Every day delivers more problems than hours — burst pipe, staff no-show, surprise pallet delivery, inspector at the door — and you must publicly drop commitments, with the dropped ones compounding into next week's fires. Relief comes only from begging neighboring shop owners (real players) for emergency help, creating favor-debts that they will absolutely call in at the worst time. Nobody tells you which fire was the one that mattered.

### 50. Curb Appeal
Pitch: You control only your shop's exterior — the sign, the window display, the sandwich board, the paint — and your storefront appears as a card in other real players' walk-by feeds as they play their own games. Taps convert to sales, but you never see who scrolled past, how many, or why they didn't stop; your only data is conversions against an invisible denominator. Streets form emergent visual arms races as neighboring players escalate their frontages against each other.
