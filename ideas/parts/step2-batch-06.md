### 126. Pallet of Fog — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Buying under irreducible information asymmetry, and learning to price the information itself (clues, planted tips) rather than the goods.
Cheats: The core transaction is a lottery ticket dressed as judgment — Storage Wars with extra steps — and appraisal-by-rattle will be datamined into weight-to-contents tables within a month.
Note: The player-set resale market keeps half the uncertainty epistemic, but the purchase side degenerates from appraisal into gambling the moment clue tables hit the wiki.

### 127. The Corner Is Yours (Until It Isn't) — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: In informal economies property rights are social relationships you continuously fund and defend, not assets you own.
Cheats: The beautification sweeps are explicitly unlearnable pure noise — punishment with no hypothesis to test — and the cart business is a cardboard prop under a turf-politics game.
Note: The alliance web is genuinely human and un-wikiable, but the design drifts toward gang-territory MMO where the lesson taught is loyalty, not entrepreneurship.

### 128. Ninety Days of Rain — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: The newsvendor problem in its natural habitat — committing inventory against a demand you can only infer from sellouts and rot, with brutal attribution ambiguity.
Cheats: Other players are invisible weather rather than counterparties — no outreach, no trust, no faces — so it trains statistical inference more than dealing with humans.
Note: Uncertainty is properly epistemic and permanently non-stationary (rival carts shift the distribution), which resists wiki-solving better than most run-based designs.

### 129. Say Nothing, Sell Everything — TEACH: 4/10
Teaches: Deals die in silence, and walking away unexplained is a move, not a malfunction — a real negotiation truth.
Cheats: It is a ranked esport minigame about one skill; with no venture, no runway, and no customer discovery, negotiation without a business is just poker with flavor text.
Note: Human opponents keep it epistemic, but expect it to degenerate into a solved timing-meta the way every bluffing ladder does.

### 130. The Bale — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Supplier trust is only learnable through purchases expensive enough to hurt, and inventory value is set by a fashion meta you do not control.
Cheats: "The A-grade stamp lies exactly often enough to keep you guessing" is a confession — designer-tuned noise cosplaying as learnable counterparty behavior.
Note: If suppliers are NPCs their deceit rates get spreadsheeted and the trust lesson dies; the player-driven fashion demand is the durable, epistemic half.

### 131. Tontine — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Credit without enforcement is pure trust calibration — vouching chains, reading people, and the greedy timing question of when to take your pot.
Cheats: It teaches informal finance, not venture-building — there is no product, customer, or market, just counterparty risk on a loop.
Note: Structurally wiki-proof because the hidden system is other humans, but vulnerable to a lemons death-spiral where rampant defection makes strangers uninvestable and circles close to friends.

### 132. Cold Calls at the Kiosk — TEACH: 9/10
Teaches: Cold outreach as it actually is — read receipts followed by eternal silence, where the skill is crafting openers and extracting signal from the specific flavor of nothing.
Cheats: The kiosk it allegedly stocks is vestigial set-dressing — this is an outreach simulator in a retail costume, and if NPC suppliers have formulaic reply triggers the wiki wins that half instantly.
Note: Against real player suppliers it cannot degenerate — shared opener templates go stale exactly like real cold email, making the meta-shift itself the lesson.

### 133. Inspector Rotation — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Operating under rules that are real, consistent, and unpublished — the App Store/Amazon/SEO experience — where every fine is a data point and the rulebook resets without notice.
Cheats: Within any single rotation the vendor channels will crowd-solve the rulebook in days, so the individual epistemic work the pitch romanticizes mostly gets outsourced to Discord.
Note: Poisoned intel is the save — it keeps community knowledge permanently suspect, the rare anti-wiki mechanic that turns the wiki against itself.

### 134. The Fixer — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: A middleman's capital is relationships that decay silently — you are never fired, the referrals just stop, and you must infer disintermediation from absence.
Cheats: Its entire moat assumes discovery is hard, but players will route around the fixer via Discord in a week, and any artificial friction added to stop that is fake scarcity.
Note: Silence is the most load-bearing in the batch here, but the game dies the moment out-of-game coordination is cheaper than an in-game introduction.

### 135. Suitcase Economy — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Information decays in transit — trading on secondhand reports about a market you cannot see, and paying for intel of unknowable freshness.
Cheats: Customs is an explicitly uninsurable pure dice roll that can vaporize a whole trip — exactly the RNG-that-teaches-nothing this genre should be ashamed of.
Note: Arbitrage spreads get centralized into community spreadsheets fast; the intel-bazaar trust game is the only part that survives the wiki.

### 136. Dawn at the Mandi — TEACH: 4/10
Teaches: Hesitation has a price and overcommitment has a worse one — decisiveness under time pressure with real capital at stake.
Cheats: Ten-minute open-outcry rounds train auction reflexes and crowd-reading twitch, not entrepreneurial judgment — a trading-floor party game with produce.
Note: The split-lot stranger alliances are the one real trust mechanic; the rest collapses into a solved bidding meta within a season.

### 137. No Stars for the Cart — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: You can never see your own reputation, only its symptoms — and everyone selling you a mirror has an incentive to warp it.
Cheats: The blindness collapses the instant a friend screenshots your score for free — the report-selling economy depends on information friction the internet does not permit.
Note: Genuinely epistemic (inferring a hidden number from queue length and credit terms), but one Discord bot away from total degeneration.

### 138. The Ledger of Aunt Rosa — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Due diligence as archaeology — testing an inherited business's undocumented assumptions against live reality, entry by entry.
Cheats: It is a finite one-shot puzzle: once your ledger is decoded the entrepreneurship evaporates, and there is no repeatable judgment loop underneath.
Note: The paired-mystery structure (players holding halves of each other's ledgers) is lovely but content-exhaustible — a mystery box, not a market.

### 139. Matatu Kings — TEACH: 3/10
Teaches: Route ownership as a socially-enforced fiction is a true observation about informal transit economics.
Cheats: In practice it is a gang-turf MMO with buses — rider revenue is admitted to be unknowable noise, so all skill expression flows to cartel politics and fleet grinding, the exact attribute-grind the brief forbids.
Note: Cartel consolidation locks out newcomers and kills the server; the entrepreneurship is a paint job on a territory-control war game.

### 140. Scrap Cartography — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Knowledge is depreciating capital, and the only validation for purchased information is walking the route yourself.
Cheats: Adverse selection guts the signature mechanic — nobody rational sells an honest map to their best routes — so the map market converges on decoys-only and buyers rationally stop buying.
Note: The core loop is gathering-grind; the epistemic layer is real, but the spawn algorithm will be datamined, leaving only the con.

### 141. Two Currencies — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: The posted rules are not the real rules — official information is systematically wrong, and calibrating HOW wrong is a skill real founders recognize.
Cheats: One community-run rate-tracker bot makes the true rate public and free, and the entire premise dies — the design has no defense against its own players' spreadsheets.
Note: Fleecing newcomers via the UI itself is bold and genuinely load-bearing, but it is also a churn machine; hyper-localizing the black rate is the only survival path.

### 142. Grandmother's Recipe Is Wrong — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Attribution agony — one variable per day against silent churn and a drifting taste meta is exactly what iterating a product with no exit interviews feels like.
Cheats: The one-change-per-day rule is an artificial experiment-hygiene guardrail real founders never get; reality lets you flail on five variables at once, and the flailing is part of the lesson.
Note: Player-driven taste drift makes the target permanently unsolvable by wiki — epistemic and non-stationary in the right way, provided signal-to-noise is tuned above slot-machine.

### 143. The Queue — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Markets grow on friction like mold — speculating on queue positions, complete with bubbles and panics, is genuine informal entrepreneurship around bureaucratic scarcity.
Cheats: Which windows actually work gets crowd-solved fast, forcing the designer into rule churn so constant that folklore becomes noise and learning becomes pointless.
Note: The player-to-player position market is the durable epistemic core; the bureaucracy behind it is a content treadmill.

### 144. Genny Row — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: The oversell temptation in one clean mechanic — invisible capacity, one blown fuse torching every customer's trust simultaneously, and a market that evaporates the week the incumbent grid fixes itself.
Cheats: Illegal-tap shrinkage is near-pure noise observable only as creeping fuel costs — an inference problem with almost no experiment available to run against it.
Note: The grid-works-all-week event is the best startup-mortality lesson in the batch: your business existing at all is a bet on someone else's continued failure.

### 145. Festival Chasers — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Committing scarce, irreversible resources (diesel, pitch fees) to hearsay, plus the reflexive trap that a crowd chasing the same rumor destroys the opportunity it chased.
Cheats: Rainouts and cancellations are unhedgeable pure RNG, and the vendor business underneath is a generic sell-stuff loop with no depth of its own.
Note: The crowd-splitting reflexivity is naturally wiki-proof — public knowledge of the "best" festival self-destructs — rarer and more valuable than the pitch seems to realize.

### 146. The Apprentice Who Sweeps — TEACH: 4/10
Teaches: Tacit knowledge transfers by watching what masters do, not what anyone says — and masters have no incentive to teach you.
Cheats: The entire structural rule ("no tutorial exists") is annihilated by the first YouTube guide; you cannot design the absence of documentation in a world where players write documentation.
Note: A negative-space mechanic, not a mechanic — everything hinges on an underlying trading game the pitch never actually describes.

### 147. Rent Is Due Friday — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Cash-flow triage — overlapping obligations, choosing who to pay late, and creditor patience as hidden state whose shape you only learn when it snaps.
Cheats: "Inconsistent" creditor patience is either humanlike hidden state (great) or designer dice deployed to defeat spreadsheeting (fake) — the pitch does not know which, and the whole teach hinges on it.
Note: Player lenders-of-last-resort at desperation rates is real predatory-credit education and structurally un-wikiable; the most direct runway teacher in the batch.

### 148. Counterfeit Alley — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Trust as capital built slowly and torched instantly, with consequences arriving on a delay (wear-over-time reveals) exactly like real reputation.
Cheats: The classic lemons collapse looms — if player counterfeiters get good enough, rational buyers exit and the market seizes, teaching the last players nothing but nihilism.
Note: Deception quality being player skill makes it permanently epistemic and wiki-proof; naming-and-shaming Discords are not degeneration, they ARE the word-of-mouth mechanic externalized.

### 149. The Barker's Cut — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Selling a service with unmeasurable attribution — clients churn without telling you what they believed, and looking effective pays exactly the same as being effective.
Cheats: If barking has no true in-engine effect on foot traffic, honest work is provably pointless and the game curdles from a lesson about attribution into a cynical con-artist sim.
Note: The learnable system is the client's belief-formation, not the product — genuinely epistemic, and uncomfortably true to the agency business.

### 150. Umbrella Men — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Punditry survivorship bias in miniature — forecast-sellers are celebrities until the storm they missed, and calibrating self-reported track records is the real skill.
Cheats: The underlying trade is a coin flip by design ("genuinely stochastic"), so the commerce layer is a casino and only the meta-market in forecasters teaches anything.
Note: Five-minute mobile rounds push it toward slot-machine dopamine; if the game ever verifies track records, the trust-calibration lesson evaporates too.
