### 376. Ring Trade — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Alpha decay — proprietary knowledge of who-really-wants-what IS the margin, and the moment your loop goes public it evaporates, which is exactly how real deal-flow edges die.
Cheats: It is opportunity arbitrage without a venture — no product, no payroll, no runway — so it trains the scout's instincts but never the builder's commitments.
Note: Uncertainty is properly epistemic (stale and bait wants are learnable through burned fingers) and player-driven loop churn resists wiki capture indefinitely.

### 377. Caravan Divergence — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Your brilliant idea is not unique — arriving into a market crashed by fifty players with the same insight is the crowded-trade lesson founders pay tuition for.
Cheats: This is EVE/Elite hauling with rumor lag bolted on, and that genre's history proves third-party price-sharing tools will solve it into a logistics spreadsheet within a month.
Note: High degeneration risk — the fog is purely infrastructural (information delay), and player tooling erases infrastructural fog faster than any patch cycle can rebuild it.

### 378. Recipe Shadow — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Forensic demand inference — customers who will never explain themselves force you to hypothesize from purchase patterns and validate with small stocking experiments, which is real B2B founder work.
Cheats: The warehouse-of-worthless-moss punishment tips from lesson into lottery when a crafter pivot is genuinely unobservable in advance — some losses teach nothing by design.
Note: Degeneration-resistant because demand is downstream of secret, perpetually reinvented player recipes — the hidden system regenerates itself instead of getting wiki-solved.

### 379. The Spread — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Adverse selection in the gut — a sudden rush of eager counterparties means you are the dumbest money in the room, and the game never tells you why.
Cheats: It teaches trading, not entrepreneurship — no product, no customer discovery, no runway, just quote management; a founder curriculum reduced to one (excellent) finance instinct.
Note: Best-in-batch load-bearing silence, wrong curriculum — mastery converges on market-making skill, not entrepreneurial judgment.

### 380. Shelf Wars — TEACH: 6/10
Teaches: Fixed-cost commitment under drifting demand — signing tomorrow's rent against yesterday's foot-traffic data is small-retail truth delivered weekly.
Cheats: With only 200 slots the real game becomes landlord cartel politics, and early incumbents will ossify the slot meta into rent-seeking that newcomers just read about on a wiki.
Note: The emergent pivot from selling goods to renting attention is the most founder-honest mechanic here; whether it survives slot-oligopoly capture is the design's entire fate.

### 381. Rot Ledger — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Cash-flow pressure made physical — spoilage is a burn rate you can smell, and dumping stock late signals a desperation rivals punish, exactly like discounting your way to death.
Cheats: Hidden and variable spoilage clocks flirt with pure RNG — if decay is not learnable by category and handling, losses stop teaching and start merely annoying.
Note: Demand from real players who owe you nothing keeps the crowd-reading lesson permanently unsolved; the dawn-auction commitment is honest runway pressure.

### 382. Handshake Futures — TEACH: 9/10
Teaches: Trust is priced, not given — collateral demands, vouch webs, and whispered reputation are the actual mechanics of doing business where no contract can save you.
Cheats: Early-game is a griefer's paradise where defaulting on strangers is nearly free, so the first hundred hours may teach nihilism before the reputation web gets dense enough to teach trust.
Note: Structural and unpatchable — counterparty character is never wiki-soluble, and emergent player escrow/court businesses mean the game grows its own institutions, the deepest lesson in the batch.

### 383. Cap Table — TEACH: 9/10
Teaches: Fundraising as it actually feels — a feed of silent scrolls, passes with no reasons, and the late discovery that your valuation is set by shareholder skittishness rather than your product.
Cheats: Feed-pitching will breed clickbait meta-templates, and investors risking game-money may fund for entertainment rather than return, softening the sting of real capital allocation.
Note: Silence is fully load-bearing (no explanation is ever owed), and post-funding governance by real humans keeps teaching after the raise — rare and exactly right.

### 384. Mintmakers — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Confidence is the asset — a currency, like a startup, is worth what strangers believe this week, and belief is manufactured, attacked, and defended.
Cheats: "Rumors you can't trace" edges into pure noise, and playing central banker is a cartoon adjacent to entrepreneurship rather than the thing itself.
Note: Likely degenerates into perpetual pump-and-dump griefing where no coin ever earns durable trust — the equilibrium teaches cynicism, not issuance discipline.

### 385. The Dumb Ledger — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: You do not know your own numbers until you build the instruments — designing price experiments to de-noise your own P&L is the founder discipline nobody has gamified.
Cheats: Players can rationally route around the fog with external shadow-spreadsheets of their own transactions, and if that exploit works the entire mechanic evaporates overnight.
Note: Superbly epistemic if the design hides transaction-level truth (remote agents, aggregated sales); if it doesn't, this is the most wiki-fragile great idea in the batch.

### 386. Allocation Day — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Booms end unannounced — spending today's scarcity to buy the relationships you will need when you are nobody again is channel wisdom most founders learn too late.
Cheats: It starts after product-market fit, skipping the zero-to-one struggle entirely — the player inherits success rather than earning it, which halves the curriculum.
Note: Making the player the rejector usefully inverts the silence lesson; scalper-wrecked street prices are epistemic feedback, not dice.

### 387. Brownout — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Capacity economics with asymmetric downside — idle hours bleed loan interest quietly while one shortage night destroys your name loudly, the exact math of every cloud provider, clinic, and kitchen.
Cheats: When spikes come from unschedulable player wars and festivals, small operators face outcomes indistinguishable from dice no matter how well they read the weather.
Note: Selling interruptible contracts to customers who swear they will never invoke them is a genuinely sophisticated trust-calibration mechanic; uncertainty is epistemic at aggregate scale.

### 388. Standard Bearers — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Network effects are invisible until they have already happened — betting a warehouse on which standard tips is platform-era judgment (VHS, USB, rail gauge) rendered honestly.
Cheats: For any single mid-sized player the tipping outcome is close to pure luck — the levers (adapters, subsidies, consortia) are real but their effect sizes may be too small to ever feel causal.
Note: Player-driven adoption resists wiki-solving, but the design must make the levers demonstrably matter or every loss reads as a lottery ticket instead of a lesson.

### 389. Built to Last — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Your installed base is your competitor — durable-goods saturation forces the invention of repeat business, the dilemma behind every upgrade cycle you have ever resented.
Cheats: It is one legible trade-off (durability vs. saturation) with a calculable optimum — the community will publish the durability curve and the dilemma dies into a wiki number.
Note: Reading used-market prices as your earliest customer-preference signal is the one genuinely epistemic mechanic; the rest is solvable optimization homework.

### 390. The Last Shoal — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Reading indirect signals early — your own falling catch-per-trip is the only sonar, and acting before the consensus panic is the entire margin.
Cheats: This is a commons-ecology parable wearing a business hat — the entrepreneurial moves (aquaculture pivot, buying distressed boats) are epilogue, not the core loop.
Note: The hidden biomass model is a fixed system: community scientists will reverse-engineer respawn math within weeks, converting tragedy into scheduled quota spreadsheets.

### 391. Honesty Box — TEACH: 4/10
Teaches: Experimentation with hopelessly confounded data — same bread, different cloth, different corner, and a bag of mixed coins that never explains itself is real A/B-testing pain.
Cheats: With real anonymous players, pay-what-you-want collapses to free-riding, the signal dies, and no founder on earth runs this model — it is a behavioral-econ toy in a market stall.
Note: Once payments approach zero the data becomes pure noise, failing the epistemic test at the population level even though the experiment loop itself is charming.

### 392. Taste — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Most launches die in total silence — seeding influencers, staging scarcity, and watching a rival's ugly thing win anyway is consumer-brand truth with the anesthesia removed.
Cheats: If fad formation is genuinely undiscoverable ("no discernible reason"), players cannot form testable beliefs — without a learnable culture-reading skill this is a slot machine with influencers.
Note: Fashion among real players never wiki-solves, making it degeneration-proof; all the risk sits on the epistemic side, none on the staleness side.

### 393. Payroll — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: The uncertainty is in people — unwritten wage norms, unmeasurable loyalty, and mid-contract ghosting are the management reality no spreadsheet game has ever touched.
Cheats: The entire design is hostage to population liquidity — with a thin playerbase there is no labor market, and "hiring real players" quietly becomes hiring the same six friends.
Note: Trust calibration here is permanently epistemic because humans never get patched, but the game only exists at scale — a structural fragility rather than a mechanical one.

### 394. Idle Iron — TEACH: 5/10
Teaches: Utilization religion — depreciation plus loan interest ticking against every idle hour builds genuine capital-intensity intuition.
Cheats: The core loop is a legible optimization problem; once rate cards and boom indicators circulate, it plays like fleet-management homework with counterparty flavor text.
Note: The real uncertainty (strangers breaking your capital, unforeseeable construction crazes) is authentic but thin — most sessions will be spreadsheet, not judgment.

### 395. Pooled — TEACH: 9/10
Teaches: Interest is not demand — fronting your own deposit against strangers' soft yeses and watching enthusiasm evaporate at payment time is the most expensive lesson in validation, delivered weekly.
Cheats: Success dumps you into allocation fights and repeat-expectation politics that can drift into community-management tedium rather than fresh entrepreneurial decisions.
Note: Unpatchable by wiki — human flakiness at the moment money is due is a permanent, self-regenerating truth, and eating the shortfall personally makes the runway pressure real.

### 396. Tariff Runners — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Regulation is a competitor who can change the rules mid-game — margins that exist only until a player-senator notices them is fintech/crypto/Uber reality made playable.
Cheats: Rules rewritten "specifically to bankrupt a trader they dislike" makes ruin feel like griefing rather than lesson, and lobbying may collapse into whoever fields the biggest guild.
Note: Epistemic-social uncertainty of the right kind (watch the politics, price the gap's half-life), but rule-changes need friction or this is arbitrary tyranny, not regulatory risk.

### 397. Depth Unknown — TEACH: 4/10
Teaches: Probing hidden demand with small test orders is honest epistemology — form a belief about invisible depth, spend a little to test it, update.
Cheats: The landing-page analogy lives in the pitch copy, not the mechanics — what is actually on screen is day-trading tape-reading, a curriculum for quants, not founders.
Note: Player-spoofed prints keep it from wiki-death, but mastery converges on market microstructure, which is the wrong lesson for this product.

### 398. On Consignment — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Your judgment is the product — curating strangers' goods while foot traffic never explains itself, and managing anxious principals who accuse you of skimming, is retail truth plus a free course in stakeholder handling.
Cheats: The shopkeeper's downside is externalized — the consignors own the dead stock — so the classic founder terror of cash sunk into inventory is structurally missing.
Note: Demand-side silence is real and epistemic; taste churn among player buyers keeps the window-allocation problem alive against wiki capture.

### 399. Downline — TEACH: 8/10
Teaches: Every incentive you write gets gamed within a week — designing comp plans against adversarial humans with unobservable effort is mechanism design as lived experience, ending in the chilling discovery of the exact week your channel becomes a pyramid.
Cheats: The MLM frame risks teaching the dark arts better than the lesson — the optimal play may simply be running the pyramid, with the ethics arriving only as flavor text.
Note: Degeneration-proof because the exploiters are real players who adapt to any plan; the uncertainty is permanently epistemic and adversarial.

### 400. On Spec — TEACH: 7/10
Teaches: Inventory risk at existential scale — eighteen weeks of sunk wages, pivotable half-built hulls, and buyers who can count your carrying cost per day teach negotiating from visible weakness.
Cheats: With maybe three serious buyers a season, a thin playerbase turns lumpy demand into no demand — the design bets everything on matchmaking density it cannot guarantee.
Note: The mid-build refit decision (war vs. whaling) is a true forced-pivot mechanic, and buyer intentions living in real players' heads keeps the timing lesson permanently unsolved.
