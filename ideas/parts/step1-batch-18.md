### 426. Cameras Off
Pitch: You pitch your company over a scarily authentic video-call client to a grid of black tiles — cameras off, names like "iPhone" and "Meeting Room 4B", and no way to know whether the decision-maker is even present. The tiles are real players attending from inside their own games, and the post-call silence never resolves into a yes, a no, or any hint of which tile actually mattered.

### 427. Everyone Says They'd Buy It
Pitch: A customer-discovery game played over a call UI where every interviewee — a real player — earns points for being pleasant and loses points for committing to anything, which reproduces the founder's oldest trap: compliments that feel like validation and predict nothing. Your craft is question design and the hard ask, because the only signals that count are costly ones — a deposit, an intro to their boss, time on their real calendar. Build whatever the interviews "validated" and the market will grade the gap between what people said and what they meant.

### 428. Reactions Only
Pitch: A Slack-sim where the buyers, investors, and partners you court never type a reply — decisions reach you exclusively as emoji reactions, and every organization has its own unwritten dialect. A thumbs-up from the wrong person is worthless, an eyes-emoji from the right one means a deal is quietly moving, and in one org the party-popper is how they say no politely. The loop is ethnography under cash pressure: mapping who matters and what their emojis actually mean from outcomes you only understand weeks later, while rival players seed misleading reaction-lore into the community's shared dictionary.

### 429. Top of Mind
Pitch: An ARG about staying remembered, played in five-minute touches across real months: your contacts are real players whose needs arise on hidden, random timetables, and the contract goes to whichever supplier happens to exist in their head that week. Every contact has an invisible decay curve and an invisible pest threshold — check in too rarely and you're forgotten, too often and you're muted forever, and no interface ever shows you either line. The skill under the luck is note-keeping, spacing, and making each tiny touch worth receiving; the luck under the skill is whose warehouse floods the week after you happened to say hello.

### 430. #general-anxiety
Pitch: You join a company Slack as the founder of a just-acquired startup, and the game is navigating channels — including the ones you can see exist but can't open — to figure out who actually decides whether your product survives integration. Real players fill the exec, EM, and cynical-veteran roles, threads reference meetings you weren't in, and your earn-out depends on decoding an org chart nobody will show you. Sessions run a real week per "quarter," with the ambient dread of watching #leadership-private light up after you post.

### 431. Call to Confirm
Pitch: Your suppliers, landlord, and biggest customer all pay and get paid by wire — and some of the emails asking you to "update bank details" are from adversary players running social-engineering crews. The core verb is verification: calling back on the number you had before, not the one in the signature; inventing shared-history challenges; deciding how much friction a legitimate counterparty will tolerate before your paranoia costs you the relationship. One wrong trust call drains the account with no undo, and the game confirms nothing — money just leaves, and you find out at reconciliation.

### 432. Gatekept
Pitch: An asymmetric duel: one side cold-calls trying to reach the executive, the other plays the receptionist who is paid per blocked call — but secretly loses everything if they block the one caller whose deal would have made their boss rich. Neither side can see the other's payoff table, so receptionists develop folk heuristics for "smells like money" while callers reverse-engineer what gets past them this season. Matches are three-minute improv phone theater, and the community meta shifts weekly like a fighting game.

### 433. Cc: Everyone
Pitch: Your company's make-or-break negotiation plays out entirely in email threads, and your only real verbs are cc, bcc, forward, reply-all, and the withheld reply. ### 434. Business Hours
Pitch: A dialer game about timing, not talking: every lead is another real player's storefront with hidden hours, moods, and time zones, and calling at the wrong moment doesn't just fail — it burns the lead permanently. ### 435. This Message Was Deleted
Pitch: Your entire supply chain runs through group chats you have to be invited into, where prices get posted at midnight and retracted by dawn, and the words "This message was deleted" hang exactly where your margin used to be. ### 436. Other (Please Specify)

### 437. Offer Extended

### 438. Exhibit A
Pitch: An email-client game with a time bomb: eighteen months from now, one deal in your company's life will end in a lawsuit, and everything in your sent folder becomes discoverable evidence read aloud in the worst possible light. ### 439. Circle Back
Pitch: An enterprise sale rendered entirely as a calendar app: the meeting that decides your startup's future gets moved, shrunk from 60 minutes to 15, made "optional," double-booked, and cancelled at 4:58pm — by a real player playing procurement, who scores points for extracting concessions per week of delay. ### 440. All Systems Operational
Pitch: You run a SaaS company through its public status page and incident channel: the product breaks in ways you don't understand yet, enterprise customers — real players — refresh the page mid-outage, and every word you publish is a trust transaction. ### 441. The Quiet Fund

### 442. Somebody's Watching
