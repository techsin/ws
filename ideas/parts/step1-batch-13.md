### 301. Ex Post Facto
Pitch: You run a perfectly legal business in a republic where the legislature passes laws retroactively: this spring's bestselling product can become last winter's crime, complete with back-dated fines. Gameplay is operating under permanent legal ambiguity — reading gazette rumors, hedging with player-sold "amnesty insurance" of dubious enforceability, and courting committee clerks (some player-run) whose docket is a state secret. Nobody, including the archivists, can produce a complete current copy of the law, and citing it too accurately is itself considered suspicious.

### 302. Indulgences, Inc.
Pitch: A carbon-credit satire set in the afterlife economy: you broker penance offsets, matching sinners who want absolution futures against monasteries (player-run) that generate certified virtue. The exchange rate between specific sins and units of penance is set by a Curia that never publishes its methodology and occasionally declares entire vintages of indulgence retroactively void, wiping out whole portfolios. Since final settlement only occurs at each client's Judgment, every product you sell is untestable within your lifetime — and marketing does the rest.

### 303. Built to Flip
Pitch: A startup parody where customers barely matter: the real market is corp-dev tourists from player-run acquirers, each hunting targets that fit secret acquisition theses, and your company's job is to look like whatever they're currently looking for. You groom metrics, stage "strategic overlap," and leak fake bidding wars, but you can't tell window-shopping from diligence, and most flirtations end in silence after you've already reshaped the entire company to please one buyer. Actually building a good business remains the most reliable path — which everyone knows, and almost no one can bring themselves to do.

### 304. Spin Cycle
Pitch: You run a genuinely functional laundromat that is also a front, keeping two ledgers: the public one inspector-players can audit and the real one that pays your debts to an NPC crime family who never explain their expectations. The tension is mechanical double-entry deception, and you never learn which specific discrepancy tipped off an auditor, only that men in gray suits are suddenly interested in your fabric softener orders. Cooperating fronts can launder through each other, meaning one sloppy partner can sink a whole network that never sees it coming.

### 305. Nice Place You Got Here
Pitch: A negotiation game conducted entirely in euphemism: the crews in your neighborhood never make explicit demands, so protection terms, prices, and threats arrive as compliments about your window display and questions about your family. You reply in the same coded register, and every misread — treating a shakedown as small talk, or a genuine compliment as a shakedown — escalates somewhere off-screen, surfacing weeks later as a fire, a discount, or a wedding invitation. Veteran players maintain contradictory phrasebooks, and the crews (other players) deliberately drift the dialect to keep every phrasebook worthless.

### 306. Spot Zoning
Pitch: The city redraws its zoning map nightly by an unexplained procedure, and every morning some businesses wake up nonconforming, condemned, or suddenly the only legal liquor vendor for six blocks. The game is anticipatory: chasing surveyor gossip, courting the plat clerk's assistant's cousin, and deciding whether to relocate at ruinous cost or gamble on "grandfather status," a doctrine no two officials describe the same way. Leaked draft maps circulate in a thriving player market, and at least some are forgeries drawn by rival shopkeepers to trigger stampedes.

### 307. Batch 12
Pitch: A cohort-based accelerator parody where twenty player-founders build tiny real products (in-game services other players actually use) while being ranked weekly on KPIs the accelerator refuses to name. Mentors give confident, mutually contradictory advice, the leaderboard reshuffles for invisible reasons, and Demo Day funding is decided by an algorithm whose criteria are a permanent mystery the community endlessly reverse-engineers. Alumni get one cryptic sentence of feedback each, which becomes tradeable lore.

### 308. Reaper, Inc.
Pitch: A gig-economy satire where you're a freelance grim reaper serving Death's dispatch app: accept collection jobs, race timers, and watch your driver rating drift for reasons support will never disclose. Deactivation comes without warning or explanation, appeals go to an inbox that answers in form letters, and the only strategy guides are player-written superstitions about what the algorithm wants. Reapers can unionize, scab, or build rival dispatch co-ops, all of which the app punishes in ways nobody can quite prove.

### 309. Dead Letter Office
Pitch: A correspondence game played over real days: your import/export business in a crumbling empire runs entirely through letters to regulators, suppliers, and partners, and every reply takes 12 hours to 3 real weeks to arrive, if it arrives. You must commit capital now based on letters that answer questions you asked a fortnight ago about a market that has since changed, and the postal system occasionally delivers other players' mail to you by mistake, which is both a scandal and an intelligence goldmine. Sessions are five minutes of agonized letter-writing; the dread is free and continuous.

### 310. Motion to Dismay
Pitch: Paperwork as combat: your company exists only as a stack of filings, and competitors attack by filing objections, injunctions, and zoning challenges against you while you counter-file, with each document resolving through an opaque clerk system that rules unpredictably and cites precedents no one can look up. The deepest players build "filing decks" the way others build MTG decks, but the meta shifts whenever the invisible clerks change disposition. Law-firm guilds sell representation to players who'd rather run their business than fight, which is exactly what the guilds are counting on.

### 311. We Regret to Inform You
Pitch: An inversion game: you run a boutique rejection bureau, the outsourced office that writes and sends rejections for every bank, landlord, and licensing board in the game world, and business is booming. The loop is triaging real applications from other players and choosing rejection tone, timing, and whether to reject at all — while your own bureau's contracts get silently dropped by clients for reasons your account manager won't share. Players on the receiving end never know your bureau exists, and reading their forum threads about "the system" while being the system is the game's blackest joke.

### 312. Focus Group of the Damned
Pitch: You're a market-research consultancy in the afterlife, interviewing the dead about products they'll never get to buy, then selling the insights to living-world clients (other players) who can't verify your methods. The dead answer truthfully but obliquely — in regrets, non-sequiturs, and grudges — so the skill is extracting real signal from unreliable oracles, and you never find out whether your delivered insight actually worked until a client silently doesn't renew. Rival consultancies can bribe your ghosts to lie to you.

### 313. Trust Exercise
Pitch: A social-deduction economy where everyone knowingly joins an openly-labeled pyramid scheme — the brochure literally says "this will collapse" — and the entire game is reading other players to time your exit before the cascade. Uncertainty is purely human: the math is public but the moment of collective panic isn't, and the whales quietly recruiting hardest are often the ones halfway out the door. Between rounds, survivors and the wiped-out share a lobby, which is where the real psychology gets played.

### 314. Laurels
Pitch: An awards-industrial-complex satire where trophies are financial instruments: "Best Emerging Disruptor" can be pledged as loan collateral, and lenders (player-run) haircut each prize according to the issuing gala's floating credibility. Anyone can found an award body, so the deep game is minting prestige — running ceremonies selective enough to appreciate but generous enough to sell tables — while whisper campaigns can crater a trophy's book value overnight. Businesses that ignore the circuit save a fortune and become unbankable; that's the joke, and it isn't one.

### 315. Past Due
Pitch: You run a collections agency for Hell's receivables, buying portfolios of soul-debt at blind auction — bundled, unverifiable IOUs whose signatories may be dead, reincarnated, or never have existed at all. The craft is triage: tracing debtors from smudged infernal paperwork, knocking on living doors to collect on contracts nobody remembers signing, and learning which portfolio sellers (other players) salt their bundles with fabricated notes. Head office never discloses how it prices your remittances, and top collectors are "promoted" into servicing portfolios of debts owed by other collection agencies.

### 316. Knock Knock
Pitch: A door-to-door roguelike set in Limbo's endless suburbs, where you sell afterlife insurance along procedurally-generated streets and most doors simply never open no matter how long you stand there — the game deliberately refuses to distinguish "nobody home" from "watching you through the peephole." Each answered door is a tiny improvised dialogue puzzle with permadeath stakes for the day's commission, and route intel (which streets answered yesterday) is the scarce tradeable resource between player salesfolk. Top sellers' routes get farmed out, ruined, and blamed on them.

### 317. The 41st Floor
Pitch: You are the CEO of a company you are never allowed to see: gameplay is reading quarterly reports of dubious accuracy, memos from middle managers with agendas, and anonymous tips, then issuing directives into the fog and waiting a quarter to learn what they collided with. The company is partially simulated and partially staffed by other players who summarize, distort, and bury information for their own advancement, so your uncertainty is literally other people's careers. Shareholder players can oust you based on numbers you both suspect are fiction.

### 318. Feed the Code
Pitch: A bureaucratic tamagotchi: your business comes with a Regulatory Code, a living creature that must be fed completed paperwork daily, grows a new organ (and a new appetite) every time anyone on the server files a complaint, and screams at frequencies that attract auditors when hungry. No manual exists; players collectively maintain a wiki of the creature's observed diets that is perpetually out of date because the creature evolves in response to being understood. Neighboring businesses can gift each other paperwork, or slip yours a form it's allergic to.

### 319. Post-Mortem
Pitch: A reverse-chronology narrative game that opens at your startup's bankruptcy auction and plays backwards toward founding day, letting you revisit one decision per scene — but changing a decision re-derives everything downstream (which you've already seen) through an opaque causal engine that rarely does what you hope. The dark joke is mechanical hindsight bias: even knowing the ending, you can't reliably save the company, because the fatal cause is never the scene you'd have bet on. Other players' failed companies enter the public archive, and playing someone else's collapse backwards is the multiplayer mode.

### 320. Five Stars, Or Else
Pitch: A mob-adjacent economy layered on top of other players' real in-game shops: you run a "reputation management consultancy" that is transparently a review-protection racket, selling five-star floods and withholding one-star artillery. The opacity is social — shop owners never know if a review wave is organic, a rival's hit, or your sales pitch arriving early — and the platform's review-fraud detection bans according to rules it won't publish, so even the racket operates under a capricious god. Rackets can war over territory, with innocent shopfronts as the battlefield.

### 321. The Stamp
Pitch: On each server there exists exactly one physical Stamp of Commercial Approval, and no business may legally open without its mark; the Stamp is an object in the world that players hold, rent, hide, steal, and assassinate over. All entrepreneurial play — finding who has it, what they want, whether their calendar of promises is already three years deep — happens through rumor and negotiation, since the game provides no tracker for the Stamp's location. Every few months the Bureau announces the Stamp may have been counterfeit all along, invalidating an unknown fraction of existing licenses.

### 322. Pending
Pitch: A betting-parlor game built on bureaucratic silence: players submit real applications (loans, permits, appeals) into the game's glacial ministries, and everyone else wagers on whether and when each one will get a response, like horse racing for paperwork. Applicants can share their filing details to attract favorable odds or conceal them to bet on themselves, and the ministries' behavior shifts with unannounced internal reorganizations that gamblers detect only through odds anomalies. The house is the Ministry itself, and it also does not answer letters.

### 323. No-Show
Pitch: You run a genuinely functioning construction firm whose payroll is padded with no-show employees you were never explicitly asked to hire — names simply appear on the roster after a nod from someone whose surname you don't say aloud. The game is delivering real buildings while carrying phantom labor: making bids pencil out, surviving headcount audits, and never learning which no-shows are protected, which are freeloaders exploiting the fear, and which one is the union inspector's nephew. Firing the wrong name is instant catastrophe; firing the right one earns quiet new contracts, and there is no way to know in advance which is which.

### 324. Going Up
Pitch: A proximity-voice MMO set in an infinite skyscraper where every floor is a different market and the only place you can pitch investors is inside the elevators — your pitch lasts exactly as long as the shared ride does, and the investor chooses the floor. Rejection is architectural: they simply step out, doors close, and you'll likely never share a car with them again; the elite play is engineering "coincidental" long rides to the penthouse. Elevator operators are player-run gatekeepers who can be tipped, and everyone pretends that isn't what's happening.

### 325. Audit Siege
Pitch: A tower-defense comedy where the attackers are auditors tunneling into your business through its own records, and your defenses are built from paperwork — walls of receipts, moats of amended filings, decoy subsidiaries that route investigators into infinite loops. You can't see which of your past filings spawned the siege or what the auditors already know; their pathing follows your actual bookkeeping history, so sloppy early-game records become late-game breach points. In co-op, allied businesses share defensive structures, but a settlement deal lets any ally turn state's witness and open a gate.
