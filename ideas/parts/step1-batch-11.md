### 251. Sight Unseen
Pitch: A courthouse-steps auction game where you bid live against other players on foreclosed houses you legally cannot enter first — all you get is a curb photo, a tax record, and whatever rumors the chat coughs up. The uncertainty is total: mold, squatters, a gutted interior, or a pristine grandma house are all behind the same front door, and your bankroll only survives a few bad guesses. Veteran players sell "drive-by reports" of dubious accuracy to newer ones, creating a gray-market information economy.

### 252. Test Pit
Pitch: A development game where the ground itself is the hidden opponent: every parcel conceals procedurally generated strata — clay lenses, high water tables, buried fuel tanks, an unrecorded creek — and the only way to learn anything is to buy point-samples that are true but wildly incomplete. Borings come from player-run drilling outfits of varying rigor (and one or two quietly selling recycled logs), so your core decision is how much information to pay for before committing to a foundation you can't unwind, with the real dirt bill arriving mid-pour when walking away is no longer an option. Veterans' only durable edge is a private mental map of which neighborhoods hide what, assembled entirely from expensive surprises.

### 253. Lowball
Pitch: A sealed-bid contracting game: each week a batch of construction jobs posts, everyone submits bids blind, and losers get only the word "declined" — never the winning number, never the reason. The core skill is inferring the invisible market from your own scar tissue, plus a post-award rumor bazaar where players trade (and fabricate) intel about what jobs really went for. Win too cheap and the job's hidden scope bleeds you dry mid-build.

### 254. Yellow Letters
Pitch: A slow-burn mobile game about off-market deal hunting: you write and mail letter campaigns to thousands of procedurally generated (and some real-player-owned) property owners, and 97% of them produce absolute silence — not even a bounce. The loop is tuning lists, copy, and timing against a response curve you can never see directly, with the occasional angry callback or golden lead arriving as a real push notification days later. Players can sell each other "lead lists" of unknown freshness.

### 255. Anchor
Pitch: You run a co-working space where revenue depends less on desks sold than on the invisible relationship graph between your tenant players — who collaborates, who feuds, who's about to poach whose employees and leave together. You can only sense the graph indirectly (coffee machine chatter, booking patterns, who lingers), and your moves are spatial: seating charts, events, kicking out a toxic anchor tenant on a hunch. Churn arrives as a terse "we're not renewing," never an explanation.

### 256. Locker Ghosts
Pitch: A storage-unit empire game where the units you auction off contain the actual abandoned inventories of other players who went bankrupt in-game — their failed flips, unsold merch, and half-finished projects. Bidding happens through a five-second door-crack peek, and what you win might be treasure, liability (disposal fees), or a story about someone else's collapse. Former owners can anonymously watch their old locker get sold, and occasionally buy their own past back.

### 257. Right of Way
Pitch: The state has secretly drawn a highway alignment through the region and will confirm nothing for eighteen in-game months — but survey crews, soil rigs, and quiet title searches leave faint traces on the shared map for anyone paying attention. You option farmland and dying strip parcels purely on inference from those noisy signals, racing rival speculators who watch the same crews, knowing condemnation pays only flat appraisal value so the real money sits in the parcels just outside the taking. Players can hire decoy crews to plant false signals, and the route can genuinely move at the last minute for political reasons nobody ever sees — turning some perfectly reasoned portfolios into worthless dirt.

### 258. Net-90
Pitch: You're a subcontractor in a shared construction economy where general contractors (other players) pay net-90, late, short, or never, and your whole game is managing float: payroll hits Friday whether or not that invoice cleared. Weapons include mechanic's liens, factoring your receivables at a haircut, and a whisper network scoring which GCs actually pay. Tone is tense kitchen-table drama; a session is one brutal quarter.

### 259. Assemblage
Pitch: A social-deduction inversion: you're secretly assembling six adjacent parcels owned by six different real players for a tower deal, and the moment anyone realizes an assembly is happening, holdout prices go vertical. You buy through shell identities, stagger timing, and plant decoy interest in other blocks, while landowner players actively hunt for patterns in who's buying what. One leak — or one paranoid seller — and your capital is stranded in five useless lots.

### 260. Track Record
Pitch: A syndication game where you raise real-player money for property deals: your public track record is the only stat that exists, LPs see almost nothing inside your current deal, and every raise is a trust exercise conducted in pitch meetings and update letters you actually write. Deals fail for reasons beyond your control, and the game never tells your investors whether you were unlucky or lying — they decide, and their verdict follows you. Blow up twice and watch your inbox go silent forever.

### 261. Main Street
Pitch: A persistent MMO city where every storefront is operated by a real player-entrepreneur, and you play the landlord curating a block's tenant mix like a deck: the ramen player drives traffic to the bookstore player, the vape shop kills the daycare. Your rent roll depends on cross-tenant synergies nobody can measure directly, and tenants leave without warning when their own businesses fail. Rival landlords poach your best tenants with quiet offers you never see.

### 262. The Walkthrough
Pitch: You get fifteen real-time first-person minutes inside a procedurally generated distressed house — flashlight, notepad, a knuckle to tap walls — then you must submit a binding rehab estimate and live with it through the entire renovation, where everything you missed becomes your loss. Water stains, DIY wiring, and fresh paint over trouble are all learnable tells, but each house hides at least one thing no walkthrough could catch. Weekly houses are identical for everyone, and the leaderboard shows the terrifying spread of what different players estimated.

### 263. Leave a Message
Pitch: A game played almost entirely through asynchronous voicemail: you record 30-second pitches to property owners, lenders, and city officials (a mix of bots and real players), and your inbox is mostly nothing — silence stretching for real-world days. The craft is in the voice itself: tone, brevity, the callback hook, and learning which of your hundred messages somehow landed. When a real human calls back live, it's a heart-pounding event. Genuinely weird structural core: audio-only, no screens beyond a call log.

### 264. Stamped
Pitch: A deck-builder where your cards are permit forms, engineer letters, fee waivers, and political favors, and each submission is played into a black-box municipal validator that returns only APPROVED or DENIED — no reasons, ever. The metagame is collective inference: players diff their winning and losing hands on community boards to reconstruct the hidden rule engine, which the developers silently patch every few weeks like a real code update nobody announced. It's Slay the Spire where the enemy is a filing window.

### 265. Key Turn
Pitch: A short-term-rental arbitrage game: you lease units from player landlords, furnish and list them into a live marketplace where the guests are other real players choosing where their in-game avatars stay. The listing algorithm's ranking factors are real, opaque, and mutated without notice — one day your photos matter, the next it's response time — so top hosts are the ones running constant experiments. A landlord canceling your lease can vaporize your whole portfolio overnight.

### 266. Points and Fees
Pitch: You play the hard-money lender, and the borrowers pitching you flip deals are real players who are allowed — encouraged — to shade the truth about their rehab budgets and experience. Your loop is underwriting humans: reading their numbers, their message cadence, their excuses, then pricing risk with points and setting draw schedules that limit the damage when they lie. Defaults hand you half-finished houses in markets you never wanted, and the best lenders develop reputations borrowers strategize around.

### 267. Lot 47
Pitch: You own surface parking lots — the most boring asset alive — in a city where demand spikes are driven by events you only hear about through an in-game rumor feed of player chatter that is sometimes wrong and sometimes planted by the lot owner across the street. The mechanic is pure dynamic pricing under misinformation: set rates too high on a dead night, or too low when a stadium secretly booked a show. Quick mobile sessions; sabotage-by-rumor is a legal and beloved strategy.

### 268. Holdout
Pitch: An asymmetric 1-vs-many negotiation game: one player is the homeowner sitting on the last parcel a developer consortium (three to five players) needs, and the consortium's budget, deadline, and desperation are all hidden from the holdout. Developers deploy escalating tactics — lowballs, silence, front companies, sudden "final offers" that aren't — while the holdout reads tells and gambles on how long to wait, knowing the project might genuinely walk away. Every match ends in a signing or a permanently soured deal, and both feel like poker.

### 269. Ten Years a Week
Pitch: A market-timing game where the world runs a full real-estate decade every real-world week: rates, credit availability, and demand move on invisible macro-cycles that are never announced, only felt through deal flow drying up or bidding wars igniting. You build positions with leverage that feels genius on Tuesday and lethal by Thursday, and the players who survive are the ones who learned to smell a top. The whole server shares one cycle, so crashes are communal events with forums full of grief and told-you-sos.

### 270. Unit 4B
Pitch: A property-management dark comedy where your tenants are real players in an adversarial role, filing maintenance tickets that range from legitimate emergencies to elaborate scams, and your repair budget covers maybe a third of them. The mechanic is triage under scarcity: every ignored ticket risks a scathing review, a rent strike, or an actual burst pipe, and you never know which complaints were real until it's too late. Tenant players win by extracting value; you win by keeping the building standing and 80% full.

### 271. Punch List
Pitch: A closing-week speedrun: your building is 98% done, and the punch list regenerates as you work — every fixed item spawns follow-ons based on a hidden inspector persona (the corrosion obsessive, the egress hawk, the guy who measures handrails) that changes each run and is never named. You infer who you're dealing with from which fixes stick and which get re-flagged without comment, racing a certificate-of-occupancy deadline that your lender's interest clock makes existential. Co-op mode gives each teammate a different trade and one shared, contradictory list.

### 272. Attorney Review
Pitch: You're a broker who owns nothing: your whole game is matching player landlords with player tenants and shepherding fragile deals through a pipeline where they die silently — a deal simply stops responding in "attorney review" and no one ever tells you why. Income is pure commission, so months of work can evaporate in a week, and your only durable asset is a relationship graph you maintain through actual conversations. The best brokers become the market's memory: they know who ghosts, who closes, and who's secretly desperate.

### 273. The Tower
Pitch: A server-wide megaproject where thousands of players co-fund a single skyscraper floor by floor, each owning fractional stakes with real governance votes — and your unit's value depends brutally on what neighbors build (a player-run casino below your medical offices, say). Uncertainty lives in the politics: funding rounds stall, factions form, the elevator consortium holds everyone hostage. It's a construction game where the hard part is four thousand co-owners, and the tower's final shape is a fossil record of their fights.

### 274. Chain of Title
Pitch: A detective game about paperwork: you run a title-insurance shop, digging through generated deed chains, handwritten easements, forged signatures, and a 1962 lien someone never released — then you insure the title and eat the loss for anything you missed. Claims surface weeks later, long after you spent the premium, and each miss teaches you a new species of defect the tutorial never mentioned. Players can farm out searches to cheaper player-run shops, importing their risk appetite along with their work product.

### 275. Centennial
Pitch: You play the building — a stubborn brick structure experiencing a century at a decade per session — and your only agency is your bones: layouts, quirks, what you make easy or hard for the humans inside. Entrepreneur players wash through you across eras (garment factory, flophouse, dot-com loft, ghost kitchen), succeeding or failing partly because of choices you made forty simulated years ago, and you feel every vacancy as literal decay. The weirdness is the inverted lens: you experience entrepreneurship as the patient, silent infrastructure that outlives everyone who ever pitched inside you.
