# Step 1 — Brainstorm: 500 Business Game Ideas

Goal: raw material for a fun game that teaches TRUE entrepreneurship — radical uncertainty, unknown unknowns, cold outreach met with silence, unexplained rejection, opaque systems, luck/timing, cash pressure, pivots — with an MMO-ish social layer and no stat-sheet grinding.

Generated in 20 themed batches of 25 (lenses: modern startups, brick-and-mortar, historical trade, sci-fi, fantasy, street hustle, creator economy, B2B sales, logistics, food, real estate, finance, satire, negotiation/social-deduction, roguelike structures, player-driven MMO economies, subverted idle games, narrative interfaces, real-time/location, experimental mechanics). 94 near-duplicates were flagged and replaced after a cross-batch scan.

### 1. Reply-All Ghosts
Pitch: The entire game is a fake email client: you research leads, craft cold outreach, hit send, and then live with the silence — no fail state, no rejection screen, just an open-tracking pixel that fires ambiguously or not at all. Deliverability, subject-line fatigue, and burned domains are hidden systems you can only infer from patterns across hundreds of sends. Other players' founder-personas are among your recipients, and a gray market exists where players sell each other lead lists of dubious freshness.

### 2. The Dashboard Lies
Pitch: Your product's true product-market fit is a hidden variable you never see; you only get noisy, sampled, occasionally broken analytics dashboards, and every feature you ship perturbs the real number invisibly. The core loop is spending scarce cash on either better instrumentation or actual product work, then making bets on data you half-trust. A shared industry-benchmark report is aggregated from real players' submitted metrics — and players can (and do) inflate theirs.

### 3. Ghost Round
Pitch: An asymmetric MMO where founders pitch and a separate population of players runs VC funds with their own portfolio-construction game whose incentives founders can't see — passing on you silently is often the VC player's optimal move, so ghosting emerges from real strategy rather than a script. Founders piece together fund theses from crumbs: who they funded, what they liked at demo day, secondhand gossip in the game's forum. Reputation flows both ways, and founder players keep a whisper network on which funds actually respond.

### 4. Runway (a fundraise roguelike)
Pitch: A deck-building roguelike where your cards are pitch slides, traction claims, and warm anecdotes, and each run is one fundraise across a gauntlet of partner meetings before the money clock hits zero. Each investor boss has a hidden thesis drawn from a live meta that quietly shifts based on what the whole playerbase has been pitching lately — last month's winning deck is this month's pattern-matched pass. Between runs, players publish (or sell) their read on the current meta, which may already be stale.

### 5. Cap Table
Pitch: A tense one-on-one negotiation duel where a founder and an investor construct a term sheet clause by clause — valuation, liquidation preference, board seats, pro rata — like a chess opening with money. The founder can't see the investor's fund vintage and reserve pressure; the investor can't see the founder's real pipeline of competing offers, and both may be bluffing about time pressure. Ranked ladder play, with famous historically brutal term sheets as puzzle scenarios.

### 6. Churnyard
Pitch: You run a subscription tool inside a persistent world where your users are real players who genuinely rely on it for their own in-game businesses — and who can silently cancel at any moment. The loop is shipping features, reading cohort curves, and running exit surveys that almost nobody answers. The horror is that churn arrives with no explanation, and win-back campaigns land in the same void your cold emails do.

### 7. API Wars
Pitch: A dev-tools MMO where your product is an API and revenue only exists when other players actually wire it into their own in-game products — you see error rates and usage graphs, never the reason an integration was abandoned. Shipping a breaking change ripples through the whole dependent economy and can nuke your reputation overnight. Endgame is platform politics: deprecating rivals, courting the biggest integrator guilds, and surviving the day a whale drops you without a word.

### 8. Cold Start
Pitch: A two-sided marketplace sim about the chicken-and-egg problem: seed drivers or riders, sellers or buyers, in a living city where every subsidy and fake-it-till-liquidity trick has an elasticity you can only discover by burning money on it. The map never tells you why a neighborhood didn't ignite. Rival marketplaces are other players who can poach your painstakingly recruited supply side with a better take rate.

### 9. Standup
Pitch: A daily-cadence game (Wordle energy) where you make exactly one founder decision per real day and consequences surface days later, making attribution genuinely foggy — did Tuesday's pricing change cause Friday's dip, or was it the market? Sessions are five minutes; companies live or die over real-world months. Players share their "Day 47: died to a payroll miss" grids, and you can run your company alongside friends' ghost companies facing the identical hidden market.

### 10. Post-Mortem
Pitch: A roguelite where your startup will almost certainly die, but each death forensically reveals one verified true fact about the hidden market model ("mid-market buyers never self-serve"), which gets written into a codex shared by the entire playerbase. The twist: the simulated economy drifts over real weeks, so codex entries silently rot, and the community must constantly re-test its own received wisdom. Progress is epistemic, not stat-based — you get smarter, not stronger.

### 11. The Feed
Pitch: Every player sells apps into a store ranked by an actual black-box recommender model whose weights are secret and periodically retrained on the whole playerbase's behavior — so any discovered growth hack destroys itself as players pile in. The loop is shipping variants, watching rank twitch, and deciding whether a spike was your change or the algorithm's mood. Guilds form around competing folk theories of the algorithm, most of which are wrong.

### 12. Voicemail
Pitch: A voice-interface game played like a phone: you cold-call into simulated enterprises, talking your way past LLM-driven receptionists, procurement gatekeepers, and the eternal "she's in a meeting," with most calls dead-ending in voicemail purgatory you never hear back from. Org charts are hidden; the champion who loves you may have no budget, and you find out three weeks of calls later. Players trade hand-annotated org-chart intel on target accounts in a shared map that's part wiki, part rumor mill.

### 13. Burn
Pitch: A survival-horror take on cash flow: your office at night is lit only by your remaining runway, payroll day stalks the calendar like a recurring monster, and choosing which invoice to pay first is the combat system. Revenue arrives late, disputed, or not at all, and you can't see which. Two-player co-op casts cofounders with split authority over the bank account, which is where the real horror lives.

### 14. Term Sheet Poker
Pitch: A fast party/tabletop-style digital game where founders and investors sit at one table betting with hidden information: founders' traction cards may be real or vapor, and investors bet via term sheet structures where liquidation preferences are the kicker that reveals who actually believed. Bluffing your metrics is legal; getting caught craters your reputation for future hands. Best played with voice chat and four friends who will never trust each other again.

### 15. Fake Door
Pitch: You never build a product — the whole game is landing pages, ad micro-budgets, and waitlists, hunting for demand signals in conversion noise where the truth shifts weekly. Validated demand is itself the commodity: you sell proven concepts on an in-game exchange to builder-type players who bear the execution risk. The dread mechanic is that a great signal can simply evaporate between validation and sale, and no one will tell you why.

### 16. Null Result
Pitch: A puzzle game set in a world that never gives negative feedback: every action either visibly succeeds or produces absolutely nothing, and the game is constructing experiments — control groups, staggered launches, channel isolation — to distinguish "no" from "not yet" from "wrong audience." Levels are markets; the solution is a valid inference, not a product. Players publish their experimental designs, and elegant ones get reused by the community like speedrun routes.

### 17. Warm Intro
Pitch: An MMO where cold access to capital literally does not exist as a verb — you can only reach investors through chains of real-player introductions, and every introducer stakes reputation that burns if you flame out in the meeting. The social graph is entirely player-built, so gatekeeping, favor-trading, and kingmaker nodes emerge naturally. New players start outside the graph entirely and must earn their first edge, which is the game's brutal thesis statement.

### 18. Ticket Queue
Pitch: You experience your entire company through a single window: the support inbox. Product quality, market shifts, a competitor's launch, a billing bug — everything must be inferred from the stream of tickets, some of which are exaggerated, confused, or planted by rival players running sabotage campaigns. The loop is triage-as-detective-work: which three tickets this hour are the real signal about what's killing you?

### 19. Soft Landing
Pitch: You never found a company in this game — you inherit one that's already dying, procedurally generated with nine months of runway, a demoralized team, and books full of surprises, and the goal is any exit that isn't zero: acquihire, fire-sale, miracle turnaround. Corp-dev counterparties are other players with hidden mandates who go quiet for weeks mid-diligence because their internal politics shifted. A pure endgame chess problem where dignity is the score.

### 20. Replumb
Pitch: A Factorio-style machine game where your startup is literal live plumbing — leads flow in pipes, features are pumps, revenue drips out the far end — and a pivot means re-plumbing the machine while it's running and cash pressure is dropping. Market demand is a fluid whose composition you can only sample crudely at a few gauges. Players can publish blueprints of their machines, but a blueprint that printed money in last quarter's demand mix quietly fails in this one's.

### 21. Cofounder
Pitch: You're matched with a real stranger and must split equity, choose vesting, and divide control before you know their skill, honesty, or staying power — then run a company together for real weeks. The game engineers asymmetric temptations: solo acquisition offers, secret side deals, the option to just quietly stop showing up. It's a prisoner's dilemma stretched across a month, and the postmortem conversations are the real content.

### 22. Compliance
Pitch: You're a fintech founder and the regulator is an undocumented parser: submit filings, receive cryptic rejection codes, or — worse — ninety days of silence that might mean approval pending or application lost. The community collectively reverse-engineers the rule grammar like a TIS-100 forum, but the rules get silently patched, invalidating hard-won folklore. Competing players must decide whether to share regulatory discoveries or weaponize a rival's non-compliance by reporting them.

### 23. Out of Office
Pitch: A notification-native game that lives in your phone's lock screen: the sim runs in true real time, and your company contacts you via push notifications styled as Slack DMs — an engineer threatening to quit, a customer escalating, an investor finally replying at 11pm. You answer with short replies within a time window or the situation resolves without you, for better or worse. Friends' companies exist in the same world, so their crises sometimes ping you for help, mid-dinner.

### 24. Board Deck
Pitch: An information-control game where the sim mostly runs itself and your real gameplay is deciding, once a month, which metrics to show your board and how to frame them — while an invisible "narrative debt" meter compounds every time reality and your story diverge. The board (a mix of AI directors and real investor-players) controls follow-on funding and can fire you. Truth is always an available move; the game is about discovering how expensive it becomes over time.

### 25. Fork Threat
Pitch: An open-source maintainer game where your repo accrues stars, issues, and entitled users from a mix of simulated devs and real players, and the central tension is monetizing without triggering the community — because other players can genuinely fork your project and take your contributors with them. The loop is triage, licensing gambits, and shipping a paid cloud tier while reading the community's mood from noisy proxies. Nobody announces they're leaving; your download graph just quietly bends.

### 26. Dead Corner
Pitch: You scout storefronts on a persistent shared city map where foot traffic is generated by other players' avatars actually running their errands, then sign a lease and find out what the corner is really worth. Traffic data before signing is rumor-grade, corners die for reasons the game never states (a bus route quietly rerouted, a rival opened two blocks over), and your only feedback is the till. Rival players bid on the same leases, and vacated corners carry visible ghost signage of everyone who failed there before you.

### 27. Form 27-B
Pitch: A bureaucratic dungeon crawler where opening your shop requires navigating the Permit Office, a literal maze of counters, waiting rooms, and forms that reference other forms. Requirements change mid-application, rejections arrive as bare stamp codes with no explanation, and the clerks are other players moonlighting for in-game income with their own quotas and moods. Bribing, befriending, or simply outlasting a player-clerk becomes its own social metagame.

### 28. Fade
Pitch: You run a barbershop where cutting hair is a tactile skill mini-game, but the real game is regulars: actual players book chairs, sit through your cut, and either come back or silently never do. No-shows give no notice, word-of-mouth reputation spreads through channels you cannot read, and a dead Tuesday tells you nothing about why. Your Discord-style waiting room chat is where customers gossip about you, sometimes within earshot.

### 29. Quarters
Pitch: A laundromat game where cash flow is literal: coin trays fill in real time, machines depreciate and jam, and rent day demands you physically consolidate enough quarters to pay. The uncertainty lives in breakdowns you can't predict, a coin-op distributor who stops answering, and demand swings nobody explains. Scale up into a multi-location route and hire other players as collection runners who may or may not skim.

### 30. The Landlord's Table
Pitch: An asymmetric negotiation game where one player owns a strip of storefronts and everyone else wants a lease; the landlord holds hidden competing offers and silence is a legal move — your proposal can simply expire unanswered. Tenants never learn why they lost a space or what the winning terms were. Long-term, landlords develop reputations in player-run forums that may themselves be planted misinformation.

### 31. Footfall Futures
Pitch: Before committing to a location, you can buy traffic reports — but every report is authored and sold by another player acting as a data broker, with no accuracy guarantee. Ground truth only reveals itself after you've signed a twelve-month lease, at which point the broker may have vanished or rebranded. Broker reputation, fraud, and revenge reviews form the emergent social economy.

### 32. Ghosted Gym
Pitch: You run a gym where members never quit loudly — they just stop showing up, and the game refuses to tell you why. Your only tool is experimentation: change the music, the hours, the pricing, the smell, and read noisy, confounded retention curves like tea leaves. Leagues of gym-owner players compare churn graphs, swap unverified theories, and openly poach each other's lapsed members.

### 33. Bench Fee
Pitch: A repair shop game where every broken item is a physical puzzle-box submitted by another player, and you must quote a price before you're allowed to open it up. Underquote and the repair eats your week; overquote and the customer walks without a word. Submitters earn from stumping you, creating an arms race between devious breakers and diagnosticians who learn to read external clues.

### 34. Ninety Days
Pitch: A single-run roguelike: ninety days of runway to open a shop in a city whose ordinances, inspection triggers, and customer tastes are re-rolled every run and documented nowhere — you learn the rules by receiving violation notices. Death of the business is permanent and often arrives from a rule you never saw. Everyone plays the same weekly seed, so the leaderboard doubles as a frantic community effort to reverse-engineer this week's hidden city.

### 35. Endcap
Pitch: You curate the shelves of a cramped corner store where every SKU slot is contested real estate: player-suppliers cold-pitch you their products while you cold-call distributors who mostly never write back. Products live or die silently on the shelf, and the register never tells you whether the failure was price, placement, or the neighborhood. Supplier relationships persist across seasons, and burning one gets around.

### 36. Hold Music
Pitch: A game played entirely through a phone interface — dial pads, hold queues, voicemails — as you try to open a shop by cold-calling wholesalers, inspectors, contractors, and the gas company. Eighty percent of your calls go to voicemail and are never returned; occasionally a callback lands days later in real time, and you'd better pick up. Some numbers secretly connect to other real players' businesses, and figuring out who's human is half the game.

### 37. The Strip
Pitch: Twelve storefronts on one shared street, each run by a real player, and your foot traffic depends on the tenant mix — an anchor tenant lifts everyone, a vape shop next door quietly kills your salon — but the synergy formula is never shown. The loop is running your own shop while politicking over who gets the vacant unit. Street-improvement votes, joint promotions, and passive-aggressive window signage are the social core.

### 38. Inspection Day
Pitch: Health and safety inspectors are other players, each authoring a private checklist within loose official bounds, and you never see any checklist — only a pass, or a citation code with no elaboration. The loop is running your shop day-to-day while hardening it against audits whose criteria you can only infer from accumulated scar tissue. Inspector players build reputations as hardliners or pushovers, and shop owners trade contradictory folklore about what each one cares about.

### 39. Change of Hands
Pitch: An exquisite-corpse business: every real-world week, your shop rotates to a new player-owner, and you inherit the previous owners' ledgers, half-finished supplier deals, debts, and cryptic margin notes. The central skill is archaeology — deciding which inherited commitments were genius and which were traps, with no one obligated to explain themselves. An alumni chat of past owners watches your stewardship and gossips.

### 40. Till Tape
Pitch: You experience your corner store exclusively through the register tape and the cash drawer count — no shop floor view, no camera, ever. Shoplifting, weather, a hot new product, or a clerk skimming all arrive as anomalies in a stream of receipts, and your only actions are terse written instructions left for staff you never see. Guilds of tape-readers share pattern libraries, and staff behavior is seeded from other players' management styles.

### 41. Variance
Pitch: To expand, renovate, or put up a bigger sign, you must appear at Zoning Board Night — a scheduled live event where you get three minutes to plead before a rotating board of player jurors. Verdicts are final and delivered without reasons, and the board's composition is announced only at the door. The theater of it — pre-hearing lobbying, packing the room with supportive players, whisper campaigns — is the game.

### 42. The Playbook
Pitch: A franchising game where franchisor players write real operating manuals — actual documents — and franchisee players stake their savings on following one. You discover whether the playbook is brilliance or garbage only after months of compliance, and franchisors owe you no explanation for the parts that don't work. Royalty flows, franchise trees, and public blowups when a chain collapses make the economy socially combustible.

### 43. Open Up
Pitch: A five-minute daily ritual game: raise the grate, count the float, stock the case, set out the sandwich board — while the street outside changes subtly each morning. Scaffolding going up, a new parking sign, a competitor's window papered over: the game never flags these details, but they are the only warning you get of shifts that will hit your till in a week. Neighboring shops on your street belong to other players whose morning rituals you can watch for cues.

### 44. Store Hours
Pitch: Your shop is open only during real-world hours you commit to, and walk-ins arrive as push notifications you must answer live — miss one and that customer is gone forever, identity unknown. The core tension is scheduling your fictional shop around your actual life while inferring, from silence, when the neighborhood wanted you open. Customers are real players running cross-shop errands, so a street of players ends up negotiating de facto opening rhythms.

### 45. Word on the Street
Pitch: A small-business sim that is deliberately undocumented: no tooltips, no stable wiki, and core mechanics that quietly rotate every season, so the only reliable knowledge is gossip traded in the barbershop chairs and back booths of other players' shops. Information is a decaying, tradable resource — and some of it is planted by rivals. The loop is running your shop while constantly deciding whose word to buy, believe, or poison.

### 46. As-Is
Pitch: A live auction game for dead businesses: you bid against other players seeing only a photograph of the storefront and one self-reported number from the departing owner — who is a real player with every incentive to have lied. The turnaround phase is discovering what you actually bought: the loyal customer list, or the mold. Auction-floor bluffing and post-sale grudges carry across seasons.

### 47. The Regulars
Pitch: Your shop survives on exactly twelve named regulars, each with needs they never state and a social graph you can't see — when one drifts away, their whole cluster follows in silence weeks later. The loop is noticing micro-signals (shorter visits, changed orders) and tending relationships before the quiet cascade. Regulars can be courted by rival player shops, and a poached regular takes friends.

### 48. Across the Street
Pitch: A 1v1 fog-of-war rivalry: another live player runs the shop directly across from yours, and your only intelligence is what's visible through your front window — their signage, the length of their line, what's in their dumpster. Copying what you see risks copying their mistakes; their thriving look may be a bluff staged for your benefit. Matches run for a real-time week, and the street's foot traffic is a shared pool one of you will starve the other out of.

### 49. Small Fires
Pitch: Every day delivers more problems than hours — burst pipe, staff no-show, surprise pallet delivery, inspector at the door — and you must publicly drop commitments, with the dropped ones compounding into next week's fires. Relief comes only from begging neighboring shop owners (real players) for emergency help, creating favor-debts that they will absolutely call in at the worst time. Nobody tells you which fire was the one that mattered.

### 50. Curb Appeal
Pitch: You control only your shop's exterior — the sign, the window display, the sandwich board, the paint — and your storefront appears as a card in other real players' walk-by feeds as they play their own games. Taps convert to sales, but you never see who scrolled past, how many, or why they didn't stop; your only data is conversions against an invisible denominator. Streets form emergent visual arms races as neighboring players escalate their frontages against each other.

### 51. Dead Letters
Pitch: An MMO played entirely through in-game correspondence: you build a trading house by writing letters to distant agents, suppliers, and strangers, then waiting real hours or days for replies that may never come. Silence is the core mechanic — an unanswered letter could mean shipwreck, insult, a rival's better offer, or simply distance, and you must commit capital before you know which. Every correspondent might be a real player or an NPC, and the game never tells you.

### 52. The Incanto
Pitch: Sealed-bid auctions for state galley routes in Renaissance Venice: each season players bid blind for convoy charters, then must fill holds and hire crews on borrowed ducats before the fleet sails. Route profitability hinges on diplomatic situations the Senate knows and you don't, and losing bidders never learn what the winner paid or why. Players form syndicates to pool bids and plant spies in rivals' warehouses.

### 53. Kontor
Pitch: You are an outsider trying to gain admission to a Hanseatic trading post, doing favors, gifting, and trading on the margins while your membership petition circulates among a committee of veteran players who vote in secret. Blackballing is anonymous and unexplained; you can only guess whom you offended and try a different approach next season. Once inside, you become the gatekeeper, and the guild's internal politics become the endgame.

### 54. The Relay
Pitch: A Silk Road game where no player travels more than one segment: you buy from the westbound trader at your oasis and sell to the next, so your livelihood depends on markets three handoffs away that you will never see. Demand signals arrive only as distorted secondhand rumor and the prices scrawled on crates. Being cut out of a chain is silent — your partner simply stops appearing at the caravanserai, and you must rebuild relationships segment by segment.

### 55. Ledger of Little Falls
Pitch: A cozy-looking frontier general store where the real game is the credit ledger: everyone buys against next harvest, and you decide who gets how much using nothing but gut feel and gossip. Failed crops, sudden illness, and customers skipping town are all invisible until settling day, when your year's judgment is graded at once. Rival players run stores in neighboring towns, and you can share — or quietly falsify — deadbeat blacklists.

### 56. Underwriters' Row
Pitch: You play a marine insurance underwriter initialing slips in a coffeehouse, pricing risk on real voyages run by other players who may be skilled, reckless, or planning to scuttle for the payout. The loop is reading shipping lists, setting premiums under radical ignorance, and sweating for weeks until ships are posted arrived or lost. Syndicates of players share signatures on big risks, so trust among underwriters becomes its own market.

### 57. The Rumor Bourse
Pitch: A market where the only commodity is news: buy sealed reports of distant prices, wars, and sinkings — of unknown age and truthfulness — then trade on them or resell before they go stale. A report's value collapses as it spreads, but you can never see how many copies already exist. Other players are the sources, planting false rumors is perfectly legal, and a burned source's reputation dies without anyone announcing it.

### 58. Dead Reckoning
Pitch: A sailing trade roguelike where the map never shows your position: you navigate by log line, compass, and star fixes, and half of profit is simply arriving where you intended before your water runs out. The uncertainty is spatial — a rich cargo means nothing if you make landfall on the wrong coast. Passing player ships can exchange position estimates that may be honestly mistaken or deliberately poisonous.

### 59. The Revenue Men
Pitch: An asymmetric bluffing game in ten-minute port calls: smuggler players declare cargo manifests while player customs officers, limited to a few inspections per shift, choose which holds to crack open. Tariff schedules change by secret decree that sometimes reaches the officers late too, so both sides are guessing at the rules they're playing under. Long-run pattern reading between regulars becomes a psychological metagame.

### 60. Hawala
Pitch: You move money across a continent without money moving: build a web of player correspondents who honor your paper chits on trust alone, earning spread on every transfer. Defaults surface only months later when a chain unwinds, and you discover who was rotten all along. Established networks refuse to clear a newcomer's paper and never say why, so you start by overpaying on the fringe until someone vouches for you.

### 61. The Sample Case
Pitch: A traveling wholesaler game where you sell from samples: take orders today for goods delivered next season at today's promise, then scramble to source them as prices and fashions drift underneath you. Cancellations arrive as silence, not notice — a retailer who changed their mind simply isn't at the depot. The retailers are other players, and they remember exactly who delivered short last spring.

### 62. Fair Season
Pitch: The world only exists during scheduled real-time fair weeks, Champagne-fair style: four days of frantic simultaneous trading in a massively multiplayer pit, then the market closes and you spend the interval on asynchronous prep — letters, financing, caravan dispatch — with zero price feedback until the gates reopen. Everything between fairs is committed blind against a market you can only imagine. Reputation from last fair is the only signal anyone carries in.

### 63. Bottle Post
Pitch: A stranger-economy game with no chat, no names, and no contracts: you cache goods and handwritten offers at remote drops — beaches, oases, crossroads shrines — for unknown future players to take, honor, rob, or counter. Your entire business is unenforceable one-sided offers to people you will never meet, and the only feedback is what you find (or don't) when you return. Folk conventions of honesty emerge, mutate, and collapse server by server.

### 64. One Hull
Pitch: Your entire enterprise is a single named ship, forever: upgrades, crew, cargo, and reputation all ride on one keel, and if she sinks, your merchant drowns and the account's story ends. The loop is choosing ever-richer voyages against storms and pirates you can only partially foresee through paid intelligence of dubious quality. Famous surviving hulls become server celebrities, courted for escort pacts and worth betraying.

### 65. Terra Incognita, Ltd.
Pitch: You sell maps as a product: fund surveys, compile charts, and sell them to other players who will bet their ships and caravans on your accuracy. The opacity is inverted — you decide how honest your product is, and the market learns the truth slowly, through who fails to come back. Your reputation is a P&L with a months-long feedback lag, and a rival can forge your imprint.

### 66. The Saffron Bubble
Pitch: A commodity whose value is purely social consensus among players: no NPC ever buys it, there is no sink and no floor, only what the next merchant believes. The loop is talking it up, cornering supply, and timing your exit; the crash arrives without announcement because everyone simply got quiet at once. Servers develop their own manias, folklore, and generational scar tissue.

### 67. The Factor
Pitch: You are the overseas agent for other players' capital: principals consign goods and coin to you to trade "at your best judgment," and you remit proceeds with a letter explaining yourself. Clients fire you by simply never consigning again — no exit interview — so your book of business erodes invisibly until dockside gossip tells you why. The game is performance plus correspondence: factors who write good letters keep clients through bad seasons.

### 68. The Patient Market
Pitch: A trading world populated by the absent: standing orders persist for weeks after their owners log off, so you are constantly trading against decisions made by people who are no longer there, under conditions that no longer hold. You can also dispatch cargo timed to arrive into a future market state, effectively doing business with players who haven't logged in yet. Order books show prices but never freshness, and reading staleness becomes the master skill.

### 69. Entrepot
Pitch: You run a free-port warehouse, renting space and handling everyone's transshipments — and every crate through your doors is information you can read, aggregate, and quietly trade on for yourself. Skim too obviously and merchants route around you without a word of complaint; you learn from the empty bays. Rival player warehouses across the strait compete not on price but on discretion.

### 70. Thirty Coins
Pitch: A tactile money-changer game: dozens of local coinages of drifting fineness, sweated edges, and outright counterfeits cross your table, and the loop is fast sensory judgment — weight, ring, shear — under queue pressure. Mints debase their coin by secret decree, and you find out from your own losses weeks later. Bad coin passes player to player through the economy like a disease, and tracing an outbreak back to its source is a community event.

### 71. Change Alley
Pitch: A social-VR coffeehouse where you pitch expeditions to a room of player investors: post a prospectus on the wall, work the tables, and watch most people simply not look up. Rejection is spatial and unspoken — nobody comes to your corner, and you'll never know if it was the numbers, the rumor about your last venture, or your coat. Funded ventures resolve over real weeks while the room gossips about the returns.

### 72. The Tea Race
Pitch: First cargo home takes the premium and the second takes scraps: an annual season where you race rival players' clippers around the world with zero knowledge of their positions after the start gun. Every sail-versus-safety gamble is made against ghosts; intelligence about rivals can be bought in ports they touched, but it is always days old. Between seasons, players finance, crew, and sabotage each other's refits.

### 73. Peddler's Mile
Pitch: A real-world walking mobile game: your pack peddler advances along his territory as you rack up actual steps, knocking on procedurally placed doors — most never open, some slam, and a precious few become weekly route customers with expectations. Houses give no signal before the knock; the only way to learn a street is shoe leather. Other players' peddlers work overlapping territory, and you discover a poached customer only by her absence.

### 74. House of Years

### 489. Wire Deals
Pitch: All deals happen over an anonymous text wire where you can never learn who your counterpart is — a whale, a broke newbie, a syndicate of twelve players, or a bot — and every claim they make about themselves is unverifiable by design. Negotiation becomes a pure reading-the-text skill under identity fog, and the game never resolves the fog even after the deal closes. Long-term players develop styles recognizable enough that "I think I've traded with this one before" becomes its own currency.

### 490. The Lying Manual
Pitch: The game ships with a lavish, beautifully produced 200-page in-fiction tutorial written by a celebrity guru whose advice is confidently, specifically wrong about a third of the time — and finding out which third is the actual game. Following the manual faithfully produces a mediocre doomed business; the fun is the dawning, paranoid re-reading after your first collapse. The community endgame is a shared annotated edition, which itself accumulates errors, because the guru's estate issues revised editions that re-poison settled knowledge.

### 491. Strata
Pitch: You dig through geological layers of a marketplace where the customers are recorded ghosts — actual purchasing behavior of real players from past seasons — and build businesses to serve the patterns you excavate. The trap is regime change: deeper strata are richer and more legible, but the further back you dig, the more likely that demand no longer exists up on the surface. Current players' live behavior becomes the next stratum, so everyone is simultaneously an archaeologist and a fossil.

### 492. Burn to Speak
Pitch: The only way to communicate anything about your product's quality is to publicly burn resources — money, inventory, your own time-in-queue — in the town square; words, ads, and chat about quality are mechanically nonexistent. Customers must decide what your bonfire means, con artists burn big on garbage products, and honest newcomers can't afford to say anything at all. The market becomes a brutal, legible-yet-ambiguous theater of costly signals that players endlessly try to counterfeit.

### 493. Anecdata
Pitch: Your entire analytics stack is one anecdote: each day the game replays you the complete first-person experience of a single randomly chosen customer — a real player — and shows you no aggregates, ever. The discipline is statistical humility: yesterday's furious sample might be your only unhappy customer or the tip of an exodus, and overcorrecting to an N of one is how most ventures die. Players beg, trade, and lie about their daily anecdotes, assembling a folk census nobody can verify.

### 494. Eminent Domain
Pitch: Your storefront is permanent but the city isn't: every season a council of randomly conscripted real players votes on petitions to reroute roads, move the market square, and bridge or dam the river — silently redrawing whose door the city walks past. Half the game is commerce; the other half is lobbying strangers, reading council politics, and hedging against a map that might abandon you. The best players build businesses that survive being left on the wrong side of a bridge that didn't exist last quarter.

### 495. Control Group
Pitch: Every business ships with a shadow twin: a frozen autopilot copy of your company that keeps doing exactly what you did last quarter, forever — and your only dashboard is the live difference between you and it. The design makes causality visceral: when both twins crater, that was the market; when only you do, that was you. Except the counterfactual drifts noisier every month, and deciding how much to keep trusting your own control group becomes the quiet endgame.

### 496. The Seeded Deck
Pitch: Every venture draws weekly events from a deck you can never inspect — and a portion of your deck is secretly seeded by your competitors, who paid to slip disasters (and occasionally poisoned gifts) into your future. You can pay to burn cards from your deck unseen, or study the discard pile of your past, but never peek ahead. The social metagame is deducing who seeded the strike that ruined you, and whether to retaliate or ally with them.

### 497. Honesty Box
Pitch: You run unattended honesty-box stalls across a countryside — stock them, price nothing, and come back to find goods gone and whatever coins strangers (real players) chose to leave. Theft, generosity, spoilage, and weather all arrive as one merged anonymous residue, so attribution is pure inference: was the empty box a robbery or your best day ever? Scaling means placing boxes in territory you'll visit even less, buying more revenue with less knowledge — the game's whole thesis in a tin box.

### 498. Séance Round
Pitch: Your advisory board is a ouija circle of dead founders who answer questions one letter at a time, agonizingly slowly, and whose knowledge froze on the day their business died — the 1998 mail-order queen gives brilliant advice for a world that no longer exists. The tension is triage: séance time is scarce, ghosts ramble, and you must decide which fragments of stale wisdom still transfer. Ghosts are voiced by players whose own ventures died, haunting the living as a post-bankruptcy game mode.

### 499. Same Product, Different Day
Pitch: Every player in the season sells the literally identical product — same specs, same cost, same quality — and the only decisions are when and where to launch, scale, and exit, against hidden regime clocks that make the same move brilliant in March and fatal in May. By stripping out product differences, the game isolates timing, and the season-end reveal shows the full launch-date-versus-outcome scatter plot: a public monument to how much of everyone's fate was calendar luck. Players write manifestos claiming they read the regime; the scatter plot quietly disagrees.

### 500. Shopkeeper in Someone Else's Game
Pitch: You run a business embedded as an "NPC vendor" inside a live fantasy MMO whose adventurer players mostly don't know which shopkeepers are humans — you set prices, stock inventory, spread quest rumors to steer foot traffic, and survive rent in a world whose patch notes, metas, and gold-farming economies you don't control and were never explained. Your market shocks are another game's balance changes, arriving without warning or apology. The deepest uncertainty is social: adventurers who suspect you're human start testing you, and being unmasked changes everything about how they haggle.
