# Step 6 — The Final Five

**Goal recap:** a genuinely effective *and* fun game that teaches true entrepreneurship — radical uncertainty, unknown unknowns, cold outreach met with silence, rejection without explanation, systems nobody explains to you, luck and timing, cash pressure — in an MMO-ish frame where other real players are the counterparties, and with no character-attribute grinding.

**How we got here:** 500 original ideas (step 1) → three full critical passes over every idea — teach (step 2), fun (step 3), MMO-structure/exploits/verdict (step 4) → 100 hybrids recombining the best-surviving mechanics (step 5) → five independent rankers (educator, game director, live-ops lead, producer, founder) voted a shortlist of 14 from the 600-idea pool → every shortlisted design faced a three-lens adversarial panel (teach-skeptic, fun-skeptic, systems-skeptic) → final selection made from the full panel record.

Two shortlisted designs were eliminated on fatal systems verdicts (#503 The Monday Port, #513 The Almanac — both die to the same wound: a dev-authored weekly regime that a 200-player probe cartel solves by Tuesday and publishes out-of-game). Everything else survived as a CONTENDER with named scars. The final five were chosen for strength *and coverage*: each teaches a different region of the entrepreneurship curriculum, each sits on a different structural chassis, and each ships with the panel's fixes adopted as design amendments.

## 1. BOTH SIDES OF THE TABLE  *(#508 — the fundraising game)*

**Lineage:** Ghost Round #3 (ghosting as the emergent optimal play of a real counterparty population) + Zombie Fund #329 (runway-priced meetings; investors who cannot actually wire) + Batch 12 #307 (the 20-founder cohort as sharding-and-cold-start unit).

**One line:** A fundraising MMO where every player holds both a founder deck and an investor checkbook, some checkbooks secretly cannot wire, every meeting burns literal runway — and the ghosting you receive is dealt by humans doing the same brutal triage you did an hour ago.

**The loop.** You join a cohort of twenty founders — your Demo Day class, your alumni network, your home unit. Each week you get a hand of pitch meetings drawn from a server-wide pool of other players' checkbooks (never your own cohort — anonymity is what makes ghosting real). Each meeting costs runway-days; qualifying *who can actually pay* is the core skill, because zombie funds keep taking meetings to look alive to their own backers. Your raised capital buys something real: founder decks are tiny actual services sold into the live server economy, and checkbook capital comes from prior portfolio returns — fundability and zombie status are earned economic states, not dealt roles. Demo Day is the cohort's public reckoning, with a mid-batch pivot-week wildcard for redemption arcs. Then your batch graduates, and its alumni become the investors the next class must learn to read — with your own history following you to the other chair.

**What it teaches.** Qualification discipline (meetings are runway — spending one on a corpse is the death every second-time founder learned to avoid); that silence is triage, not verdict — after you personally ghost twelve of fifteen inbound pitches under real attention scarcity, you are permanently inoculated against reading investor silence as a judgment of your worth (the panel called this "an emotional recalibration no lecture can deliver"); that last cycle's winning pitch pattern is this cycle's automatic pass; and — via the amendment below — that some counterparties are lying to themselves before they lie to you.

**Panel scars and adopted fixes.**
- *No company under the pitch* (teach-skeptic): fixed by re-inheriting #307's organ — decks are real services sold to strangers in the server economy; investor returns have ground truth.
- *Cohort intimacy kills anonymity; gossip solves every zombie in two hands* (fun-skeptic): fixed by decoupling matchmaking from sharding — cohort as home unit, meetings drawn server-wide.
- *Zombie status provable by out-of-band screenshot* (systems-skeptic): fixed by the panel's best single idea in the whole exercise — **solvency opaque to its own holder.** Nobody, including the checkbook's owner, knows whether their fund can wire until a commitment settles; capacity drifts and resolves only at settlement. Screenshots prove nothing, collusion rings carry real counterparty risk, and the zombie chair stops being a villain role because everyone might be lying to themselves first — which is also truer to real zombie VCs.

**Why it won the top slot:** highest vote total by a wide margin (top pick for both the educator and the founder perspectives), the strongest single transferable lesson in the pool, and a chassis the systems-skeptic called "untouchable" — cohorts shard identically at 400 or 10,000, nobody ever pitches an empty room on day one, and the other nineteen humans are the content.

## 2. SETTLEMENT DAY  *(#570 — the trust economy)*

**Lineage:** Handshake Futures #382 (unenforced forward contracts + whispered reputation; the top-scoring original idea at 26/30) + The Patient Market #68 (scriptable standing-order agents) + The Sunrise Clearing #470 (sealed batch settlement) + Surveyor's Gambit #98 (costly identity as the load-bearing prerequisite).

**One line:** A forward-contract economy where the game refuses to enforce any promise, your scripted agent gives your word while you sleep, and all deliveries, defaults, and betrayals resolve in one weekly sealed Settlement Day the whole server gathers to watch.

**The loop.** Everything is a handshake: supply deals, loans, partnerships — either side can walk, and the only enforcement is what players build (escrow houses, vouch brokers, blacklists that are themselves businesses). You script your agent's personality — how your word gets given in your absence — and spend the week on whispers: repricing counterparties, courting vouches, deciding which losing promise you'll honor because your name is worth more than the cargo. Then Settlement Day: one sealed weekly batch per region where the week's promises resolve as public theater. A volatility engine on the underlying goods (harvest failures, storm losses, demand shocks) means even honest players sometimes deliver short — so every default is ambiguous between betrayal and misfortune, and reading which is which is the game.

**What it teaches.** Trust is priced, not given; collateral, vouching webs, and reputation are the actual mechanics of commerce where no contract can save you; institutions (credit history, escrow, courts) are *inventions players will be forced to reinvent* — the founding problem of commerce as a lived experience. The step-4 verdict on its parent still holds: the community "solving" the game means building institutions, so degeneration and the lesson are the same event.

**Panel scars and adopted fixes.**
- *Free churn = one parting-shot default* (teach-skeptic): live collateral scaled to open promise exposure, forfeited pro-rata to injured counterparties when a name goes dark — churn becomes a priceable, collateralized credit risk, which is exactly how real markets solve the unobservable-horizon problem.
- *Honest majority's Settlement Day decays into a bank statement* (fun-skeptic): the volatility engine above — every week swings for everyone.
- *Public resolutions feed a community credit-score tracker that never rots* (systems-skeptic): **publish drama, not data.** The square sees THAT a default happened; only the parties and their vouchers see terms, magnitudes, and chains. Any off-platform credit score must be assembled from self-interested victim testimony — one more rumor the market has to price. Escrow-house custody capped at posted bonded capital, so the institutional rug-pull can never outscale its collateral.

**Why it's here:** the panel's systems-skeptic — the harshest judge on the shortlist — called the weekly sealed settlement "undentable": it converts betrayal from a 3 a.m. ambush into scheduled communal theater, and solves timezone density, lifestyle hostility, and the live-ops incident surface in one stroke. This is the deepest sandbox of the five, and the natural home for half the salvage list (Vouchsafe's apprenticeship funnel, Thursday Street's credit market, The Mutual's insurance pools can all live inside it as player businesses).

## 3. PARTNERS IN ABSENTIA  *(#561 — the partnership game)*

**Lineage:** Ghost Shift #216 (consequences-only management) + Shift Change #425 (handoff-log epistemics: self-serving partner prose reconciled against hard numbers) + Net-90 #258 (payroll clears Friday regardless).

**One line:** Three strangers in three timezones co-own one company that only operates while each owner is away — and your fifteen-minute daily visit is a forensic read of your partners' self-serving handoff prose against numbers that may say otherwise, with payroll clearing from the shared account every Friday whether you trust each other yet or not.

**The loop.** Each real day resolves as three "watches" mapped to the hours each partner is absent, run by that partner's standing rules and incentives. Your daily visit (any hour, fifteen minutes) delivers a forensic bundle: hard numbers, physical artifacts — till discrepancies, grainy floor stills — and your partners' handoff logs. Crucially (panel amendment), **each partner authors their own watch's books**; only the shared bank balance is engine-truth. Everything above the cash line is human testimony — skimming, spin, or honesty — so the forensic substrate is a live adapting mind, never a noise model you can learn. When suspicion hardens, you have a trigger to pull: a costly audit/forced-buyout verb that partially opens the true records, pays correct suspicion in equity and public vindication, and prices false accusation in cash and reputation. An open accusation freezes the shared account until settlement — so Friday's payroll has teeth.

**What it teaches.** The oldest question in small business — *is my partner skimming, lying, or just unlucky?* — plus delegation (you manage what you design, not what you watch), acting before trust can be resolved (payroll waits for no one), and the cost of both suspicion and naivety. The fun-skeptic called it "the best lifestyle fit and best emotional truth on the shortlist."

**Panel scars and adopted fixes.** Partner-authored books (above); the audit/accusation/buyout verb (above); and for the churn problem — sealed three-player cells dying when someone quits — partner departure becomes server-scale content: a salaried **receiver profession** played under a shielded professional identity turns distressed companies around for equity upside, and orphaned partnerships trade as acquirable distressed assets. Churn feeds the game, and the trios connect into an actual economy.

**Why it's here:** the most structurally novel design of the five, the best fit for adults with jobs (the company *only* runs while you're away — absence is the mechanic, not the penalty), and a wiki-proof core: fifteen minutes a day reconciling a human's prose against hard numbers under a Friday deadline. Every panel attack hit the container; none dented the loop.

## 4. PARTY LINE  *(#528 — the attention economy, pocket-sized)*

**Lineage:** Sorry I Missed You #447 (fifteen messages, four callbacks, symmetric ghosting) + Saturday 6AM #230 (player-adjacency demand externalities) + Attorney Review #272 (silent pipeline death).

**One line:** A cozy 20-shop town where every business depends on its neighbors, your answering machine holds fifteen messages every morning, you have time to return four — and every silence you dish out lands on a real player who feels it exactly the way you do.

**The loop.** Ten minutes a day: listen to the machine, pick four calls, live with the eleven silences you just authored. The town's economy is a web of multi-shop deals (your flour from her mill, her ledger on his software) that die silently whenever any link triages you out — the baker never learns her order died because the miller spent his slots courting the new inn. Returned calls visibly bloom (pie on the counter, the friend who drops by); the weekly town meeting publishes anonymized call statistics, turning aggregate silence into arguable communal spectacle. Panel amendments: every neighbor's machine also fills with callers *you can never see* — traveling buyers, rival towns — so their silence might be queue-loss to an actor you can't count (the hypothesis space stays genuinely open); dead deals and closed shops eventually enter a public **tape archive**, so silence stays silent in the moment but always becomes attributable history (autopsy catharsis, and a quitter's global stop reads differently from a triager's selective ghosting); and churned players' shops are silently inherited by indistinguishable NPC keepers (the #524 guard), so the town is always full and every silence always *might* be human.

**What it teaches.** Attention is the scarcest capital; silence is arithmetic, not malice — a lesson *enacted* eleven times every morning rather than narrated; relationship capital compounds; and the pain of being dropped by a supply chain that never explains itself. The systems-skeptic: "the purest delivery of unexplained rejection in the batch, and the only design here that genuinely bootstraps with a single Discord community's worth of players on day one."

**Why it's here:** it is the smallest, most shippable, most human game of the five — an Animal Crossing-shaped daily ritual carrying the project's sharpest lesson. If the ambition is to actually reach people who would never install an MMO, this is the one. It also composes: Party Line is a legitimate MVP or companion app for either #570's or #520's world.

## 5. PERMIT WEEK  *(#520 — the opaque institution)*

**Lineage:** Sevenday #107 (weekly-wipe town, notebook-only persistence) + Guildcharter #106 (losers of the process rotate into administering it) + Inspection Day #38 (gatekeepers with private, unpublishable criteria) + The Licensing Office #340 (institutional knowledge that expires with the regime) + Big in Veldova #166 (paid contradictory interpreters).

**One line:** A weekly-wipe port town where every venture needs permits from an office staffed entirely by last cycle's failed founders — rules that are human, inconsistent, and die with each regime — and when your venture dies, you take the chair and discover from the inside that the process you raged against was never coherent.

**The loop.** Monday: fresh town, drifted undocumented rules, everyone equal; only your notebook survives the wipe. You probe the permit office with cheap filings (early-rotation warning slips are cheap and information-rich), buy contradictory readings from rival player-interpreters who are legally allowed to be wrong, and race to ship. Panel amendment — **the Saturday exists**: a server-wide Market Day where permitted ventures physically open and sell to the town before Sunday's wipe, so a DENIED stamp confiscates a stall you already stocked and dreamed on, and shipping is a witnessed, leaderboarded communal ritual. Filings are randomly assigned to gatekeepers with a multi-stamp quorum required to deny (jury model — kills office capture and grudge-denial while keeping criteria private and human); gatekeepers are scored on whether their approvals ship by Saturday and on consistency-under-appeal, so each regime is noisy but *genuinely* readable, and interpreter track records spanning wipes put a public price on how readable each regime actually was.

**What it teaches.** The purest version of "you don't know how the system works — or anything": opaque institutions learned by probing; institutional knowledge as a depreciating asset; how to distinguish signal from mood-noise (and when you can't); and the genre's hardest lesson, delivered only by seat time — **a rejection carries almost no information about you; don't over-update** — because every player eventually authors arbitrary denials themselves, under real workload, grudges, and score pressure. The teach-skeptic called the rotation "unfakeable experiential pedagogy my attack could not touch."

**Why it's here:** triple-redundant wiki immunity (per-human criteria, weekly expiry, poisonable folklore), the pool's best cold-start chassis (every Monday is day one; a missed week costs nothing; any exploit's blast radius caps at seven days), and a curriculum region — bureaucratic opacity and gatekeeper empathy — that none of the other four covers.

## Honorable mentions (and why they fell)

- **#514 The Mutual** (30 pts) — "the guild-pool IS the startup" teaches mechanism design like nothing else, and *public math, private panic* is the purest un-wikiable uncertainty in the pool. Fell because its fatal scar (a closed loop that insures nothing real, so honest play is negative-EV) is only fixable by anchoring it to a host economy — at which point it is the best *organ* in the pool, and its natural host is Settlement Day's world. Adopt it there.
- **#550 The Weathermakers** (22 pts) — the endogenous credit cycle plus examiner-chair rotation teaches "is it me or the market?", a lesson no finalist touches. Fell because the founder seat as designed has no execution verbs (so it's always the weather), and chair-capture via manufactured shell bankruptcies is undetectable *by construction*. Its macro engine deserves to be the weather system inside Settlement Day or Permit Week.
- **#542 Thursday Street** (20 pts) — the Thursday-night credit scramble is the best single scene in the pool, but six of seven days have no designed verb; it's a credit organ awaiting a host business.
- **#575 Sweet Nothings** (17 pts) — the Mom Test as a two-seat session game; brilliant, but the evader seat needs real latent demand (all three skeptics converged on the same fix), and it's a training-ground companion piece rather than a world.
- **#562 The Slow Post** (16 pts) — the most lifestyle-native loop in the shortlist (self-expiring offers; guaranteed daily mailbag), but its NPC-ambiguity guard is a Turing tightrope with a permanent LLM bill, and its newcomer cold-start needs #566's estate graft.
- **#565 Fairweek** (16 pts) — the opening-bell detonation is a unique communal spike; fell to inherited cartel pre-clearing (fixable by sealed anonymous batch clearing, but the between-fairs void needs a whole second design).
- **#531 The Feed Is Listening** (15 pts) — the strongest anti-wiki stack in the pool and the only platform-dependence candidate; fell because the median merchant faces a four-confound slot machine with every truth channel designed shut, and an insider council leak is a perfect crime.
- **#503 / #513** — eliminated (see scoreboard); both are repaired only by becoming Sevenday again (regenerate the hidden regime from aggregate player behavior), which #520 already does with a better pedagogical payload.

## What the funnel taught (the design laws the winners obey)

1. **Opacity must be made of people.** Every fixed hidden system in 600 ideas dies to a wiki or a probe cartel; every surviving design sources its fog from live human judgment, drifting incentives, or adversarially poisoned knowledge. If even the developers could publish an answer key, someone will.
2. **Silence needs a verb, a texture, and a settlement date.** Waiting with nothing to do is a churn funeral. The winners give silence forensic texture (seen-4x, partial tells), a daily ritual around it, and a scheduled moment where it resolves into spectacle (Settlement Day, Market Day, the weekly Noon, the tape archive).
3. **The rejector must be a player with a scored game — ideally you, tomorrow.** Symmetric ghosting and chair rotation are the two most powerful teaching mechanics discovered: rejection stops being cosmic the day you dish it out under real scarcity.
4. **Identity must be expensive wherever trust matters.** Every trust economy in the pool is one free alt away from collapse; bonds, vouch-stakes, lineage, and exposure-scaled collateral are load-bearing, not polish.
5. **Absence must be safe — or be the mechanic.** Adults have jobs. Batch settlement, wipes, diegetic logout, and consequences-only management beat every design that pages you at dinner.
6. **The exploit should be the lesson.** Cartels, adverse selection, reputation laundering, insider leaks — where the nastiest player behavior *is* the market behavior the game teaches (and is priced, not banned), the design self-renews. Where the exploit merely deletes information, the design dies.
7. **Luck must be visible but not the whole game.** The strongest teaching designs let you eventually distinguish (imperfectly, late, at cost) what was you and what was the weather — and the honest answer "you can't always know" must itself be earnable, not ambient.
